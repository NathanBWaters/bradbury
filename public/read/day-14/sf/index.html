The Filbert Steps, Grace Marchant's Vertical Garden, and the Wild Parrots of Telegraph Hill

San Francisco — How an ungraded cliffside footpath of 1850s wooden cottages, a retired actress carrying soil in buckets, and a flock of cherry-headed conures created an urban Eden above the Embarcadero (1849 Gold Rush Quarry to 1949–Present) • 8 min read

“I never intended to become the birdman of Telegraph Hill; I was an unemployed musician on a bench, and a flock of wild South American parrots landed in the flowering plum trees above my head.” — Mark Bittner (2004)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Plunging down the sheer eastern cliff of Telegraph Hill between Montgomery and Sansome streets, the 377 wooden and concrete stairs of the Filbert Steps preserve a vertical sanctuary where no automobile has ever driven. Saved from nineteenth-century quarry blasting only because shipwrights and longshoremen built Carpenter Gothic cottages along the wooden plank walk of Napier Lane in the 1850s and doused their roofs with fermented Italian red wine during the April 1906 fire, the hillside was transformed between 1949 and 1982 by retired silent-film actress Grace Marchant, who hauled tons of garbage off the mud slope by hand to plant a half-acre terraced garden—now the noisy canopy home of over two hundred wild cherry-headed conures (Psittacara erythrogenys) made famous by writer Mark Bittner.

San Francisco's 1847 street survey by Jasper O'Farrell famously imposed an uncompromising right-angled grid directly over the peninsula's steepest hills, assuming horses and later automobiles would simply climb whatever slope lay ahead; yet on the eastern precipice of Telegraph Hill along the line of Filbert Street between Montgomery and Sansome, the 45-degree cliff was so sheer that the city never paved a road at all. Why is the eastern face of Telegraph Hill a vertical wall of fractured Franciscan greywacke sandstone? Beginning in the 1850s and accelerating through 1914, quarrymen led by contractors like George Fletcher and Gray Brothers blasted millions of tons of blue-gray rock out of the base of Telegraph Hill with black powder and dynamite to build the Embarcadero seawall and sell stone ballast to empty grain ships.

Perched precariously above the quarrymen's dynamite charges along the Filbert Steps and the narrow wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane stood a cluster of one- and two-story redwood cottages built between 1852 and 1875 by ships' carpenters, waterfront stevedores, and Irish and Italian immigrants who carried their groceries and coal up three hundred wooden steps on their backs. During the third day of the Great Earthquake and Fire on Friday, April 20, 1906, as flames roared up the western and southern slopes of Telegraph Hill and the cast-iron water mains lay dry, Italian-American families along the Filbert Steps and Montgomery Street broke open their backyard cellars, formed bucket brigades with hundreds of gallons of homemade red wine, and soaked burlap sacks over their redwood roofs to save the cottages.

In 1936–1937, while Art Moderne apartments including Richard Neutra's Kahn House and the glass-block Malloch Apartment Building (1360 Montgomery Street—where Lauren Bacall sheltered Humphrey Bogart in the 1947 noir film Dark Passage) rose at the top of the cliff, the unbuilt municipal right-of-way alongside the wooden Filbert Steps remained a tangled ravine of rusted bedsprings, broken bottles, and invasive blackberries. In 1949, sixty-three-year-old Grace Marchant—a former silent-screen actress and retail corset buyer—moved into a small shingle cottage at 224 Filbert Steps with her mother and looked out her window at the trash-choked city-owned cliffside falling away toward the Sansome Street warehouses.

Without asking permission from the Department of Public Works or hiring a single laborer, Grace Marchant put on heavy leather gloves in 1949 and spent the next thirty-three years hauling rusted iron, coal cinders, and broken glass down the steps in canvas sacks while carrying topsoil, compost, and dry-laid retaining stones back up the cliff one bucket at a time. Because the eastern face of Telegraph Hill is shielded by the 284-foot summit ridge from the freezing summer westerlies blowing through the Golden Gate and catches the morning sun reflecting off San Francisco Bay, Marchant discovered she was gardening inside a frost-free, banana-belt microclimate (USDA Zone 10b) where roses bloomed in January alongside tree ferns, fuchsias, calla lilies, and loquats.

When Grace Marchant died in 1982 at the age of ninety-six—still tending the vertical terraces past her ninetieth birthday—her neighbor Gary Kray and the Friends of the Filbert Steps stepped in to preserve the half-acre garden under the stewardship of the San Francisco Parks Trust, maintaining wooden boardwalks, koi ponds, and antique streetlamps. Then, around 1988–1990, a second miracle descended into the canopy of Grace Marchant's fruit trees and Monterey cypresses: a raucous, emerald-green and crimson-crowned flock of wild cherry-headed conures (Psittacara erythrogenys), native to the dry tropical forests of Ecuador and Peru, began roosting and nesting in the cliffs of Telegraph Hill.

At the time, Mark Bittner—a homeless, self-taught philosopher and musician from Washington State who had come to San Francisco following the footsteps of the Beat poets—was house-sitting a tiny cottage on the Filbert Steps for artist Aliceirene Estes while living on next to nothing. Sitting on a garden bench beneath the flowering cherry-plum trees in 1994 with a handful of raw sunflower seeds, Bittner began keeping meticulous daily field notebooks on the wild flock, learning to distinguish each individual parrot by the unique crimson feather pattern around its eyes and naming them Connor, Tupelo, Picasso, Mandela, and Dogen.

Rejecting detached laboratory behaviorism while maintaining rigorous observational accuracy, Bittner documented how the former pet-trade escapees had formed complex social hierarchies, lifelong mated bonds, cross-species adoption of a blue-crowned conure, and seasonal foraging circuits stretching from the Cotoneaster berries of the Presidio to the hawthorn trees of Ferry Park. When filmmaker Judy Irving released her documentary The Wild Parrots of Telegraph Hill in 2003 and Bittner published his bestselling 2004 memoir of the same name, millions of readers worldwide discovered the secret symbiosis of the Filbert Steps—prompting the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in 2007 to pass an ordinance banning the feeding of wild parrots in public parks to protect their wild foraging independence while also protecting the cypress trees where they nest.

Today, the wild flock of cherry-headed and mitred conures has grown past two hundred native-born San Francisco birds, their metallic shrieks echoing off the glass towers of the Financial District every morning at dawn as they launch out over Levi's Plaza and Jackson Square. Climbing the 377 steps from Sansome Street up through the wooden porch rails of Napier Lane while hummingbirds dart through Grace Marchant's heirloom roses and wild South American parrots chatter overhead, a walker steps out of the twenty-first-century grid into a vertical ark that survived dynamite, the 1906 inferno, and urban conformity.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The 1906 Red-Wine Bucket Brigade and Quarry-Cliff Survival

Why did the 1850s and 1870s wooden cottages along Napier Lane and the Filbert Steps survive the April 1906 earthquake and fire when stone mansions atop Nob Hill burned to ash? First, the cottages' light redwood balloon frames were pinned directly into solid Franciscan sandstone rather than loose landfill; second, on April 20, 1906, when dynamic fires crept up the hill and city water hydrants ran dry, Italian residents rolled casks of homemade red wine out of their cellars and soaked gunny sacks over their wooden shingles until the wind shifted.

2. Subtropical Lee-Slope Microclimate and Feral Conure Adaptation

How can tropical cherry-headed conures (Psittacara erythrogenys) from Ecuador and Peru thrive outdoors year-round in foggy San Francisco? Telegraph Hill's 284-foot ridge acts as an aerodynamic windshield against the cold summer marine layer funneled through the Golden Gate. Combined with San Francisco's global palette of ornamental street trees—flowering cherry-plums, Victorian box (Pittosporum undulatum), eucalyptus, and Grace Marchant's year-round fruiting loquats—the eastern cliff provides an uninterrupted twelve-month banquet of seeds, blossoms, and berries.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this story with three essential details: first, Napier Lane, the narrow twenty-foot wooden plank walkway intersecting the Filbert Steps where 1860s shipwright cottages still open directly onto a timber boardwalk; second, Grace Marchant (1886–1982), who spent thirty-three years transforming a city garbage ravine into a half-acre vertical botanical garden; and third, the 1937 Art Moderne Malloch Building at 1360 Montgomery Street at the top of the steps, immortalized in the 1947 noir classic Dark Passage.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Begin at the base of the cliff at Filbert and Sansome streets beside Levi's Plaza and climb the lower concrete stairs to the wooden steps entering the Grace Marchant Garden. Pause quietly on the wooden landing at Napier Lane to admire the 1870s cottages and listen for the metallic chatter of the cherry-headed conures in the cypress canopy, then climb to the cul-de-sac of Montgomery Street to see the streamlined 1937 facade of 1360 Montgomery before continuing up the Greenwich Steps to Coit Tower.
