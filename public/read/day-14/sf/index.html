The Filbert Steps, Grace Marchant's Vertical Garden, and the Wild Parrots of Telegraph Hill

San Francisco — How an ungraded cliffside footpath of 1850s wooden cottages, a retired actress carrying soil in buckets, and a flock of cherry-headed conures created an urban Eden above the Embarcadero (1849 Gold Rush Quarry to 1949–Present) • 20 min read

“I never intended to become the birdman of Telegraph Hill; I was an unemployed musician on a bench, and a flock of wild South American parrots landed in the flowering plum trees above my head.” — Mark Bittner (2004)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Plunging down the sheer eastern cliff of Telegraph Hill between Montgomery and Sansome streets (37.8021° N, 122.4036° W), the 377 wooden and concrete stairs of the Filbert Steps preserve a vertical sanctuary where no automobile has ever driven. Saved from nineteenth-century quarry blasting only because shipwrights and longshoremen built Carpenter Gothic cottages along the wooden plank walk of Napier Lane in the 1850s and doused their roofs with fermented Italian red wine during the April 1906 fire, the hillside was transformed between 1949 and 1982 by retired silent-film actress Grace Marchant, who hauled tons of garbage off the mud slope by hand to plant a half-acre terraced garden—now the noisy canopy home of over two hundred wild cherry-headed conures (*Psittacara erythrogenys*) made famous by writer Mark Bittner.

Standing today at the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane in the heart of the eastern cliffside of Telegraph Hill (37.8021° N, 122.4036° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of 1849 Gold Rush Quarry to 1949–Present. San Francisco's 1847 street survey by Jasper O'Farrell famously imposed an uncompromising right-angled grid directly over the peninsula's steepest hills, assuming horses and later automobiles would simply climb whatever slope lay ahead; yet on the eastern precipice of Telegraph Hill along the line of Filbert Street between Montgomery and Sansome, the 45-degree cliff was so sheer that the city never paved a road at all. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how gardener Grace Marchant (1886–1982), naturalist Mark Bittner, and the 1850s shipwrights of Napier Lane and a generation of San Franciscans confronted reclaiming a blasted 45-degree quarry slope and garbage dump into a self-sustaining terraced forest and wild parrot sanctuary, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Filbert Street Steps and the Grace Marchant Garden emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the eastern cliffside of Telegraph Hill. Why is the eastern face of Telegraph Hill a vertical wall of fractured Franciscan greywacke sandstone? Beginning in the 1850s and accelerating through 1914, quarrymen led by contractors like George Fletcher and Gray Brothers blasted millions of tons of blue-gray rock out of the base of Telegraph Hill with black powder and dynamite to build the Embarcadero seawall and sell stone ballast to empty grain ships. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during 1852–1906 (cottage construction & wine firefighting), 1949–1982 (Grace Marchant), and 1990–2007 (wild conure study). Perched precariously above the quarrymen's dynamite charges along the Filbert Steps and the narrow wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane stood a cluster of one- and two-story redwood cottages built between 1852 and 1875 by ships' carpenters, waterfront stevedores, and Irish and Italian immigrants who carried their groceries and coal up three hundred wooden steps on their backs. Contemporary observers who walked past the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood gardener Grace Marchant (1886–1982), naturalist Mark Bittner, and the 1850s shipwrights of Napier Lane, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of ungraded cliffside timber terracing, subtropical microclimate horticulture, and urban avian ethology proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. During the third day of the Great Earthquake and Fire on Friday, April 20, 1906, as flames roared up the western and southern slopes of Telegraph Hill and the cast-iron water mains lay dry, Italian-American families along the Filbert Steps and Montgomery Street broke open their backyard cellars, formed bucket brigades with hundreds of gallons of homemade red wine, and soaked burlap sacks over their redwood roofs to save the cottages. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of ungraded cliffside timber terracing, subtropical microclimate horticulture, and urban avian ethology imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the Filbert Street Steps and the Grace Marchant Garden. In 1936–1937, while Art Moderne apartments including Richard Neutra's Kahn House and the glass-block Malloch Apartment Building (1360 Montgomery Street—where Lauren Bacall sheltered Humphrey Bogart in the 1947 noir film *Dark Passage*) rose at the top of the cliff, the unbuilt municipal right-of-way alongside the wooden Filbert Steps remained a tangled ravine of rusted bedsprings, broken bottles, and invasive blackberries. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through 1852–1906 (cottage construction & wine firefighting), 1949–1982 (Grace Marchant), and 1990–2007 (wild conure study), the surrounding streets of the eastern cliffside of Telegraph Hill became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. In 1949, sixty-three-year-old Grace Marchant—a former silent-screen actress and retail corset buyer—moved into a small shingle cottage at 224 Filbert Steps with her mother and looked out her window at the trash-choked city-owned cliffside falling away toward the Sansome Street warehouses. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Without asking permission from the Department of Public Works or hiring a single laborer, Grace Marchant put on heavy leather gloves in 1949 and spent the next thirty-three years hauling rusted iron, coal cinders, and broken glass down the steps in canvas sacks while carrying topsoil, compost, and dry-laid retaining stones back up the cliff one bucket at a time. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the Filbert Street Steps and the Grace Marchant Garden toward private speculation—forcing gardener Grace Marchant (1886–1982), naturalist Mark Bittner, and the 1850s shipwrights of Napier Lane and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane. Because the eastern face of Telegraph Hill is shielded by the 284-foot summit ridge from the freezing summer westerlies blowing through the Golden Gate and catches the morning sun reflecting off San Francisco Bay, Marchant discovered she was gardening inside a frost-free, banana-belt microclimate (USDA Zone 10b) where roses bloomed in January alongside tree ferns, fuchsias, calla lilies, and loquats. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of ungraded cliffside timber terracing, subtropical microclimate horticulture, and urban avian ethology and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the Filbert Street Steps and the Grace Marchant Garden, the day-to-day craft of ungraded cliffside timber terracing, subtropical microclimate horticulture, and urban avian ethology produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the eastern cliffside of Telegraph Hill. When Grace Marchant died in 1982 at the age of ninety-six—still tending the vertical terraces past her ninetieth birthday—her neighbor Gary Kray and the Friends of the Filbert Steps stepped in to preserve the half-acre garden under the stewardship of the San Francisco Parks Trust, maintaining wooden boardwalks, koi ponds, and antique streetlamps. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the Filbert Street Steps and the Grace Marchant Garden was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of the 200 block of the Filbert Steps and the wooden boardwalk of Napier Lane. Then, around 1988–1990, a second miracle descended into the canopy of Grace Marchant's fruit trees and Monterey cypresses: a raucous, emerald-green and crimson-crowned flock of wild cherry-headed conures (*Psittacara erythrogenys*), native to the dry tropical forests of Ecuador and Peru, began roosting and nesting in the cliffs of Telegraph Hill. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

At the time, Mark Bittner—a homeless, self-taught philosopher and musician from Washington State who had come to San Francisco following the footsteps of the Beat poets—was house-sitting a tiny cottage on the Filbert Steps for artist Aliceirene Estes while living on next to nothing. 1. The 1906 Red-Wine Bucket Brigade and Quarry-Cliff Survival

Why did the 1850s and 1870s wooden cottages along Napier Lane and the Filbert Steps survive the April 1906 earthquake and fire when stone mansions atop Nob Hill burned to ash? How can tropical cherry-headed conures (*Psittacara erythrogenys*) from Ecuador and Peru thrive outdoors year-round in foggy San Francisco?
