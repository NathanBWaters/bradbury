A Meditation Upon a Broomstick

Jonathan Swift (1710) • 7 min read

Surely mortal man is a broomstick! Swift's wickedly brilliant parody of pious meditations turns a worn-out household broom into an upside-down mirror of human hypocrisy.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written around 1703 while serving as chaplain to the Earl of Berkeley and published in 1710, 'A Meditation Upon a Broomstick, According to the Style and Manner of the Honourable Robert Boyle's Meditations' is Jonathan Swift's razor-sharp miniature satire. Each evening the devout Countess of Berkeley asked Swift to read aloud from chemist Robert Boyle's pious Occasional Reflections upon Several Subjects (1665), which drew solemn moralizing lessons from everyday objects. Weary of Boyle's earnest platitudes, the future author of Gulliver's Travels slipped his own handwritten parody inside Boyle's book and read it aloud with a deadpan face, fooling the Countess completely. Comparing hypocritical mortal man to an upside-down broomstick that sweeps others' floors while growing covered in dirt itself, Swift exposes human pretension with devastating wit.

This single stick, which you now behold ingloriously lying in that neglected corner, I once knew in a flourishing state in a forest. It was full of sap, full of leaves, and full of boughs; but now in vain does the busy art of man pretend to vie with nature, by tying that withered bundle of twigs to its sapless trunk; it is now at best but the reverse of what it was, a tree turned upside-down, the branches on the earth, and the root in the air; it is now handled by every dirty wench, condemned to do her drudgery, and, by a capricious kind of fate, destined to make other things clean, and be nasty itself; at length, worn to the stumps in the service of the maids, it is either thrown out of doors or condemned to the last use — of kindling a fire.

When I beheld this, I sighed, and said within myself, “Surely mortal man is a broomstick!” Nature sent him into the world strong and lusty, in a thriving condition, wearing his own hair on his head, the proper branches of this reasoning vegetable, till the axe of intemperance has lopped off his green boughs, and left him a withered trunk; he then flies to art, and puts on a periwig, valuing himself upon an unnatural bundle of hairs, all covered with powder, that never grew on his head; but now should this our broomstick pretend to enter the scene, proud of those birchen spoils it never bore, and all covered with dust, through the sweepings of the finest lady’s chamber, we should be apt to ridicule and despise its vanity. Partial judges that we are of our own excellencies, and other men’s defaults!

But a broomstick, perhaps you will say, is an emblem of a tree standing on its head; and pray what is a man but a topsy-turvy creature, his animal faculties perpetually mounted on his rational, his head where his heels should be, grovelling on the earth? And yet, with all his faults, he sets up to be a universal reformer and corrector of abuses, a remover of grievances, rakes into every slut’s corner of nature, bringing hidden corruptions to the light, and raises a mighty dust where there was none before, sharing deeply all the while in the very same pollutions he pretends to sweep away. His last days are spent in slavery to women, and generally the least deserving; till, worn to the stumps, like his brother besom, he is either kicked out of doors, or made use of to kindle flames for others to warm themselves by.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed around 1703 while Jonathan Swift (1667–1745)—future author of Gulliver's Travels (1726) and A Modest Proposal (1729) and Dean of St. Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin—was serving as chaplain to the Earl of Berkeley, and published in 1710, 'A Meditation Upon a Broomstick' originated as a literary practical joke. Each evening the devout Countess of Berkeley asked Swift to read aloud from the Hon. Robert Boyle's pious Occasional Reflections upon Several Subjects (1665), in which the famous chemist drew solemn moral lessons from everyday objects like a fire or a fishpond. Growing weary of Boyle's earnest platitudes, Swift slipped his own handwritten parody inside the cover of Boyle's book and read 'A Meditation Upon a Broomstick' with a completely straight face—and the Countess praised it as Mr. Boyle's most edifying reflection!

II. Core Thesis & Central Argument

At the center of the essay stands Swift's unforgettable metaphysical conceit: 'Surely mortal man is a broomstick!' Once a living green tree in the forest, the broom is now 'a tree turned upside down' with a dead bundle of somebody else's birch twigs tied onto its sapless trunk—just like an aging eighteenth-century gentleman who has lost his hair to intemperance and struts around in a powdered periwig made of somebody else's hair. Even more devastating is Swift's satire of self-righteous moralists and politicians: like a broom in a dusty room, 'he raises a mighty cloud of dust where there was none before... while he partakes of the filth he was employed to remove,' before being thrown onto the fire when worn down to the stump.

III. Rhetorical Architecture & Prose Style

Swift is the grandmaster of deadpan ironic mimicry. In the opening paragraph, he reproduces the rhythmic cadences, melancholy sighs ('When I beheld this, I sighed, and said within myself...'), and biblical inversions of seventeenth-century devotional prose so flawlessly that the reader is halfway through paragraph two before realizing the trap has sprung. Calling human beings 'this reasoning vegetable' anticipates Gulliver's Houyhnhnms inspecting the Yahoo. IV. Critical Reception & Intellectual Legacy

Samuel Johnson, William Makepeace Thackeray, and George Orwell all admired Swift's 'Broomstick' as a miniature rehearsal for Gulliver's Travels, demonstrating how satire strips away human pomposity (the periwig, the courtroom robe, the pedant's borrowed quotations) by holding up a two-penny kitchen tool as our true mirror.
