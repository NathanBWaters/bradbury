A Meditation Upon a Broomstick

Jonathan Swift (1710) • 7 min read

Surely mortal man is a broomstick! Swift's wickedly brilliant parody of pious meditations turns a worn-out household broom into an upside-down mirror of human hypocrisy.

Key Context & Why It Matters

A Meditation Upon a Broomstick is a tiny piece of satire, barely four hundred words, by Jonathan Swift, the Irish-born writer who later gave the world Gulliver's Travels. Swift wrote it around 1703, while serving as chaplain, a kind of household clergyman, to the family of Lord Berkeley. It was printed in 1710. Its target is a style of writing popular then, the pious meditation, in which a thinker looks at an ordinary object and draws a solemn moral lesson from it. The most famous model was the scientist Robert Boyle, whose Occasional Reflections did exactly that, and Lady Berkeley liked having them read aloud. Swift decided to show how silly the formula could be. He picks a broomstick, an old, battered, upside-down tree, and announces that mankind is just like it. The result is a joke, but a bitter one, and it contains many of Swift's lifelong themes: human vanity, hypocrisy and decay.

This single stick, which you now behold ingloriously lying in that neglected corner, I once knew in a flourishing state in a forest. It was full of sap, full of leaves, and full of boughs; but now in vain does the busy art of man pretend to vie with nature, by tying that withered bundle of twigs to its sapless trunk; it is now at best but the reverse of what it was, a tree turned upside-down, the branches on the earth, and the root in the air; it is now handled by every dirty wench, condemned to do her drudgery, and, by a capricious kind of fate, destined to make other things clean, and be nasty itself; at length, worn to the stumps in the service of the maids, it is either thrown out of doors or condemned to the last use — of kindling a fire.

When I beheld this, I sighed, and said within myself, “Surely mortal man is a broomstick!” Nature sent him into the world strong and lusty, in a thriving condition, wearing his own hair on his head, the proper branches of this reasoning vegetable, till the axe of intemperance has lopped off his green boughs, and left him a withered trunk; he then flies to art, and puts on a periwig, valuing himself upon an unnatural bundle of hairs, all covered with powder, that never grew on his head; but now should this our broomstick pretend to enter the scene, proud of those birchen spoils it never bore, and all covered with dust, through the sweepings of the finest lady’s chamber, we should be apt to ridicule and despise its vanity. Partial judges that we are of our own excellencies, and other men’s defaults!

But a broomstick, perhaps you will say, is an emblem of a tree standing on its head; and pray what is a man but a topsy-turvy creature, his animal faculties perpetually mounted on his rational, his head where his heels should be, grovelling on the earth? And yet, with all his faults, he sets up to be a universal reformer and corrector of abuses, a remover of grievances, rakes into every slut’s corner of nature, bringing hidden corruptions to the light, and raises a mighty dust where there was none before, sharing deeply all the while in the very same pollutions he pretends to sweep away. His last days are spent in slavery to women, and generally the least deserving; till, worn to the stumps, like his brother besom, he is either kicked out of doors, or made use of to kindle flames for others to warm themselves by.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The piece begins as though a preacher were speaking. "This single stick, which you now behold ingloriously lying in that neglected corner, I once knew in a flourishing state in a forest." There is a worn old broom in the corner, and the speaker remembers it as a living tree, full of sap, leaves and boughs. Now it is a clump of withered twigs tied to a dead trunk, a tree turned upside down, with its branches on the ground and its root in the air. It is used by every dirty housemaid to clean other things while being filthy itself, and when it is worn to stumps it is either thrown out of doors or used as kindling.

Then the speaker sighs and delivers the lesson: "Surely mortal man is a broomstick!" What follows is a rapid series of comparisons. Nature sent man into the world strong and healthy, with his own hair, the branches of what Swift calls a "reasoning vegetable." The axe of intemperance, meaning drink and excess, chops off those green boughs, and he is left a withered trunk. Then he turns to art, that is, artifice, and puts on a wig, a powdered mass of hair that never grew on his head. Swift asks you to picture a broomstick proudly displaying birch twigs it never grew, covered with the dust of a fine lady's bedroom, and says we would laugh at its vanity. Yet we are blind to our own.

The third paragraph twists the knife. Someone might say the broom is an emblem of a tree standing on its head, but what is a man except a topsy-turvy creature, his animal appetites riding on top of his reason, his head where his heels should be, grovelling on the earth? And yet this creature sets himself up as a universal reformer, raking into every slut's corner of nature, uncovering hidden corruptions and raising a mighty dust where there was none before, all the while sharing in the same pollutions he claims to sweep away. His last years are spent in slavery to women, generally the least deserving, until, worn to the stumps like his brother besom, he is kicked out of doors or burnt to warm others.

The layers a first reader might miss are two. First, the whole thing is a parody. The solemn form, "I beheld this, and I sighed, and said within myself," imitates Boyle's religious meditations, and the comic effect depends on the gap between a high-minded method and a ridiculous subject. Second, the target keeps shifting. Is Swift mocking vain people, the wig-wearers? Is he mocking reformers, who stir up dust? Or is he mocking the very habit of finding moral lessons in everything? He is mocking all of them at once, and the reader has no stable ground to stand on. The broomstick is humanity, but the speaker's grand moralizing is also a broomstick, raising a mighty dust.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

For something so small, it works brilliantly. The first great virtue is economy. In about four hundred words Swift sets up a parody, delivers a pun-laden central image, and extends it into three distinct satirical blows. There is no waste. Each paragraph escalates: the first describes the broom in pitiable detail, the second applies it to human vanity, and the third makes a sudden, sharper turn against reformers and against the gender politics of the age.

The language is also precise. Phrases like "a tree turned upside-down" and "to make other things clean, and be nasty itself" are both simple and nasty. The idea that the broom is condemned "by a capricious kind of fate" to clean while remaining dirty is a perfect compressed paradox, and it neatly foreshadows the point about reformers, who claim to clean up the world while sharing in its filth. The "reasoning vegetable" is a wonderful phrase, sneaking in a joke about the supposed dignity of reason by calling a human a kind of plant.

The structure is also admirably unstable. The pretense of solemnity cracks gradually. The opening is nearly sincere, almost tender toward the abandoned stick. Only as the comparison expands do we see that the preacher has the tone of a man who cannot resist a joke, and that the whole elaborate moral machinery is a trick. The reader is lured into nodding along before being hit.

But a critic should note limitations. As a parody, it depends heavily on knowing what is being parodied. A modern reader who has never encountered Boyle's Occasional Reflections, or the habit of pious analogy, may find the jokes of manner pass them by, and enjoy only the content. Out of context, the piece can seem a mildly cynical squib about human decay, which is much less interesting than the original satire of a literary fashion.

The final paragraph's reference to men being enslaved to women, "and generally the least deserving," is also a dated and rather cheap gibe. It fits the period's conventional misogyny, and the humor depends on contempt for women as housemaids, wenches and sluts, language that now reads as crude rather than daring. The joke about the housemaids using the broom, "every dirty wench," is likewise class-based snobbery. These are the weakest moments, not because they are offensive alone but because they let Swift's satire drop from a clever attack on human pretension into ordinary prejudice.

There is a further question of tone. Swift's misanthropy, his contempt for humanity as such, is here delivered without much leavening, and some readers find the piece a little glib in its pessimism. It lacks the complexity of his later work, where the narrators are themselves fools and the satire turns back on the reader.

Even so, as a miniature, it delivers its punch cleanly. It shows an early talent at work, and it is funny.

III. The Impact It Had

The Meditation is one of Swift's early pieces, written years before the works that made him immortal, and its direct impact in his own career was modest but real. It gave him an early taste of the power of parody. He learned how adopting the voice and format of an established form, whether a pious meditation, a learned treatise or a charitable proposal, could expose its absurdities from within. That method runs through his greatest books. A Tale of a Tub, published in 1704, is a long satire in the voices of hack writers, and it grew from the same period of Swift's life. Gulliver's Travels in 1726 is narrated by a serious, dull traveler. A Modest Proposal in 1729 is delivered in the tone of a reasonable economist who suggests that the poor of Ireland should sell their babies as food. In each case the straight-faced mask is the weapon.

The piece also gave rise to a famous anecdote. According to the usual story, Lady Berkeley was fond of hearing Boyle's meditations read aloud, and Swift, finding them tiresome, wrote this mock-meditation and contrived for her to hear it read, pretending it was part of the volume. Her reaction, as the story goes, was amused. The truth of the details is hard to confirm, but the story has stuck because it suits Swift's reputation as an impish prankster who could not resist deflating pomposity.

More broadly, the Meditation has become a standard example in the study of parody and satire. It appears in anthologies of eighteenth-century literature and in courses on satire, often as a compact illustration of how a mock genre works. Teachers like it because it is short enough to read aloud and rich enough to discuss for an hour.

Its influence on later writers is mostly a matter of tone rather than direct borrowing. The tradition of the deadpan moral analogy that collapses into absurdity can be traced forward to comic writers such as Mark Twain, and the use of homely objects as emblems of human folly runs through writers as different as Samuel Johnson and, much later, humorists writing deliberately fake sermons and inspirational essays. Modern parodies of motivational writing, in which an author sees a life lesson in a coffee cup or a traffic jam, are the descendants of Swift's broom.

It is worth noting, finally, that the piece did not make Swift famous. It is a footnote to his career. But it shows that the sharpness and economy later admired in his masterpieces were already present in his youth, and that satire could be done on a very small canvas.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who enjoy Swift tend to love the Meditation for its audacity and compression. There is a sheer pleasure in watching a writer take a ridiculous image and push it as far as it will go. The idea of a human being as an upside-down tree, with his head, the seat of reason, where his heels should be, is silly and unforgettable. Readers often say the image lodges in the mind: after you have read it, you cannot see a broom without thinking of a wigged and withered old man.

They also love the surprise of its turn. The piece starts so solemnly that for a moment you may think it is a real sermon, and the shift into comedy is a delight. This is the pleasure of any good parody, in which you recognize the original and also see it undone. For readers who know Boyle, or who know the many other solemn essays of the time, the fun is even greater.

Another quality admired is the mixture of laughter and discomfort. The broomstick is amusing, but the description of the end of a human life, worn to the stumps, kicked out of doors or burned to warm others, is a grim truth underneath the joke. A reader might laugh and then feel slightly chastened, which is the effect Swift generally aims at. His satire works by inviting you to laugh at others before you realize you are included.

People also enjoy the portrait of the human reformer. The final paragraph's description of man as a creature who sets himself up as a universal corrector of abuses, raising a mighty dust and sharing in the pollutions he sweeps away, remains a favorite passage. It has been quoted whenever someone wants to describe a self-righteous crusader who makes a mess while pretending to clean. In a time of loud public moralizing, readers find the line fresh.

Finally, its brevity is a virtue. It can be read in two minutes, read aloud at a dinner party, and discussed afterward. It makes a good introduction to Swift for readers who might find Gulliver's Travels or his political pamphlets daunting. Within a few paragraphs you get his wit, his scorn and his love of a perfectly aimed sentence, and many readers find that taste is enough to send them looking for more.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Meditation has not been the subject of fierce controversy in the way Swift's larger works have, partly because it is so small. But it has met several kinds of resistance, and most of them are really objections to Swift's outlook as a whole.

The first is the charge of misanthropy. Swift himself wrote, in a letter to his friend Alexander Pope in 1725, that he hated and detested that animal called man, while loving individual people. Many readers, from his own day onward, have found his view of humanity too bleak, too ready to see people as filthy and foolish. The broomstick is an early sample. Man is born strong, then ruined by his own excess, then vainly covers the damage with a wig, then becomes a meddling nuisance, then is thrown away. There is little compassion in that story. Critics ask whether a satirist who sees only vanity and decay has understood human beings at all, or has merely settled on a joke that works every time.

The second is the complaint about its attitudes to women and the working classes, which I touched on earlier. The remark about being slaves to women, and the casual contempt for housemaids, are typical of the period but are hard to enjoy today. Feminist and social critics have pointed to such passages as evidence that Swift's satire often punches downward, turning ordinary servants and wives into emblems of dirt and corruption. Others defend him by noting that the target of satire is always the speaker's own pretension, but the passages in question remain uncomfortable.

A third criticism is about religion. Swift was a clergyman and a loyal member of the Church of England, yet a parody of pious meditation could be read as mocking religion itself. In his own time, and especially after the publication of A Tale of a Tub, some readers suspected him of irreverence, and this suspicion damaged his hopes for advancement in the Church. Defenders respond that he was attacking cheap, mechanical piety, not faith, and that his target here was a literary habit, not God. The debate shows how the same text can be seen as devout or mocking depending on the reader.

There is also the plain view that the piece is minor. Some critics regard it as a clever squib, amusing but slight, lacking the imaginative depth of Gulliver or the moral shock of A Modest Proposal. It is, they say, a joke dressed as an essay, and one that depends on knowing its target. That judgment is fair, although most would add that it is a very good joke.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Meditation belongs to the great age of English satire, the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, when writers such as Swift, Alexander Pope, John Dryden and Daniel Defoe used wit as a political and moral weapon. It also belongs to the Augustan age, so called because writers admired the Roman poets of Augustus's time, and valued clarity, balance and the sharp, civilized insult. Swift is the fiercest of these writers, and this little piece is a compact example of his method.

It descends from a long tradition of mock forms. The Greek writer Lucian made fun of philosophers and gods. Erasmus's Praise of Folly, written in 1509, speaks in the voice of Folly herself. Rabelais, in sixteenth-century France, turned scholarship and religion into comic mayhem. Swift inherits their habit of attacking by imitation. In English, the immediate model is the practice of meditation on common things, which Boyle and many religious writers used, and which Swift lampoons.

What descends from it is the modern comic essay and the deadpan parody. Writers who imitate the voice of earnest authority to reveal its emptiness, from Mark Twain to the satirical newspapers of today, are working in a line that runs through Swift. The broomstick's final lesson, that the self-appointed reformer is as dirty as what he cleans, has an obvious afterlife in political satire, where a speaker's moral confidence is exposed as a cover for the same faults.

Comparison with Swift's other works is useful. A Modest Proposal takes the same approach of adopting a reasonable voice, but with devastating moral purpose and a specific political target, the poverty of Ireland and the indifference of the rich. Gulliver's Travels extends the idea of human absurdity across four voyages, with a far more complex narrator. Set beside them, the Meditation is a sketch, but a revealing one, showing the underlying structure of an image, a pious tone and a sudden drop into disgust.

Compare it also with the work of Robert Boyle himself, whose reflections were not meant to be silly; Boyle was a serious scientist and a devout man, and his meditations were sincere attempts to find God in everyday things. Swift's parody is a reminder that such sincerity can be mocked, and that mockery can have its own kind of seriousness.

Why read it now? Because it takes two minutes and leaves a mark. It is a lesson in how satire works, a sample of one of the great English prose styles, and a useful warning against the temptation to turn every small event into a sermon. And it might make you look at the broom in your own closet with new respect, and perhaps a little unease.
