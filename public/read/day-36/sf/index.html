The 1900–1908 San Francisco Bubonic Plague Outbreak, Dr. Joseph Kinyoun, and Dr. Rupert Blue

San Francisco — How the founder of the NIH Hygienic Laboratory identified Yersinia pestis in the Globe Hotel—withstood a governor's cover-up—and how constitutional law and rat ecology saved the city (Turn-of-the-Century Bacteriology & Post-1906 Epidemiology (1900–1908)) • 8 min read

“You cannot repeal the laws of bacteriology by a resolution of the Chamber of Commerce or a proclamation from the Governor’s Mansion.” — Dr. Joseph J. Kinyoun, U.S. Marine Hospital Service (1900)

Key Context & Why It Matters

On March 6, 1900, inside the damp basement of the Globe Hotel at the corner of Jackson and Dupont (now Grant) streets in San Francisco's Chinatown, forty-one-year-old lumber merchant Wong Chut King died of a mysterious swollen-gland fever—marking the first confirmed case of the Third Pandemic of bubonic plague (Yersinia pestis) on the continental United States. When federal bacteriologist Dr. Joseph J. Kinyoun (founder of the Hygienic Laboratory that became the NIH) confirmed the plague bacilli under his microscope at Angel Island, two historic battles erupted: a discriminatory rope quarantine around Chinatown struck down by Federal Judge William W. Morrow in Wong Wai v. Williamson (1900), and a brazen two-year scientific denial campaign led by California Governor Henry Gage—ended only when Dr. Rupert Blue proved that flea-bearing rats, not ethnicity, carried the pathogen.

In 1894, a lethal pandemic of bubonic plague broke out in southern China and Hong Kong, where Swiss-French bacteriologist Alexandre Yersin and Japanese researcher Kitasato Shibasaburo isolated the causative rod-shaped bacterium (Yersinia pestis); transported across the Pacific inside the holds of steamships, the plague struck Honolulu in December 1899 and approached the Golden Gate. Stationed at the federal Marine Hospital Service quarantine facility at Hospital Cove (Ayala Cove) on Angel Island was thirty-nine-year-old Dr. Joseph James Kinyoun—a brilliant American scientist who had studied in Berlin under Robert Koch and in Paris with Louis Pasteur, and who in 1887 had founded the one-room Hygienic Laboratory that evolved directly into the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

On the morning of March 6, 1900, Chinese police surgeon Dr. Frank Wilson examined the body of forty-one-year-old Chinese American lumber dealer Wong Chut King in a basement room beneath the Globe Hotel at Jackson and Dupont (now Grant) streets, noticed enlarged dark inguinal lymph nodes ('buboes'), and sent tissue fluid to City Bacteriologist Dr. Wilfred Kellogg and to Dr. Kinyoun at Angel Island. Applying Koch's strict four postulates in his Angel Island laboratory, Kinyoun stained the bipolar rod-shaped bacteria on glass slides and inoculated test guinea pigs, a rat, and a monkey; within ninety-six hours, on March 11, 1900, the test animals died of classic bubonic plague, proving conclusively that the Black Death had reached the North American mainland.

Panic and anti-Chinese prejudice immediately distorted the city's initial public health response: overnight on March 7, 1900, the San Francisco Board of Health strung hemp ropes and stationed police officers around the twelve square blocks of Chinatown, trapping 25,000 Chinese residents inside while allowing white pedestrians and merchants inside the same boundary to walk freely in and out. Three months later, in May 1900, health officials went even further, ordering that every Chinese resident of San Francisco submit to injection with an experimental, painful prophylactic vaccine developed by Waldemar Haffkine before being allowed to board any train or ferry leaving the city.

Backed by the Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association (the Chinese Six Companies) and represented by constitutional attorneys Maguire & Gallagher, Chinese merchant Wong Wai filed an urgent civil-rights suit in the U.S. Circuit Court for the Northern District of California challenging the compulsory race-based vaccination and travel ban. On May 28, 1900, in the landmark constitutional decision Wong Wai v. Williamson (followed days later by Jew Ho v. Williamson striking down the gerrymandered rope quarantine), Federal Judge William W. Morrow ruled that San Francisco's quarantine violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment—declaring that public health orders written with 'an evil eye and an unequal hand' based on race rather than medical science were unconstitutional.

At the exact same time, however, an opposite threat emerged from Sacramento and downtown San Francisco business elites: fearing that any admission of bubonic plague in San Francisco would trigger nationwide rail embargoes against California fruit and ruin the railroad and hotel trade, Governor Henry T. Gage, the Southern Pacific Railroad, and the San Francisco Chronicle, Call, and Bulletin launched a coordinated campaign claiming that no plague existed in California. For two full years between 1900 and 1902, while Yersinia pestis quietly killed more than a hundred residents inside Chinatown, Governor Gage accused Dr. Joseph Kinyoun of forging evidence and possibly injecting cadavers with laboratory plague bacilli, while newspapers printed caricatures calling him 'Suspicious Kinyoun' and state assemblymen demanded on the legislature floor that Kinyoun be hanged from a lamppost.

Although Kinyoun's findings were completely vindicated in February 1901 by an independent federal blue-ribbon commission of America's three greatest medical scientists—Dr. Simon Flexner of the University of Pennsylvania, Dr. F. G. Novy of the University of Michigan, and Dr. Lewellys Barker of the University of Chicago—political concessions led the Marine Hospital Service to transfer the exhausted Kinyoun out of San Francisco in April 1901. The denialist cover-up finally collapsed in 1903 when Newly elected Governor George Pardee (himself a physician) replaced Gage and Surgeon General Walter Wyman threatened to move the national quarantine conference out of state unless San Francisco cooperated with Dr. Kinyoun's successor: thirty-five-year-old South Carolina epidemiologist Dr. Rupert Blue.

Unlike the heavy-handed officials of 1900, Dr. Rupert Blue set up a modest frame office right inside Chinatown, hired Chinese American interpreter Wong Chung, earned the trust of the Chinese Six Companies, and applied the newly discovered breakthrough of French researcher Paul-Louis Simond: bubonic plague was not spread by 'racial miasma' or dirty laundry, but by the Oriental rat flea (Xenopsylla cheopis) biting infected brown and black rats (Rattus norvegicus and Rattus rattus). By systematically replacing wooden basement floors in Chinatown with poured concrete to cut rats off from soil burrows, elevating garbage cans, and trapping rodents across the waterfront, Dr. Blue halted the first outbreak completely by 1904 after 121 cases and 113 deaths.

Then came the earthquake and fire of April 18, 1906: as rubble piles, broken sewers, and outdoor refugee camp kitchens covered four square miles of ruined San Francisco, the city's rat population exploded and bubonic plague flared a second time in May 1907—this time striking predominantly white neighborhoods across the Marina, North Beach, and the Mission, proving forever that the bacillus knew no racial boundaries. Called back to San Francisco by President Theodore Roosevelt in 1907, Dr. Rupert Blue and the Citizens' Health Committee organized the largest urban vector-control mobilization in American history—paying a ten-cent bounty per rat, trapping and examining over one million rodents, pouring 700,000 square yards of concrete foundations, and eradicating urban plague from San Francisco by November 1908, a triumph that propelled Blue to become Surgeon General of the United States.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Bacteriological Integrity Against Political Denialism (1900–1903)

Why does the story of Dr. Joseph Kinyoun and Governor Henry Gage remain a textbook case study in every school of public health today? When Kinyoun used Robert Koch's laboratory postulates to identify Yersinia pestis in March 1900, California's governor and commercial press spent two years denying the bacterium's existence to protect railroad freight revenues. The Flexner Commission's 1901 report proved that suppressing inconvenient epidemiological truth never protects an economy—it only gives the pathogen time to spread.

2. Equal Protection and Epidemiology: Wong Wai v. Williamson (1900)

How did Chinatown's legal resistance in May 1900 simultaneously advance American constitutional law and scientific epidemiology? In Wong Wai v. Williamson and Jew Ho v. Williamson, Federal Judge William W. Morrow struck down race-based quarantines that zigzagged around white storefronts while penning 25,000 Chinese residents inside crowded blocks—noting that herding healthy people into quarantine zones without addressing the underlying vector actually accelerated disease transmission.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three historical anchors from this two-act epidemic: first, Dr. Joseph Kinyoun's Angel Island quarantine laboratory, where the first mainland U.S. plague diagnosis was confirmed in March 1900; second, the Fourteenth Amendment rulings Wong Wai and Jew Ho v. Williamson (1900); and third, Dr. Rupert Blue's 1903–1908 ecological rat-proofing campaign, which poured concrete basement floors across post-quake San Francisco.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk to the intersection of Grant Avenue (historic Dupont Street) and Jackson Street in the heart of Chinatown, the historic site of the Globe Hotel where the 1900 outbreak was first identified. As you walk down the narrow alleys of Ross Alley and Waverly Place, look at the poured-concrete sidewalk baselines and basement grates first installed during Dr. Rupert Blue's 1904 and 1907 vector-control campaigns.
