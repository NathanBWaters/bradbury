The 1900–1908 San Francisco Bubonic Plague Outbreak, Dr. Joseph Kinyoun, and Dr. Rupert Blue

San Francisco — How the founder of the NIH Hygienic Laboratory identified Yersinia pestis in the Globe Hotel—withstood a governor's cover-up—and how constitutional law and rat ecology saved the city (Turn-of-the-Century Bacteriology & Post-1906 Epidemiology (1900–1908)) • 20 min read

Key Context & Why It Matters

On March 6, 1900, inside the damp basement of the Globe Hotel at the corner of Jackson and Dupont (now Grant) streets in San Francisco's Chinatown (37.7958° N, 122.4065° W), forty-one-year-old lumber merchant Wong Chut King died of a mysterious swollen-gland fever—marking the first confirmed case of the Third Pandemic of bubonic plague (*Yersinia pestis*) on the continental United States. When federal bacteriologist Dr. Joseph J. Kinyoun (founder of the Hygienic Laboratory that became the NIH) confirmed the plague bacilli under his microscope at Angel Island, two historic battles erupted: a discriminatory rope quarantine around Chinatown struck down by Federal Judge William W. Morrow in *Wong Wai v. Williamson* (1900), and a brazen two-year scientific denial campaign led by California Governor Henry Gage—ended only when Dr. Rupert Blue proved that flea-bearing rats, not ethnicity, carried the pathogen.
