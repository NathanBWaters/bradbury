The 1441 Cheugugi Rain Gauge: How Joseon Invented Quantitative Meteorology Two Centuries Before Europe

Korea — Installed across every county magistrate's yard by King Sejong and Crown Prince Munjong, standardized bronze cylinders created the world's longest rainfall record (Joseon Dynasty (August 1441 – 1907)) • 20 min read

“Because soil is parched in one valley and saturated in another, measuring how deep rainwater soaks the dirt is unreliable; therefore cast a bronze vessel and measure the water in chi and pun.” — Sejong Sillok, Year 23, 8th Month, 18th Day (1441)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Ask a European science textbook who invented the first standardized meteorological rain gauge, and it will usually credit Italian monk Benedetto Castelli in 1639 or English architect Christopher Wren in 1662. Yet nearly two hundred years earlier—on August 18, 1441 (lunar calendar)—King Sejong the Great and his scientifically gifted son Crown Prince Yi Hyang (later King Munjong) unveiled the Cheugugi ('Rain-Measuring Instrument'): a standardized cast-bronze cylinder mounted on a granite pedestal (Cheugudae) and deployed to every provincial governor's office and 330 county magistracies across Korea, giving Seoul the longest continuous daily instrumental rainfall record in human history. Anchored at Royal Observatory and Provincial Gamyeong Yards, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

Walk into the lobby of the **Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA)** in Dongjak-gu, Seoul, or the National Science Museum in Daejeon, and stop before a three-section cast-bronze cylinder resting inside a circular socket carved into the top of a rectangular granite block. Measuring **31.9 centimeters high and 14.9 centimeters in internal diameter**, its dark patinated bronze wall is cast in relief with a Classical Chinese inscription recording its casting in **1837** (*Do-gwang Jeong-yu*) for the *Geumyeong* (Provincial Governor’s Office in Gongju, South Chungcheong Province). This is the **Geumyeong Cheugugi** (금영 측우기, **National Treasure No. 329**)—the only surviving original bronze cylinder from a nationwide meteorological network launched four centuries earlier in **1441**. Yet Korea’s climate is governed by the East Asian monsoon (*Jangma*, 장마): more than 60 percent of the entire year’s precipitation dumps violently between late June and August, while April and May are notoriously bone-dry, and granitic mountain rivers drain rapidly into the sea within hours of a storm. How had county magistrates measured rainfall before 1441? Since the Goryeo Dynasty, local officials waited until a rainstorm stopped, walked out into a plowed field with an iron spade, dug a hole in the dirt, and measured with a ruler how many inches deep the rainwater had soaked into the soil (*우택*, *utaek*). Based on that wet-soil depth, the magistrate reported to Seoul whether the county had received enough rain for rice. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Nine months later, on **May 8, 1442** (the eighth day of the fourth lunar month of Sejong’s twenty-fourth year), King Sejong and the Ministry of Taxation (*Hojo*) promulgated a standardized national meteorological law across all eight provinces of Joseon. 1. Fluid & Atmospheric Physics: Why Cylinder Aspect Ratio Prevents Splash and Evaporation

Modern hydrometeorological testing of the 1442 and 1770 Cheugugi dimensions (approximately 31.9 cm tall by 14.9 cm diameter, an aspect ratio of roughly 2.1:1) confirms the empirical brilliance of Joseon engineers.
