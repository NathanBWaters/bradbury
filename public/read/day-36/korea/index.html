The 1441 Cheugugi Rain Gauge: How Joseon Invented Quantitative Meteorology Two Centuries Before Europe

Korea — Installed across every county magistrate's yard by King Sejong and Crown Prince Munjong, standardized bronze cylinders created the world's longest rainfall record (Joseon Dynasty (August 1441 – 1907)) • 10 min read

“Because soil is parched in one valley and saturated in another, measuring how deep rainwater soaks the dirt is unreliable; therefore cast a bronze vessel and measure the water in chi and pun.” — Sejong Sillok, Year 23, 8th Month, 18th Day (1441)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Ask a European science textbook who invented the first standardized meteorological rain gauge, and it will usually credit Italian monk Benedetto Castelli in 1639 or English architect Christopher Wren in 1662. Yet nearly two hundred years earlier—on August 18, 1441 (lunar calendar)—King Sejong the Great and his scientifically gifted son Crown Prince Yi Hyang (later King Munjong) unveiled the Cheugugi ('Rain-Measuring Instrument'): a standardized cast-bronze cylinder mounted on a granite pedestal (Cheugudae) and deployed to every provincial governor's office and 330 county magistracies across Korea, giving Seoul the longest continuous daily instrumental rainfall record in human history.

Walk into the lobby of the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) in Dongjak-gu, Seoul, or the National Science Museum in Daejeon, and stop before a three-section cast-bronze cylinder resting inside a circular socket carved into the top of a rectangular granite block. Measuring 31.9 centimeters high and 14.9 centimeters in internal diameter, its dark patinated bronze wall is cast in relief with a Classical Chinese inscription recording its casting in 1837 (Do-gwang Jeong-yu) for the Geumyeong (Provincial Governor’s Office in Gongju, South Chungcheong Province). This is the Geumyeong Cheugugi (National Treasure No. 329)—the only surviving original bronze cylinder from a nationwide meteorological network launched four centuries earlier in 1441.

To understand why fifteenth-century Korea invented the world’s first standardized, state-operated quantitative rainfall network two hundred years before Galileo’s pupil Benedetto Castelli measured rain in a glass jar in Italy in 1639, look at the hydro-climatology of the Korean peninsula. Korea’s agricultural economy rested on wet-paddy rice (byeo-nongsa), which requires abundant standing water during the May–June transplanting season (monaegi). Yet Korea’s climate is governed by the East Asian monsoon (Jangma): more than 60 percent of the entire year’s precipitation dumps violently between late June and August, while April and May are notoriously bone-dry, and granitic mountain rivers drain rapidly into the sea within hours of a storm.

Furthermore, under King Sejong’s landmark 1444 Gongbeop tax reform, land taxes were no longer levied at a flat rate; instead, every county in the kingdom was graded annually across nine harvest tiers (Yeonbun 9-deungbeop, 9) based on actual weather and crop yield so drought-stricken farmers would automatically receive tax relief! How had county magistrates measured rainfall before 1441? Since the Goryeo Dynasty, local officials waited until a rainstorm stopped, walked out into a plowed field with an iron spade, dug a hole in the dirt, and measured with a ruler how many inches deep the rainwater had soaked into the soil (utaek). Based on that wet-soil depth, the magistrate reported to Seoul whether the county had received enough rain for rice.

Crown Prince Yi Hyang (1414–1452, the future King Munjong), who worked nightly alongside his father King Sejong and engineer Jang Yeong-sil on scientific projects, spotted the fatal physical error in digging dirt holes. Depending on whether the field was dry clay, loose sand, or already damp from last week’s shower—or whether the rain fell as a three-day drizzle versus a twenty-minute cloudburst that ran off the surface—two inches of actual rainfall could soak anywhere from half an inch to seven inches into the soil! As recorded in the Sejong Sillok on August 18, 1441 (the eighteenth day of the eighth lunar month of Sejong’s twenty-third year), Crown Prince Yi Hyang conducted controlled experiments inside theDonggung (the Crown Prince's Palace): whenever rain fell, he set out a cast-bronze cylindrical vessel in the open courtyard away from roof eaves and measured the exact depth of collected rainwater with a graduated ruler.

Nine months later, on May 8, 1442 (the eighth day of the fourth lunar month of Sejong’s twenty-fourth year), King Sejong and the Ministry of Taxation (Hojo) promulgated a standardized national meteorological law across all eight provinces of Joseon. The royal foundry cast official iron and bronze cylinders called Cheugugi (literally 'Rain-Measuring Instrument'), each measuring exactly 1 cheok 5 chon in height (roughly 31.0 to 32.0 centimeters) and 7 chon in diameter (roughly 14.5 to 15.0 centimeters), and dispatched a master dimensional model to every provincial governor (Gwanchalsa) and all 330 county magistrates (Suryeong). Look at the metrological rigor of the May 1442 Royal Protocol: first, every county office had to mount its Cheugugi cylinder atop a heavy, leveled stone pedestal (Cheugudae) in an open courtyard so wind could not tip the cylinder and splashing mud from the ground could not bounce into the mouth of the gauge!

Second, the Hojo mandated that every single station in the kingdom measure the collected rainwater using the exact same decimal standard ruler: the Jucheok (where 1 cheok = 20.7 centimeters, divided into 10 chon/chi [2.07 cm] and 100 pun [2.07 mm]). Thus, five centuries before the modern World Meteorological Organization (WMO) standardized millimeter rain gauges, Joseon magistrates were logging rainfall to 2-millimeter precision! Third, the law specified strict observational procedures: the clerk could not measure during the middle of a storm; he had to record the exact start hour and end hour of the precipitation, measure the water depth down to the pun immediately after the sky cleared so solar evaporation would not shrink the reading, pour out the water, and log the data in the county ledger to be transmitted to the Seoungwan (Royal Bureau of Astronomy) and Hojo in Seoul.

Simultaneously, to track how that surface rainfall translated into river runoff and flood risk along the capital’s streams, King Sejong erected the Supyo —a calibrated hexagonal granite water-depth column marked from 1 to 10 cheok anchored into the riverbed of Cheonggyecheon Stream (at Supyogyo Bridge) and along the Han River at Jeodori! After sixteenth-century wars disrupted the provincial network, the Cheugugi system was revived at full national scale in 1770 (the forty-sixth year of King Yeongjo). Ordered by King Yeongjo on May 1, 1770, to replicate King Sejong’s exact 1442 dimensions, bronze Cheugugi gauges were installed at the royal palaces (Gyeonghuigung, Changdeokgung), the Gwansanggam astronomy bureau, and all eight provincial capitals.

From 1770 until 1907—for 138 unbroken years, every single day without a gap—the Royal Secretariat logged every Seoul Cheugugi rainfall measurement down to the pun in the Seungjeongwon Ilgi (Diaries of the Royal Secretariat, UNESCO Memory of the World) and the Ilseongnok (Records of Daily Reflections). While Rome and Paris have scattered seventeenth-century monthly estimates, Seoul possesses the longest continuous daily quantitative precipitation record on planet Earth! Tragically, during the early twentieth century, nearly all of Joseon’s bronze Cheugugi cylinders were melted down or lost, and the sole surviving 1837 Gongju Geumyeong Cheugugi was carried off to Japan in 1915 by colonial meteorologist Wada Yuji (who, to his scholarly credit, published the first English and French papers introducing King Sejong’s 1441 invention to a stunned Western scientific community). After decades of diplomatic effort, the 1837 bronze Cheugugi was returned from Tokyo to South Korea in 1971 and elevated in 2020 to National Treasure No. 329, alongside the surviving granite Cheugudae pedestals of Changdeokgung (National Treasure No. 330) and Daegu Gamyeong (National Treasure No. 331).

Today, modern climate scientists at the Korea Meteorological Administration and international IPCC modeling teams actively mine the 1770–1907 Seungjeongwon Ilgi Cheugugi database to study 250-year cycles of East Asian monsoon droughts and mega-floods. Every time a weather app on your phone predicts tomorrow’s rain in millimeters, it stands on the granite pedestal that Crown Prince Yi Hyang and King Sejong set in the palace courtyard in August 1441.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Fluid & Atmospheric Physics: Why Cylinder Aspect Ratio Prevents Splash and Evaporation

Modern hydrometeorological testing of the 1442 and 1770 Cheugugi dimensions (approximately 31.9 cm tall by 14.9 cm diameter, an aspect ratio of roughly 2.1:1) confirms the empirical brilliance of Joseon engineers. If a rain cylinder is too shallow, heavy monsoon raindrops hitting the water surface bounce back out (splash loss) and wind eddies sweep droplets away; if the mouth is too narrow, wind turbulence over the rim under-catches rain. The 15-centimeter aperture and 32-centimeter depth paired with a waist-high granite Cheugudae pedestal match twentieth-century WMO standard gauge physics.

2. The 250-Year Seoul Rainfall Series: A Global Treasure for Climate Change Science

Because the Seungjeongwon Ilgi (Diaries of the Royal Secretariat) and the Pungun-gi (Weather Ledgers of the Gwansanggam) recorded every Cheugugi reading in Seoul from 1770 to 1907 in standardized Jucheok units (1 pun = ~2 mm), modern climatologists were able to splice the Joseon royal record directly onto the modern 1908–present KMA instrumental record—creating an unbroken 250+ year daily precipitation dataset used worldwide to study El Niño and monsoon variability.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Remember three meteorological terms: first, Cheugugi (National Treasure No. 329), the standardized three-piece cast-bronze rain gauge invented in 1441; second, Cheugudae (National Treasures No. 330 & 331), the inscribed granite pedestal block with a top socket that anchored the cylinder level against wind; and third, Supyo, the graduated stone river-depth pillar erected in Cheonggyecheon Stream in 1441.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit the National Meteorological Museum inside the Korea Meteorological Administration headquarters in Seoul or the Gongju National Museum to see the original 1837 Geumyeong Cheugugi (National Treasure No. 329). Then walk through Changdeokgung Palace near Seonwonjeon and King Sejong's Memorial Hall in Dongdaemun-gu (where the original 1441 Cheonggyecheon Supyo stone bridge and water-marker pillar stand in the garden).
