Dust of Snow

Robert Frost (1920) • 3 min read

In a single sentence spanning eight tiny lines, a crow shaking snow from a hemlock tree rescues a rueful day from regret.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Dust of Snow is an eight-line poem by Robert Frost, first printed in 1920 and gathered into his 1923 collection New Hampshire, the book that won him a Pulitzer Prize. It is one of the shortest poems he ever wrote, and one of the most loved. Frost wrote it in his forties and fifties, after a hard youth of failed farming, teaching and neglect, when he had finally become a famous poet. The poem is just one sentence long. A man is walking under a hemlock tree when a crow shakes a little snow down on him, and that small accident lifts a bad mood. It is easy to read as a pleasant bit of nature writing, but its quiet claim is large: that rescue from despair does not have to come through great events or deep thinking. It can arrive as a puff of cold powder from a bird most people think of as an omen of bad luck.

The way a crow

Shook down on me

The dust of snow

From a hemlock tree

Has given my heart

A change of mood

And saved some part

Of a day I had rued.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a single sentence stretched over eight lines, and it helps to restate it in plain order. A crow shook some snow down on the speaker from a hemlock tree. That act has changed the speaker's mood and has saved part of a day that he regretted. The grammar is deliberately turned around, so that the thing the crow did comes first and the result comes last. Because of this, the reader gets the pleasure of the small event before learning how large its effect was.

Each image matters. A crow, in many cultures and certainly in English tradition, is a bird of bad luck. It stands for death, carrion, gloom, and warning. A hemlock is an evergreen, but the word also carries a darker echo, because hemlock is a famous poison, the drink that killed the philosopher Socrates. Snow is cold and white and can stand for silence, endings, and winter's deadness. So Frost has assembled nearly every sign of misfortune: a black bird, a poisonous-sounding tree, a cold shower. And what happens? The omen turns into a blessing. The crow's clumsy, unintended act lifts the speaker's heart.

The final line is the key. The speaker says that the moment "saved some part / Of a day I had rued." To rue something is to regret it. It does not say how or why the day was bad. The poem withholds that, perhaps because the cause does not matter. It might have been a quarrel, a failure, or just a stretch of low spirits. What matters is that the day was spoiled, and the speaker was in the grip of it.

Then notice the modesty of the result. The day is not made good. Only "some part" of it is saved. Frost does not claim a conversion or a cure. The change of mood is slight, and the poem is wise enough not to inflate it. This is the way real moods shift. A passing bit of luck, a small beauty, does not fix the underlying trouble, but it opens a little room in which to breathe.

Under the surface, the poem is also about attention. The speaker could easily have been brushed with snow and felt only annoyance. Instead he noticed it, and the noticing was the cure. The poem suggests that moods are not only produced by our circumstances, but can be shifted by what we choose, or are lucky enough, to pay attention to. A crow, a tree, and a little snow were available all along. It took a small accident to make the speaker see them.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Dust of Snow is a model of economy, and it works largely by the care with which Frost handles sound, order, and tone. Take the form. The poem has two stanzas of four lines each, rhymed ABAB, and the lines are very short, only two or three beats each. The first stanza describes the event and the second gives its effect. That symmetry is neat, and it is not accidental. The first stanza is outside the speaker, in the world of crow and tree; the second goes inside, into the heart. The poem's movement is the movement of the snow itself, falling from a branch down into a person.

The rhymes are subtle. Crow and snow rhyme, and so do me and tree, so that the rhyme words of the first stanza pair a bird with a weather, and a speaker with a landscape. It is as though the poem were quietly joining the man and the scene. Then in the second stanza, heart and part, mood and rued, extend the pattern. The effect is smooth and unforced, so that you barely notice how tightly the poem is tied.

The single-sentence structure is another great strength. A sentence that holds back its verb until the fifth line, "Has given my heart a change of mood," creates a gentle suspense. The reader is carried along by the clause, and the payoff arrives right at the end of the poem, in the word "rued." Frost chose a slightly old-fashioned word, which gives the ending a touch of dignity.

The poem's main weakness is its own slightness. It is hard to criticize a poem that makes so few claims, but a skeptical reader may feel that it is little more than a pleasant observation. Where Frost's greater poems carry a sense of danger, this one is almost cozy, and a reader looking for his harder edges will not find them here. There is also something a little too tidy about the way the omens are reversed. A crow that brings luck instead of doom is a charming surprise, but it is a surprise of the kind we have met before, and the poem does not push it further.

The diction is slightly artificial in places. "The way a crow" is a very casual opening, but "a day I had rued" is a more literary phrase, and the two registers do not entirely sit together. Some readers find that this small awkwardness gives the poem its charm. Others hear a faint mismatch.

Still, to ask for more is somewhat to miss the point. The poem is a gesture, like a nod or a wave. It is well made, light on its feet, and it does exactly what it says it does: it lifts the heart a little, and then stops.

III. The Impact It Had

Dust of Snow has had a modest but steady influence, and much of it comes through teaching and memory. It appeared in print in 1920 and was gathered into New Hampshire in 1923, the book that earned Frost the first of his four Pulitzer Prizes for poetry. Within that large and varied book, it is one of the lightest pieces, and its appeal helped round out the picture of Frost as a poet who could be playful and consoling as well as stern.

Over the following century it became one of the most reliably taught of all American poems. Because it is so short and uses such simple language, it is often a student's first encounter with poetry that is both easy and rich. Teachers use it to illustrate the basics: stanza, rhyme, image, and tone. They also use it to show the idea of an omen, since the crow's traditional meaning is reversed. Young readers are often struck by the idea that a poem can be about a mood changing, which is a very ordinary human experience, and it is rare to see it treated so directly.

The poem's closeness to Japanese haiku has been noted by many readers. Haiku are brief poems built on a single natural moment, often with a quiet shift of feeling, and Dust of Snow fits that description very closely, although Frost used English rhyme and meter and did not follow the haiku's syllable pattern. In a period when American poets such as the imagists were looking toward East Asian poetry for models of compression, Frost arrived at something similar from his own direction. The poem is sometimes mentioned alongside William Carlos Williams's The Red Wheelbarrow as an example of how little a poem needs.

Outside the classroom, the poem has traveled in the more private way that short poems do. It turns up in anthologies of verse for hard times, in quotations used in sympathy cards and self-help writing, and in the memory of people who learned it as children. Its image of an unexpected small mercy has made it a favorite for those who think about depression and recovery, since it suggests that relief may come from the smallest source and that one need not wait for a large event to change.

Frost himself never made a great deal of it, and it did not become one of his signature performance pieces in the way that Stopping by Woods did. Its legacy is therefore quieter than that of his famous poems: a small, steady presence, found in many people's minds and in the margins of larger conversations about how to endure a bad day.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love Dust of Snow, above all, because it is true to a feeling almost everyone has had. A mood that seemed fixed lifts, for no good reason, because of something tiny: a bit of sun, a dog on the street, a line of music, a bird. We tend to think our feelings need big causes. The poem delights readers by reminding them that this is not so. A shower of snow, a clumsy crow, and the day looks different.

They love the humor, too. There is something very funny about being hit by a crow's accidental snow shower, and Frost lets the comedy sit quietly under the surface. The bird is not trying to help. It is just a crow being a crow in a tree, and the speaker is just a man who happened to be underneath. The gift is a pure accident, and the poem's gratitude toward an accident is a kind of generosity. Readers appreciate that Frost, who is often thought of as stern, has here allowed himself a little grace.

Readers also respond to the poem's reversal of expectations. Anyone who has absorbed the idea that crows are gloomy will enjoy seeing that idea turned over. The poem rewards a little cultural knowledge, but it does not require it. Even a reader who has never heard of crows as omens will feel the lightness of the moment.

They love its honesty about scale. Frost does not say that the snow solved the speaker's problems. It saved "some part" of a day. This modest claim is moving because it is believable. Readers who have been through hard times often say that they trust the poem because it does not oversell. It offers a small mercy and calls it small.

And then there is the sound. The lines are so short and the rhyme so tidy that the poem seems to fall into the mind by itself, the way the snow falls from the tree. Many people learned it without trying. It is the kind of poem that gets recited on a walk or in a car, a handful of lines that arrive just when needed. That quality of being useful, of being something a person can carry in their pocket and take out on a bad afternoon, is perhaps the deepest reason it is loved.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

There has been little sustained hostility to Dust of Snow, and honesty requires saying that it has not been a major battleground for critics. But the objections that have been raised, or could fairly be raised, are worth setting out.

The most obvious is that the poem is too slight to bear the attention it receives. Some critics prize Frost for his hard, dark, dramatic poems, such as Home Burial or Design, in which nature is menacing or indifferent and human relationships are painfully tangled. Beside those, Dust of Snow can seem like an agreeable trifle. There is a view that its popularity in schools has made it stand for Frost in a way that flatters him, presenting a sunny, consoling poet in place of the more difficult one.

A related complaint is that the poem is a little too neat in its emotional arithmetic. A mood is lifted, a day is saved, the world is briefly kind. Real despair, some readers say, is not usually dispelled by a crow and a handful of snow, and a poem that suggests otherwise risks making light of serious unhappiness. This is a fair worry. In response, defenders point to the poem's limiting phrase, "some part," which carefully avoids claiming a cure. But there is a lingering feeling for some that the poem is a little sweeter than life.

Interpretive critics have sometimes asked whether the poem is as simple as it seems. The word hemlock, for instance, with its echo of poison, has led some readers to see a darker undercurrent, a joke about death that Frost allows to pass lightly. Others think this reading is overingenious, a case of critics finding shadows in a poem that is simply pleasant. The disagreement is not settled, and a reader should feel free to take the poem at face value.

Finally, there is the question of what the poem adds to the haiku tradition it resembles. A purist might say that a haiku works by leaving the reader to supply the feeling, whereas Frost names the feeling outright: "a change of mood." This makes the poem more explanatory and less open than its Japanese cousins. It is a difference of method, not a flaw, but it means that some admirers of compressed imagist verse find Dust of Snow more talkative than they would like.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Dust of Snow sits where several traditions meet, and it is a small but telling example of Frost's place in American poetry. Its closest relative is the nature lyric, the poem in which a moment in the natural world becomes the occasion for a change of feeling. This tradition is old, running from Wordsworth, whose daffodils fill his heart with pleasure when he thinks of them later, to the American Transcendentalists, who believed that nature could teach and heal. Frost keeps that structure but takes out the grandeur. His epiphany is a puff of snow, and he is slightly amused by it.

It is also close to the poetry of the imagists, the group of early twentieth-century poets who wanted clear, concentrated images and no wasted words. Ezra Pound's famous two-line poem about faces in a crowd, and William Carlos Williams's wheelbarrow, belong to the same atmosphere of compression. Frost stood apart from these poets and disliked free verse, but the poem shows how his own preference for tight rhymed form could arrive at the same economy.

Behind both lies Japanese haiku, and behind Frost's more local tradition lie the folk beliefs about crows, which appear in English poetry from Shakespeare to Ted Hughes, whose book Crow is a very different and much darker use of the same bird. Setting Hughes's violent crow beside Frost's benevolent one is a good way to appreciate how flexible a symbol can be.

Looking forward, the poem belongs to the long line of short American poems that find a moment of grace in everyday things. You can hear its descendants in the quiet, observant verse of poets such as Mary Oliver, whose work often turns on a single act of attention in the natural world, and in the many small poems about birds and weather that have filled American magazines ever since.

Why read it today? Partly because it takes thirty seconds and costs nothing. But also because it makes a modest and valuable suggestion about how to live. Moods are fragile and often unreasonable, and one of the things that can break them is not argument or effort but a small, unplanned moment of beauty. The poem asks us to keep an eye out for the crow in the hemlock, and to be grateful when the snow falls on our head. In a loud age, a poem so quiet and so kind may be exactly what we need.
