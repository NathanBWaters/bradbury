November 10, 1952 at the Buena Vista Cafe: How Stanton Delaplane and Jack Koeppler Floated the Cream on Irish Coffee

San Francisco — How a Pulitzer-winning Chronicle columnist and a Hyde Street saloonkeeper recreated Chef Joe Sheridan's Shannon Airport nightcap—with a dairy chemistry secret from Mayor George Christopher (Mid-Century Waterfront Journalism & Culinary Icon (1952–Present)) • 9 min read

“Cream as rich as an Irish brogue, coffee as strong as a friendly hand, sugar as sweet as the tongue of a rogue, and whiskey as smooth as the wit of the land.” — Traditional Shannon Airport & Buena Vista Toast (1952)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stand inside the 1916 wooden saloon of The Buena Vista Cafe at 2765 Hyde Street, directly opposite the Powell-Hyde cable car turntable at Aquatic Park, and watch the white-jacketed bartenders line up twenty-four six-ounce heat-treated glass goblets down the mahogany rail to pour Up to 2,000 Irish Coffees a day. On the night of November 10, 1952, Pulitzer Prize–winning San Francisco Chronicle travel columnist Stanton Delaplane and Buena Vista co-owner Jack Koeppler stayed up until dawn sampling dozens of mugs to recreate the warming whiskey-and-coffee drink invented in 1943 by Chef Joe Sheridan at Foynes/Shannon Airport in Ireland—solving the mystery of why the cream kept sinking only when local dairy owner (and future San Francisco Mayor) George Christopher explained that the cream had to be aged for forty-eight hours and frothed to an exact colloidal density.

Long before tourists lined up at the corner of Hyde and Beach streets for a steaming glass of Irish whiskey and coffee, the ground floor of the three-story 1890s boardinghouse at 2765 Hyde Street had served the working waterfront since 1916, when Sofia Meister converted the first floor into The Buena Vista Saloon ('Good View') to pour whiskey and steam beer for Italian sardine fishermen, longshoremen, and dock workers from the nearby Ghirardelli chocolate factory and Del Monte fruit cannery. To understand how a neighborhood fishermen's bar at the foot of Russian Hill became one of the most famous culinary landmarks in the world on November 10, 1952, we must first fly 5,000 miles east across the Atlantic to a stormy winter night in 1943 at the flying-boat terminal of Foynes on the Shannon Estuary in County Limerick, Ireland.

On that freezing 1943 night, a Pan American flying boat bound for Newfoundland had been forced to turn back through a howling North Atlantic gale after hours of fighting headwinds; when the shivering, exhausted American passengers stumbled into the Foynes terminal restaurant, head chef Joe Sheridan brewed pots of dark hot coffee, stirred in brown sugar and a generous shot of Irish whiskey, and floated a thick collar of rich Irish country cream on top. When a grateful American passenger took a sip through the cold cream and asked, 'Hey Chef, is this Brazilian coffee?' Joe Sheridan smiled with a twinkle in his eye and replied, 'No, that's Irish Coffee!'—and when Foynes transitioned after World War II to modern Shannon International Airport at Rineanna, Sheridan's Irish Coffee became the signature welcome cup for every trans-Atlantic air traveler refueling in Ireland.

In the autumn of 1952, forty-five-year-old San Francisco Chronicle travel writer Stanton Delaplane—who had won the 1942 Pulitzer Prize for Reporting before pioneering the modern newspaper travel column with his beloved 'Postcards from Delaplane'—tasted Joe Sheridan's Irish Coffee during a stopover at Shannon Airport and returned to San Francisco determined to bring the drink to his favorite waterfront saloon. On the rainy evening of Monday, November 10, 1952, Delaplane walked into the Buena Vista Cafe at 2765 Hyde Street and challenged his friend Jack Koeppler (who had inherited the bar from his father) to recreate the Shannon Airport masterpiece right there behind the mahogany bar.

All night long through the early hours of November 11, 1952, Delaplane and Koeppler brewed pot after pot of coffee, poured bottle after bottle of Irish whiskey, and spooned in heavy cream—sampling so many test glasses that Delaplane famously nearly passed out on the cable car tracks outside. Yet two stubborn physical problems defeated them that first night: the flavor wasn't quite balanced, and every time they poured fresh California whipping cream onto the hot coffee, the cream immediately sank to the bottom of the glass or curdled into ugly white streaks instead of floating in a clean, snow-white half-inch collar on top.

Determined to crack the mystery, Jack Koeppler flew personally to Shannon Airport in Ireland to consult Chef Joe Sheridan (and actually offered Sheridan a job in San Francisco, which Sheridan accepted in 1953, working at the Buena Vista for ten years before his death in 1962 and burial in Oakland!), and then consulted a local San Francisco dairy expert: George Christopher, owner of Christopher Dairy Farms and future two-term Mayor of San Francisco (1956–1964). Here is the two-part fluid-mechanics and dairy-chemistry secret that Mayor George Christopher, Joe Sheridan, and Jack Koeppler discovered in 1952—and that the Buena Vista still follows to the millimeter every single day.

First, fluid density (specific gravity): pure black coffee and 80-proof alcohol have a lower specific gravity than cold liquid cream, which is why cream sinks in unsweetened coffee. By dropping two C&H pure cane sugar cubes into the hot coffee and stirring until completely dissolved before adding the whiskey, you increase the specific gravity (density) of the hot coffee-whiskey liquid so that it becomes denser than the aerated cream above it. Second, dairy aging and colloidal aeration: George Christopher demonstrated that brand-new fresh cream will not float properly; instead, heavy whipping cream must be aged for exactly forty-eight hours at cold refrigerator temperature and then lightly frothed (never stiffly whipped into peaks) just before service so microscopic air bubbles are trapped inside the butterfat emulsion—allowing the bartender to pour the cold cream gently over the back of a spoon so it floats effortlessly atop the steaming 185°F coffee.

Watch the theatrical choreography at 2765 Hyde Street today: a single Buena Vista bartender lines up twenty to thirty six-ounce stemmed Libby heat-treated glass goblets in a row, fills each goblet with boiling water to pre-heat the glass, dumps the water out with a rapid two-handed flip, drops two C&H sugar cubes into every glass in rapid-fire rhythm, fills them three-quarters full with hot roasted coffee, stirs to dissolve the sugar, free-pours a one-and-a-half-ounce stream of Tullamore D.E.W. Irish whiskey from waist height without spilling a drop, and crowns the entire row with a floating white collar of forty-eight-hour aged cream.

Never stir a Buena Vista Irish Coffee after it is served: the entire sensory brilliance of Joe Sheridan's and Stanton Delaplane's creation lies in drinking the piping-hot, whiskey-spiked, sugar-dense coffee through the cool, velvety, unsweetened collar of floating dairy cream. Since November 10, 1952, the Buena Vista Cafe has poured more than forty million Irish Coffees—making this single small neighborhood bar at Hyde and Beach streets the largest single consumer of Tullamore D.E.W. Irish whiskey on earth (going through roughly 100,000 bottles and over a million C&H sugar cubes a year). Grab a stool at the mahogany bar or stand by the tall front windows at 2765 Hyde Street as the green-and-cream Powell-Hyde cable car clangs onto the wooden turntable ten feet outside the door, raise a warm glass to Stanton Delaplane, Jack Koeppler, and Joe Sheridan, and taste the most famous nightcap in San Francisco.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The Fluid Physics of Why the Cream Floats: Specific Gravity and Colloidal Foam

Why can you not float cream on top of black coffee or sugar-free Irish Coffee? It is pure Archimedes fluid mechanics: ethanol has a specific gravity of 0.79 and hot brewed coffee is ~1.00, meaning an unsweetened coffee-whiskey mixture is less dense than liquid cream unless two physical interventions occur. First, dissolving two cubes of sucrose raises the specific gravity of the hot base liquid above 1.03; second, aging heavy butterfat cream for forty-eight hours and whipping fine micro-bubbles into it lowers the cream's bulk density below 0.95—creating a stable two-layer thermal and density stratification.

2. Transatlantic Bridge: From Chef Joe Sheridan at Foynes (1943) to Hyde Street (1952)

Unlike many culinary origin stories clouded by myth, the lineage of Irish Coffee is documented in primary journalism and bronze plaques on both sides of the Atlantic. Chef Joe Sheridan invented the drink in winter 1943 at the Foynes flying-boat port in County Limerick; Chronicle columnist Stanton Delaplane brought the concept to Jack Koeppler at 2765 Hyde Street on November 10, 1952; and Sheridan himself immigrated to San Francisco in 1953 to work behind the Buena Vista bar for the final decade of his life.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three historical names and details: first, Stanton Delaplane and Jack Koeppler's all-night experiment on November 10, 1952; second, dairyman and future San Francisco Mayor George Christopher (1956–1964), who solved the forty-eight-hour aged-cream secret; and third, the rule never to stir the floating cream—always sip the hot coffee through the cold collar.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Ride the Powell-Hyde cable car over the roller-coaster crest of Russian Hill all the way to its northern terminus at Hyde and Beach streets above Aquatic Park. Step straight across the street into The Buena Vista Cafe at 2765 Hyde Street, read the bronze commemorative plaques to Stanton Delaplane and Chef Joe Sheridan mounted by the entrance, and stand at the center of the bar to watch the bartender pour twenty-four Irish Coffees in a single row.
