Discourses I.12: Of Contentment

Epictetus (c. 108 AD) • 7 min read

Freedom is secured not by the fulfilling of men's desires, but by the removal of desire.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Published in c. 108 AD as part of Discourses of Epictetus, Book I, Chapter XII (trans. George Long), 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' is a landmark work of Roman Imperial Stoicism by Epictetus. Addressed to students at Nicopolis who complained of homesickness, family interference, or external misfortunes while claiming they wished to live according to nature. True freedom and contentment (euthymia) never come from bending the external universe to match our private wishes, for anyone who demands that events happen only as they desire makes themselves a slave to fortune; freedom comes from aligning our assent with the order of nature while governing our own inner judgments. Opening with the characteristic reflection—"There is a fifth class to whom Ulysses and Socrates belong, who say: “I move not without thy knowledge” (Iliad x 278)."—this essay remains indispensable to modern readers seeking intellectual independence and moral clarity. Readers across generations have returned to its pages to test their deepest assumptions.

With respect to gods, there are some who say that a divine being does not exist: others say that it exists, but is inactive and careless, and takes no forethought about anything; a third class say that such a being exists and exercises forethought, but only about great things and heavenly things, and about nothing on the earth; a fourth class say that a divine being exercises forethought both about things on the earth and heavenly things, but in a general way only, and not about things severally. There is a fifth class to whom Ulysses and Socrates belong, who say: “I move not without thy knowledge” (Iliad x 278).

Before all other things then it is necessary to inquire about each of these opinions, whether it is affirmed truly or not truly. For if there are no gods, how is it our proper end to follow them? And if they exist, but take no care of anything, in this case also how will it be right to follow them? But if indeed they do exist and look after things, still if there is nothing communicated from them to men, nor in fact to myself, how even so is it right (to follow them)? The wise and good man then after considering all these things, submits his own mind to him who administers the whole, as good citizens do to the law of the state. He who is receiving instruction ought to come to be instructed with this intention: How shall I follow the gods in all things, how shall I be contented with the divine administration, and how can I become free? For he is free to whom everything happens according to his will, and whom no man can hinder. What then is freedom madness? Certainly not: for madness and freedom do not consist. But, you say, I would have everything result just as I like, and in whatever way I like. You are mad, you are beside yourself. Do you not know that freedom is a noble and valuable thing? But for me inconsiderately to wish for things to happen as I inconsiderately like, this appears to be not only not noble, but even most base. For how do we proceed in the matter of writing? Do I wish to write the name of Dion as I choose? No, but I am taught to choose to write it as it ought to be written. And how with respect to music? In the same manner. And what universally in every art or science? Just the same. If it were not so, it would be of no value to know anything, if knowledge were adapted to every man’s whim. Is it then in this alone, in this which is the greatest and the chief thing, I mean freedom, that I am permitted to will inconsiderately? By no means; but to be instructed is this, to learn to wish that everything may happen as it does. And how do things happen? As the disposer has disposed them? And he has appointed summer and winter, and abundance and scarcity, and virtue and vice, and all such opposites for the harmony of the whole; and to each of us he has given a body, and parts of the body, and possessions, and companions.

Remembering then this disposition of things, we ought to go to be instructed, not that we may change the constitution of things⁠—for we have not the power to do it, nor is it better that we should have the power⁠—but in order that, as the things around us are what they are and by nature exist, we may maintain our minds in harmony with the things which happen. For can we escape from men? and how is it possible? And if we associate with them, can we change them? Who gives us the power? What then remains, or what method is discovered of holding commerce with them? Is there such a method by which they shall do what seems fit to them, and we not the less shall be in a mood which is conformable to nature? But you are unwilling to endure and are discontented: and if you are alone, you call it solitude; and if you are with men, you call them knaves and robbers; and you find fault with your own parents and children, and brothers and neighbours. But you ought when you are alone to call this condition by the name of tranquillity and freedom, and to think yourself like to the gods; and when you are with many, you ought not to call it crowd, nor trouble, nor uneasiness, but festival and assembly, and so accept all contentedly.

What then is the punishment of those who do not accept? It is to be what they are. Is any person dissatisfied with being alone? let him be alone. Is a man dissatisfied with his parents? let him be a bad son, and lament. Is he dissatisfied with his children? let him be a bad father. Cast him into prison. What prison? Where he is already, for he is there against his will; and where a man is against his will, there he is in prison. So Socrates was not in prison, for he was there willingly. Must my leg then be lamed? Wretch, do you then on account of one poor leg find fault with the world? Will you not willingly surrender it for the whole? Will you not withdraw from it? Will you not gladly part with it to him who gave it? And will you be vexed and discontented with the things established by Zeus, which he with the Moirae (fates) who were present and spinning the thread of your generation, defined and put in order? Know you not how small a part you are compared with the whole. I mean with respect to the body, for as to intelligence you are not inferior to the gods nor less; for the magnitude of intelligence is not measured by length nor yet by height, but by thoughts.

Will you not then choose to place your good in that in which you are equal to the gods? “Wretch that I am to have such a father and mother.” What then, was it permitted to you to come forth and to select and to say: Let such a man at this moment unite with such a woman that I may be produced? It was not permitted, but it was a necessity for your parents to exist first, and then for you to be begotten. Of what kind of parents? Of such as they were. Well then, since they are such as they are, is there no remedy given to you? Now if you did not know for what purpose you possess the faculty of vision, you would be unfortunate and wretched if you closed your eyes when colors were brought before them; but in that you possess greatness of soul and nobility of spirit for every event that may happen, and you know not that you possess them, are you not more unfortunate and wretched? Things are brought close to you which are proportionate to the power which you possess, but you turn away this power most particularly at the very time when you ought to maintain it open and discerning. Do you not rather thank the gods that they have allowed you to be above these things which they have not placed in your power, and have made you accountable only for those which are in your power? As to your parents, the gods have left you free from responsibility; and so with respect to your brothers, and your body, and possessions, and death and life. For what then have they made you responsible? For that which alone is in your power: the proper use of appearances. Why then do you draw on yourself the things for which you are not responsible? It is, indeed, a giving of trouble to yourself.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed and published in c. 108 AD within Discourses of Epictetus, Book I, Chapter XII (trans. George Long), 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' captures Epictetus writing at a pivotal juncture of Roman Imperial Stoicism. Addressed to students at Nicopolis who complained of homesickness, family interference, or external misfortunes while claiming they wished to live according to nature. Far from writing in an ivory tower detached from lived reality, Epictetus addressed an audience grappling with rapid intellectual, social, and moral transformation. By grounding philosophical reflection in concrete historical experience, 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' transformed a pressing contemporary debate into a permanent touchstone of Stoic & Moral Philosophy. When we read its opening movement tonight—"There is a fifth class to whom Ulysses and Socrates belong, who say: “I move not without thy knowledge” (Iliad x 278)."—we hear a thinker speaking across time with immediate personal urgency.

II. Core Thesis & Central Argument

At the intellectual core of 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' stands a bold and liberating thesis: True freedom and contentment (euthymia) never come from bending the external universe to match our private wishes, for anyone who demands that events happen only as they desire makes themselves a slave to fortune; freedom comes from aligning our assent with the order of nature while governing our own inner judgments. Rather than accepting surface appearances or passive social consensus, Epictetus presses beneath conventional assumptions to expose the hidden premises governing human behavior. Midway through the argument, the text crystallizes this insight in memorable terms: "And if we associate with them, can we change them?" Notice how Epictetus refuses to separate abstract philosophical theory from the daily conduct of life; every conceptual distinction carries an immediate ethical consequence for how we perceive the world and govern our own minds.

III. Rhetorical Architecture & Prose Style

From the standpoint of literary craft and rhetorical architecture, 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' exemplifies Epictetus's mastery of the essay form. Notice Epictetus's surgical psychological diagnosis: 'What is it that makes a man free from hindrance and makes him his own master? For wealth does not do it, nor consulship, nor provincial government, nor royal power; driving away false desires does it.' Spanning 5 paragraphs and 1,335 words, the essay alternates deliberately between compressed, quotable aphorisms and expansive, concrete illustrations drawn from history, nature, and everyday observation. Rather than lecturing the reader from above, Epictetus constructs an intimate conversational rhythm that anticipates objections and builds toward its concluding cadence: "Will you not then choose to place your good in that in which you are equal to the gods?" This dynamic interplay between concrete sensory imagery and high philosophical abstraction gives the prose its unmistakable momentum and musical durability.

IV. Critical Reception & Intellectual Legacy

The critical reception and intellectual legacy of 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' reach across multiple generations of writers, philosophers, and citizens. Baruch Spinoza (in Part IV and V of the Ethics), Michel de Montaigne, and Viktor Frankl all echoed Epictetus's insight that inner freedom lies in transforming our attitude toward necessity. Within the broader trajectory of Stoic & Moral Philosophy, this essay helped redefine what non-fiction prose could achieve—proving that rigorous argument and high literary art belong together. Subsequent thinkers repeatedly returned to Epictetus's formulations in 'Discourses I.12: Of Contentment' whenever technological acceleration, political orthodoxy, or cultural fatigue threatened to narrow the scope of human thought. Today, more than a century after its composition, the essay remains a staple of university seminars, anthologies, and private libraries around the world. Its central questions have only grown more urgent in our own age of information overload and fragmented attention.
