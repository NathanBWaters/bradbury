Discourses I.12: Of Contentment

Epictetus (c. 108 AD) • 7 min read

Freedom is secured not by the fulfilling of men's desires, but by the removal of desire.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This short chapter from the Discourses of Epictetus deals with a question people in every age ask: how should we feel about the way the world is run? Epictetus was a Greek Stoic teacher in the Roman Empire around the year 100. He had been a slave, and he taught in a small school in northwestern Greece. A student named Arrian wrote down his talks, which is how we have them in their lively, argumentative spoken style. Here he begins with a quick survey of what people believe about the gods, from outright denial to the belief that the divine cares about everything. Then he argues that a wise person should willingly accept the order of the world, as a good citizen accepts the law. This is not resignation, he insists, but real freedom. The chapter is one of the clearest statements of the Stoic idea that peace comes not from changing the world but from changing what we ask of it.

With respect to gods, there are some who say that a divine being does not exist: others say that it exists, but is inactive and careless, and takes no forethought about anything; a third class say that such a being exists and exercises forethought, but only about great things and heavenly things, and about nothing on the earth; a fourth class say that a divine being exercises forethought both about things on the earth and heavenly things, but in a general way only, and not about things severally. There is a fifth class to whom Ulysses and Socrates belong, who say: “I move not without thy knowledge” (Iliad x 278).

Before all other things then it is necessary to inquire about each of these opinions, whether it is affirmed truly or not truly. For if there are no gods, how is it our proper end to follow them? And if they exist, but take no care of anything, in this case also how will it be right to follow them? But if indeed they do exist and look after things, still if there is nothing communicated from them to men, nor in fact to myself, how even so is it right (to follow them)? The wise and good man then after considering all these things, submits his own mind to him who administers the whole, as good citizens do to the law of the state. He who is receiving instruction ought to come to be instructed with this intention: How shall I follow the gods in all things, how shall I be contented with the divine administration, and how can I become free? For he is free to whom everything happens according to his will, and whom no man can hinder. What then is freedom madness? Certainly not: for madness and freedom do not consist. But, you say, I would have everything result just as I like, and in whatever way I like. You are mad, you are beside yourself. Do you not know that freedom is a noble and valuable thing? But for me inconsiderately to wish for things to happen as I inconsiderately like, this appears to be not only not noble, but even most base. For how do we proceed in the matter of writing? Do I wish to write the name of Dion as I choose? No, but I am taught to choose to write it as it ought to be written. And how with respect to music? In the same manner. And what universally in every art or science? Just the same. If it were not so, it would be of no value to know anything, if knowledge were adapted to every man’s whim. Is it then in this alone, in this which is the greatest and the chief thing, I mean freedom, that I am permitted to will inconsiderately? By no means; but to be instructed is this, to learn to wish that everything may happen as it does. And how do things happen? As the disposer has disposed them? And he has appointed summer and winter, and abundance and scarcity, and virtue and vice, and all such opposites for the harmony of the whole; and to each of us he has given a body, and parts of the body, and possessions, and companions.

Remembering then this disposition of things, we ought to go to be instructed, not that we may change the constitution of things⁠—for we have not the power to do it, nor is it better that we should have the power⁠—but in order that, as the things around us are what they are and by nature exist, we may maintain our minds in harmony with the things which happen. For can we escape from men? and how is it possible? And if we associate with them, can we change them? Who gives us the power? What then remains, or what method is discovered of holding commerce with them? Is there such a method by which they shall do what seems fit to them, and we not the less shall be in a mood which is conformable to nature? But you are unwilling to endure and are discontented: and if you are alone, you call it solitude; and if you are with men, you call them knaves and robbers; and you find fault with your own parents and children, and brothers and neighbours. But you ought when you are alone to call this condition by the name of tranquillity and freedom, and to think yourself like to the gods; and when you are with many, you ought not to call it crowd, nor trouble, nor uneasiness, but festival and assembly, and so accept all contentedly.

What then is the punishment of those who do not accept? It is to be what they are. Is any person dissatisfied with being alone? let him be alone. Is a man dissatisfied with his parents? let him be a bad son, and lament. Is he dissatisfied with his children? let him be a bad father. Cast him into prison. What prison? Where he is already, for he is there against his will; and where a man is against his will, there he is in prison. So Socrates was not in prison, for he was there willingly. Must my leg then be lamed? Wretch, do you then on account of one poor leg find fault with the world? Will you not willingly surrender it for the whole? Will you not withdraw from it? Will you not gladly part with it to him who gave it? And will you be vexed and discontented with the things established by Zeus, which he with the Moirae (fates) who were present and spinning the thread of your generation, defined and put in order? Know you not how small a part you are compared with the whole. I mean with respect to the body, for as to intelligence you are not inferior to the gods nor less; for the magnitude of intelligence is not measured by length nor yet by height, but by thoughts.

Will you not then choose to place your good in that in which you are equal to the gods? “Wretch that I am to have such a father and mother.” What then, was it permitted to you to come forth and to select and to say: Let such a man at this moment unite with such a woman that I may be produced? It was not permitted, but it was a necessity for your parents to exist first, and then for you to be begotten. Of what kind of parents? Of such as they were. Well then, since they are such as they are, is there no remedy given to you? Now if you did not know for what purpose you possess the faculty of vision, you would be unfortunate and wretched if you closed your eyes when colors were brought before them; but in that you possess greatness of soul and nobility of spirit for every event that may happen, and you know not that you possess them, are you not more unfortunate and wretched? Things are brought close to you which are proportionate to the power which you possess, but you turn away this power most particularly at the very time when you ought to maintain it open and discerning. Do you not rather thank the gods that they have allowed you to be above these things which they have not placed in your power, and have made you accountable only for those which are in your power? As to your parents, the gods have left you free from responsibility; and so with respect to your brothers, and your body, and possessions, and death and life. For what then have they made you responsible? For that which alone is in your power: the proper use of appearances. Why then do you draw on yourself the things for which you are not responsible? It is, indeed, a giving of trouble to yourself.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The chapter opens like a lecture on theology. Epictetus sorts people into five camps. Some say there is no divine being. Some say there is but it does nothing. Some say it looks after heaven and great matters but not the earth. Some say it looks after everything only in a general way, not in detail. And a fifth group, which includes Odysseus and Socrates, says, in a line from the Iliad, that "I move not without thy knowledge." Epictetus belongs with the fifth. He thinks the first job of a student is to decide which of these is true, because everything else depends on it. If there are no gods, or they do not care, there is nothing to follow.

Once that is settled, he turns to the question of freedom, and the argument becomes surprising. A free person, he says, is one to whom everything happens according to his will, whom no one can hinder. Does that mean doing whatever you like? No, that is madness. He compares it to writing. When you write the name of Dion, you do not spell it as you please, you learn to spell it as it ought to be written. The same goes for music and every other craft. Knowledge would be worthless if it bent to anyone's whim. So the greatest skill, freedom, must work the same way. To be educated in it is "to learn to wish that everything may happen as it does."

That phrase is the center of the chapter and it is easy to misread. Epictetus does not say that you should stop trying to improve things. He says that the pattern of events, summer and winter, abundance and scarcity, even virtue and vice, was arranged as a whole, and you were handed a body, possessions and companions as part of it. You cannot escape other people or fix them. What you can do is keep your own mind in harmony with what occurs.

The rest of the chapter works out what that means in daily life. The person who is alone calls it loneliness, and the person who is with others calls them thieves and rogues. Epictetus suggests renaming: call being alone tranquillity, and call a crowd a festival. The punishment for not accepting is simply to be what you are, discontented. Someone who is in a place against his will is in prison, whatever the building. Socrates, by contrast, was not in prison, because he was there willingly.

He ends by turning to what we cannot pick: our parents, our bodies, our births. You did not choose your mother and father, so complaining is pointless. What the gods did make you answerable for is the one thing in your power, the right use of appearances, meaning how you handle what comes to you.

A first-time reader might miss that the argument is partly an argument about responsibility. The more we blame, the more we claim control we do not have, and the less we use the control we do. Contentment here is not mood but accuracy.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The chapter's best idea, that freedom means wanting what is real and not what is whimsical, is subtle and surprisingly modern. The comparison to spelling a name is a clever way of making it concrete. We all accept that a craft has rules that do not bend to our feelings. Epictetus asks why the art of living should be the one exception. It is a good, plain argument and it comes at exactly the right moment, after he has raised the suspicion that "wish things to happen as they do" sounds like surrender.

The renaming trick is also well observed. Anyone who has felt lonely in a quiet house and relaxed once they called it peace, or been irritated in a crowd and enjoyed it once they thought of it as a party, knows there is some truth here. It anticipates later psychological ideas about reframing, and it is practical enough to try tonight.

The weak points are fairly clear. The opening survey of beliefs about the gods is brisk to the point of being unfair. The five positions are lined up so that only the last survives, and the arguments against the others take a couple of sentences. A reader who is not already inclined to believe in a caring divine order will find the foundation of the chapter shaky. Everything that follows, the claim that the world has been arranged for harmony, rests on it.

There is a deeper problem. The same argument that tells you to accept your parents and your bad leg also tells you to accept cruelty and injustice, since "virtue and vice" are said to be part of the harmony. Epictetus does not quite say that evil is good, but his line that these opposites exist "for the harmony of the whole" sits uncomfortably next to the suffering of real people. Many readers will feel that a harmony that includes vice has been defined too generously. The chapter does not answer the question of why anyone should rebuild a flawed world if it is already in order.

The passage about the lame leg is another place where the logic is strained. "Must my leg then be lamed? Wretch, do you then on account of one poor leg find fault with the world?" Epictetus was himself lame, which gives the line a certain authority and a certain edge. But it is delivered with a scolding force that may not help someone in pain. The tone moves between comforting and hectoring, and a reader may prefer one or the other.

Still, the writing has energy. The questions come at you quickly, the examples are drawn from daily life, and the best lines, about calling a crowd a festival, are the sort of thing you remember. As teaching it is persuasive. As a proof, it is not, and Epictetus would probably say that this is not the job of a lecture. Its job is to change what you do when the wind blows from the wrong direction.

III. The Impact It Had

This chapter has had its largest effect through the way it distills a central Stoic doctrine, usually described as living in agreement with nature or with providence, into something a person can practice. The doctrine goes back to the founders of Stoicism, who held that the universe is governed by a rational order, and that wisdom lies in aligning one's will with it. Epictetus puts it in an everyday key, and that version became the one most people read.

Marcus Aurelius, the Roman emperor, drew heavily on this idea. His private notebook, the Meditations, returns again and again to accepting what happens as part of the whole, to loving what fate brings. It is likely that his teacher introduced him to Epictetus's lectures, and the habit of addressing yourself sternly, as this chapter does, shaped the emperor's style.

In Christian thought, the idea that a person should submit to a wise divine administration and find freedom in doing so found a ready audience. Early Christian writers admired Stoic ethics even where they disagreed on theology, and a handbook of Epictetus's teaching was adapted for monastic use in the Middle Ages. The phrase "thy will be done" has an obvious cousin in Epictetus's "if so it pleases God, so let it be," which appears elsewhere in the Discourses.

In the modern era the chapter fed into several streams. Some of the language of the Serenity Prayer, associated with the twentieth-century theologian Reinhold Niebuhr and widely used in recovery programs, echoes the Stoic division between what can and cannot be changed, though its immediate origin is Christian. Modern cognitive therapy, whose founders cited Epictetus, shares his idea that distress depends on how we interpret events.

More recently, the so-called Stoic revival has made this sort of teaching popular again. Writers and speakers promote the idea of embracing what happens, sometimes using the Latin phrase "amor fati," a love of fate, which comes from later writers but fits the spirit of this text. Nietzsche, who disliked the Stoics, still used that phrase for his own purposes.

In education, the chapter has served as a classic text for introducing Stoic ethics to students because it is short and covers a lot of ground: theology, freedom, family, solitude and responsibility. It is often paired with the Enchiridion in classes.

Within the Discourses it is also a useful bridge. It links the opening ideas about what is in our power to the later, more practical advice about the relationships and roles we hold, so scholars treat it as a hinge in the argument of the first book.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have most often loved the chapter's consoling reframing. The line about calling a crowd a festival and solitude tranquillity is the kind of thing people copy into notebooks. It gives an immediate practice to try, and many readers report that it works at least some of the time. A person stuck in traffic, a crowded train or a lonely evening can use it at once.

They also love its picture of freedom. Most people think of freedom as having options and getting what you want. Epictetus suggests that real freedom is being unbothered, a mind that cannot be hindered because it asks only for what is possible. That reversal is bracing. For a reader worn down by wanting, the idea that they might simply stop being at war with reality comes as a relief. Writers in the tradition of self-improvement have returned to it for that reason.

The comparison between life and craft is another favorite. People appreciate the plain sense of it: you accept that a spelling or a musical note must be right, so why complain that the world does not follow your preferences? It is argued in a way that does not require anyone to share Stoic physics, though the theology is there for those who want it.

Readers also admire the courage behind the calm. Because Epictetus was a former slave with a lame leg, his talk of contentment cannot be dismissed as the comfort of a rich man. When he tells the listener not to blame the world for one leg, many people feel the weight of what he has himself endured. His teaching carries more credibility because it was lived in conditions most of us would find hard.

The image of the mind as large, though the body is small, is often cited. "The magnitude of intelligence is not measured by length nor yet by height, but by thoughts" is one of the most quoted lines for readers who feel small in the face of larger forces. It says that your size in the universe is not your size in the room.

Finally, there is the sense of dignity. Epictetus calls us to thank the gods for having made us responsible only for what is in our power. It turns a limitation into a gift. The same set of facts that might look like helplessness is shown as a kind of lightness, and readers who take it in often describe feeling that a weight has lifted. That emotional effect is a large part of why the chapter has lasted.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The strongest objection is moral and political. If you are taught that the order of the world is harmonious and that you should wish for things to happen as they do, you may become less willing to resist injustice. Critics from Hegel onward have argued that Stoicism's inner freedom is a way of living with chains rather than breaking them. When Epictetus tells someone dissatisfied with their lot to stop complaining, the line can sound uncomfortably like what a powerful person would like a powerless one to hear.

A second objection concerns the claim that everything happens according to a divine plan. The existence of cruelty, plague, war and the deaths of children has led many readers to find the Stoic confidence unconvincing. The traditional problem of evil is not answered here, only waved at through the remark that opposites exist for the harmony of the whole. Philosophers who share Epictetus's ethics but not his theology have often tried to keep the practice and drop the cosmic claims.

Third, there is the charge that the teaching is cold toward family. The passage on parents, saying that you did not choose them and so are not to blame, is a kind of comfort. But the language of "responsibility" and "not in your power" can read as dismissive. Critics such as the philosopher Martha Nussbaum have asked whether a person who has trained themselves to accept the loss of loved ones as part of the natural order has lost the kind of attachment that makes life valuable.

Fourth, some readers find the tone off-putting. "You are mad, you are beside yourself," he says to the person who wants things to go his way. "Wretch" is a favorite word. This is classroom language, meant to shake students up, but it can come across as bullying. Seneca's gentler letters, or the private doubts of Marcus Aurelius, can feel more humane by contrast.

There is also the historical caution that the words come to us secondhand. Arrian's notes were not an authorized text, and scholars debate how closely they reflect Epictetus's actual words. The chapter may have been polished in the writing.

Finally, a few critics have said that the chapter's claim about freedom plays with words. When Epictetus defines a free person as one to whom everything happens according to his will, and then says that the way to achieve that is to will whatever happens, it can seem like a trick, a definition that makes freedom easy by shrinking what we ask of it. Defenders reply that this is exactly the point, and that the shrinking is the lesson. Still, the dispute is real, and it is part of how the chapter is read and argued over today.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter belongs to Stoicism, the school that began in Athens around 300 BC and flourished in Rome. The Stoics held that the universe is a single living, rational whole, sometimes called by the names of Zeus, God or providence, and that a good human life consists in living in agreement with it. Epictetus, writing in the late first and early second century, represents the later Roman phase, which concentrated on ethics and daily practice more than on the system of physics and logic. This chapter is especially close to the Stoic theme of "following God," found in the famous hymn of Cleanthes, whose line is quoted elsewhere in the Discourses.

It descends from Socrates, whom Epictetus mentions here as one who was never truly in prison, and from the broader Greek tradition that the wise person fits themselves to the order of things. It also relates to the Homeric background, as with the line from the Iliad about Odysseus asking for the goddess to know his every move.

Its relatives in other traditions are many. Buddhist teaching about the cause of suffering as craving, and the cure as releasing it, treats similar ground. The Book of Job in the Hebrew Bible confronts the question of whether one can accept a world one did not make, though it ends with a very different answer, one in which God speaks out of the whirlwind. Taoist writings, with their stress on going with the way of things, have a similar flavor. Spinoza, the seventeenth-century philosopher, argued that freedom is understanding necessity, an idea that is close to Epictetus and was likely shaped by Stoic sources.

Among later works, Boethius wrote his Consolation of Philosophy in prison in the sixth century, drawing on Stoic ideas about fortune and acceptance. Montaigne in the sixteenth century wrote essays on how to accept death and ill fortune, with plenty of Stoic echoes. In our own time, Viktor Frankl's account of his experience in Nazi camps, with its claim that we keep the last of human freedoms, the choice of our attitude, is regularly compared to Epictetus.

Compared with other chapters in the Discourses, this one is more theological and more concerned with attitude to the world as a whole. It is less about specific acts than about the background mood in which we act.

Why read it? Because it names a problem we all share, the sense that the world should be otherwise, and offers a practical response. You do not have to agree with the theology to feel the force of the exercise. Even a skeptic can try, tonight, to call a noisy room a festival and see what changes.
