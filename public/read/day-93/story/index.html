Sredni Vashtar

Saki (H. H. Munro) (1911) • 9 min read

“I thought you liked toast,” she exclaimed, with an injured air, observing that he did not touch it.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Sredni Vashtar is one of the most quietly shocking stories in the English language, and it is only a few pages long. It comes from The Chronicles of Clovis, which appeared in 1911. Its author, Hector Hugh Munro, wrote under the name Saki. He was born in Burma in 1870, lost his mother as a small child, and was raised in a gloomy Devon household by strict aunts, an experience he turned into comedy and revenge again and again. He worked as a journalist and satirist in Edwardian London, a world of polite manners that he loved to puncture. In 1914 he enlisted in the army, though he was well over forty, and he was killed in France in 1916. This story is about a lonely, sickly boy and the adult who runs his life. Notice how calmly, even sweetly, it is told, and ask yourself what is being hidden behind the calm.

Conradin was ten years old, and the doctor had pronounced his professional opinion that the boy would not live another five years. The doctor was silky and effete, and counted for little, but his opinion was endorsed by Mrs. de Ropp, who counted for nearly everything. Mrs. de Ropp was Conradin’s cousin and guardian, and in his eyes she represented those three-fifths of the world that are necessary and disagreeable and real; the other two-fifths, in perpetual antagonism to the foregoing, were summed up in himself and his imagination. One of these days Conradin supposed he would succumb to the mastering pressure of wearisome necessary things⁠—such as illnesses and coddling restrictions and drawn-out dullness. Without his imagination, which was rampant under the spur of loneliness, he would have succumbed long ago.

Mrs. de Ropp would never, in her honestest moments, have confessed to herself that she disliked Conradin, though she might have been dimly aware that thwarting him “for his good” was a duty which she did not find particularly irksome. Conradin hated her with a desperate sincerity which he was perfectly able to mask. Such few pleasures as he could contrive for himself gained an added relish from the likelihood that they would be displeasing to his guardian, and from the realm of his imagination she was locked out⁠—an unclean thing, which should find no entrance.

In the dull, cheerless garden, overlooked by so many windows that were ready to open with a message not to do this or that, or a reminder that medicines were due, he found little attraction. The few fruit-trees that it contained were set jealously apart from his plucking, as though they were rare specimens of their kind blooming in an arid waste; it would probably have been difficult to find a market-gardener who would have offered ten shillings for their entire yearly produce. In a forgotten corner, however, almost hidden behind a dismal shrubbery, was a disused tool-shed of respectable proportions, and within its walls Conradin found a haven, something that took on the varying aspects of a playroom and a cathedral. He had peopled it with a legion of familiar phantoms, evoked partly from fragments of history and partly from his own brain, but it also boasted two inmates of flesh and blood. In one corner lived a ragged-plumaged Houdan hen, on which the boy lavished an affection that had scarcely another outlet. Further back in the gloom stood a large hutch, divided into two compartments, one of which was fronted with close iron bars. This was the abode of a large polecat-ferret, which a friendly butcher-boy had once smuggled, cage and all, into its present quarters, in exchange for a long-secreted hoard of small silver. Conradin was dreadfully afraid of the lithe, sharp-fanged beast, but it was his most treasured possession. Its very presence in the tool-shed was a secret and fearful joy, to be kept scrupulously from the knowledge of the Woman, as he privately dubbed his cousin. And one day, out of Heaven knows what material, he spun the beast a wonderful name, and from that moment it grew into a god and a religion. The Woman indulged in religion once a week at a church near by, and took Conradin with her, but to him the church service was an alien rite in the House of Rimmon. Every Thursday, in the dim and musty silence of the tool-shed, he worshipped with mystic and elaborate ceremonial before the wooden hutch where dwelt Sredni Vashtar, the great ferret. Red flowers in their season and scarlet berries in the wintertime were offered at his shrine, for he was a god who laid some special stress on the fierce impatient side of things, as opposed to the Woman’s religion, which, as far as Conradin could observe, went to great lengths in the contrary direction. And on great festivals powdered nutmeg was strewn in front of his hutch, an important feature of the offering being that the nutmeg had to be stolen. These festivals were of irregular occurrence, and were chiefly appointed to celebrate some passing event. On one occasion, when Mrs. de Ropp suffered from acute toothache for three days, Conradin kept up the festival during the entire three days, and almost succeeded in persuading himself that Sredni Vashtar was personally responsible for the toothache. If the malady had lasted for another day the supply of nutmeg would have given out.

The Houdan hen was never drawn into the cult of Sredni Vashtar. Conradin had long ago settled that she was an Anabaptist. He did not pretend to have the remotest knowledge as to what an Anabaptist was, but he privately hoped that it was dashing and not very respectable. Mrs. de Ropp was the ground plan on which he based and detested all respectability.

After a while Conradin’s absorption in the tool-shed began to attract the notice of his guardian. “It is not good for him to be pottering down there in all weathers,” she promptly decided, and at breakfast one morning she announced that the Houdan hen had been sold and taken away overnight. With her shortsighted eyes she peered at Conradin, waiting for an outbreak of rage and sorrow, which she was ready to rebuke with a flow of excellent precepts and reasoning. But Conradin said nothing: there was nothing to be said. Something perhaps in his white set face gave her a momentary qualm, for at tea that afternoon there was toast on the table, a delicacy which she usually banned on the ground that it was bad for him; also because the making of it “gave trouble,” a deadly offence in the middle-class feminine eye.

“I thought you liked toast,” she exclaimed, with an injured air, observing that he did not touch it.

“Sometimes,” said Conradin.

In the shed that evening there was an innovation in the worship of the hutch-god. Conradin had been wont to chant his praises, tonight he asked a boon.

“Do one thing for me, Sredni Vashtar.”

The thing was not specified. As Sredni Vashtar was a god he must be supposed to know. And choking back a sob as he looked at that other empty corner, Conradin went back to the world he so hated.

And every night, in the welcome darkness of his bedroom, and every evening in the dusk of the tool-shed, Conradin’s bitter litany went up: “Do one thing for me, Sredni Vashtar.”

Mrs. de Ropp noticed that the visits to the shed did not cease, and one day she made a further journey of inspection.

“What are you keeping in that locked hutch?” she asked. “I believe it’s guinea-pigs. I’ll have them all cleared away.”

Conradin shut his lips tight, but the Woman ransacked his bedroom till she found the carefully hidden key, and forthwith marched down to the shed to complete her discovery. It was a cold afternoon, and Conradin had been bidden to keep to the house. From the furthest window of the dining-room the door of the shed could just be seen beyond the corner of the shrubbery, and there Conradin stationed himself. He saw the Woman enter, and then he imagined her opening the door of the sacred hutch and peering down with her shortsighted eyes into the thick straw bed where his god lay hidden. Perhaps she would prod at the straw in her clumsy impatience. And Conradin fervently breathed his prayer for the last time. But he knew as he prayed that he did not believe. He knew that the Woman would come out presently with that pursed smile he loathed so well on her face, and that in an hour or two the gardener would carry away his wonderful god, a god no longer, but a simple brown ferret in a hutch. And he knew that the Woman would triumph always as she triumphed now, and that he would grow ever more sickly under her pestering and domineering and superior wisdom, till one day nothing would matter much more with him, and the doctor would be proved right. And in the sting and misery of his defeat, he began to chant loudly and defiantly the hymn of his threatened idol:

Sredni Vashtar went forth, His thoughts were red thoughts and his teeth were white. His enemies called for peace, but he brought them death. Sredni Vashtar the Beautiful.

And then of a sudden he stopped his chanting and drew closer to the windowpane. The door of the shed still stood ajar as it had been left, and the minutes were slipping by. They were long minutes, but they slipped by nevertheless. He watched the starlings running and flying in little parties across the lawn; he counted them over and over again, with one eye always on that swinging door. A sour-faced maid came in to lay the table for tea, and still Conradin stood and waited and watched. Hope had crept by inches into his heart, and now a look of triumph began to blaze in his eyes that had only known the wistful patience of defeat. Under his breath, with a furtive exultation, he began once again the paean of victory and devastation. And presently his eyes were rewarded: out through that doorway came a long, low, yellow-and-brown beast, with eyes a-blink at the waning daylight, and dark wet stains around the fur of jaws and throat. Conradin dropped on his knees. The great polecat-ferret made its way down to a small brook at the foot of the garden, drank for a moment, then crossed a little plank bridge and was lost to sight in the bushes. Such was the passing of Sredni Vashtar.

“Tea is ready,” said the sour-faced maid; “where is the mistress?”

“She went down to the shed some time ago,” said Conradin.

And while the maid went to summon her mistress to tea Conradin fished a toasting-fork out of the sideboard drawer and proceeded to toast himself a piece of bread. And during the toasting of it and the buttering of it with much butter and the slow enjoyment of eating it, Conradin listened to the noises and silences which fell in quick spasms beyond the dining-room door. The loud foolish screaming of the maid, the answering chorus of wondering ejaculations from the kitchen region, the scuttering footsteps and hurried embassies for outside help, and then, after a lull, the scared sobbings and the shuffling tread of those who bore a heavy burden into the house.

“Whoever will break it to the poor child? I couldn’t for the life of me!” exclaimed a shrill voice. And while they debated the matter among themselves Conradin made himself another piece of toast.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, this is a simple story about a sick, lonely ten-year-old named Conradin. His doctor has said he will not live another five years, and his cousin and guardian, Mrs. De Ropp, treats him accordingly, with a smothering routine of medicines, rules and forbidden pleasures. He thinks of her as one of the three-fifths of the world that is necessary and disagreeable and real. The other two-fifths belong to himself and his imagination, and his imagination is the only thing that keeps him going.

In a forgotten corner of the garden he has found a disused toolshed, and inside it live two creatures: a ragged Houdan hen, on whom he lavishes the affection he has nowhere else to put, and, in a barred hutch, a large polecat-ferret that a friendly butcher-boy once smuggled in for a hoard of small silver. Conradin is dreadfully afraid of the beast, and it is his most treasured possession. One day he spins it a magnificent name, Sredni Vashtar, and it becomes a god. Every Thursday he holds a ceremony in the shed, offering red flowers and scarlet berries, and on festivals, stolen nutmeg. The Woman, as he privately calls his cousin, takes him to church each week, but to him that is an alien rite. His own religion is wild and fierce, the opposite of hers, and she is shut out of it entirely.

Then she finds out something is happening in the shed. She has the hen sold, to cure him of his hobby, and then goes to inspect the hutch herself, certain that he is hiding a pet. Conradin prays to his god in a new way, no longer chanting praise but asking for one thing: Do one thing for me, Sredni Vashtar. The request is never spelled out. A god, he reasons, must know. On the day she goes into the shed, he stands at the window and watches. After a long wait, the great beast emerges with dark stains on its jaws and throat and slips away into the bushes.

The ending is a masterpiece of restraint. Conradin makes himself a piece of toast, the very treat she had forbidden him, and eats it slowly while the household descends into screams and confusion. The last line has him making another piece. We are never told directly that the ferret killed her, and we are never told that Conradin is glad. We do not need to be.

What the story means depends on how you read it. Some see it as a dark fairy tale in which a child's imagination literally wins against grown-up cruelty. Others see a sharp satire of Edwardian respectability and of those who crush a child's spirit "for his good." And there is a third layer: a disturbing glimpse of what loneliness and repression can produce in a child. The story is funny and appalling at the same time, and that tension is its point.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, this is nearly flawless, and it is worth saying why. The first strength is compression. In perhaps two thousand words Saki gives us a child, a villain, a religion, a loss, a prayer and a murder, with scarcely a wasted line. Each detail does a job. The Houdan hen, whom Conradin has decided is an Anabaptist, is a case in point. He has no idea what an Anabaptist is, but he privately hopes it is "dashing and not very respectable." That one joke tells us about his hunger for rebellion, about his guardian's rigid idea of propriety and about the narrator's gleeful tone.

The second strength is control of tone. The narrator is cool, ironic and almost clinical, never begging us to feel sorry for Conradin and never apologizing for the violence. Because the story refuses to editorialize, the reader's feelings have room to complicate themselves. We pity the boy. We cheer for him. We are also a little frightened by how easily we cheer. That moral discomfort is exactly what Saki wants, and the toast scene delivers it with extraordinary economy.

Third, the structure is perfect. The details planted early, the doctor's verdict, the forbidden toast, the sold hen, the unspecified prayer, all pay off in the last paragraph. The hymn that Conradin chants, with its red thoughts and white teeth, gives the climax a fierce, ritual beauty. It is a small ceremony with the force of a battle cry.

Yet the story is not beyond criticism. Mrs. De Ropp is a villain drawn in a few strokes, and she is not quite human. Her cruelty is more of a caricature than a character, and a reader looking for a more nuanced portrait of a difficult guardian will not find one. The story also plays its cards close to the chest, and some readers find the final turn too tidy: the monster is slain, the boy is free, and the narrator seems to hold us in a sort of gleeful complicity. There is also a faint cruelty in the way the story uses the sick child's fantasy as a weapon. Its cleverness is sometimes colder than it needs to be. Nonetheless, these are quibbles. The story is built so tightly that even its limitations feel like choices, and few short stories of this length leave such a mark.

One last point of craft deserves mention: the use of absence. We never see Mrs. De Ropp die. We hear it, through a door, as noises and silences that fall in quick spasms, the maid's foolish screaming, the scuttering footsteps, the shuffling tread of those who carry a heavy burden. By keeping the horror offstage and letting a child make toast in the foreground, Saki makes the scene more chilling than any description could. It is a lesson that horror writers still study.

III. The Impact It Had

Saki's story has had a long afterlife, far larger than its modest size would suggest. It has been anthologized for more than a century and is routinely included in collections of the best short stories in English. It is a staple of school and university reading lists, particularly in Britain and the Commonwealth, where teachers use it to introduce irony, point of view and the art of the twist.

It also helped shape a particular style of dark comic short fiction. The cool, polished surface hiding something savage was a technique Saki perfected, and later writers who combined wit with cruelty, such as Roald Dahl, are often said to be his descendants. Dahl's wicked adults, his gleefully vengeful children and his stories that end with a sudden, shocking justice owe a great deal to the Saki tradition, though it would be unwise to claim a precise line of influence. The pattern of a child outwitting or outlasting an oppressive grown-up has since become one of the most popular in children's and young adult fiction.

The story has been read on radio and adapted for the screen in small ways, though it has never needed an adaptation to survive. The name Sredni Vashtar itself has taken on a life of its own. Musicians and writers have borrowed it, and it has become a kind of shorthand for a private god or a secret weapon. Few invented names from a short story have lasted so well, and fewer still have been made up, as this one apparently was, out of nothing at all.

The story also matters for Saki's own legacy. By the time he died, he was known as a clever humorist, but this story and a few others showed a darker and deeper side. His death on the Western Front in 1916 turned his work into a kind of monument to a vanished world, and critics have often read the cold humor of stories like this one as a premonition of the violence that engulfed Europe. That reading is a later one, since the story was written before the war, but it gives the tale an extra shadow that it carries to this day.

It is also worth remembering that Saki's real childhood is part of the story's legend. He and his siblings were brought up by aunts in a strict household in Devon, and readers have long guessed that Mrs. De Ropp owes something to them. That biographical connection, though no one can say how much of the story is memory, has become part of how the story is taught, and it is one of the reasons students often feel that it was written by someone who knew exactly what he was describing.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love this story first of all for its audacity. It dares to make a murder the happy ending, and it does so in a voice so smooth and so cheerful that we find ourselves going along with it before we realize what we are cheering for. People who read it as children often remember it for life, partly because the toast and the hutch are so vivid, partly because it did not behave like the improving stories they were used to.

Many readers love the way it takes the side of a child. Conradin is not saintly. He is sly, resentful and capable of deep hatred. But the story respects his inner life. It treats his religion as real and his imagination as a living force. Readers who felt misunderstood or controlled in childhood often find in the story an enormous relief, a validation of the idea that a secret inner world can be a source of power.

There is also joy in the language. Saki's sentences are elegant and pointed, full of well-turned phrases, and the story reads aloud beautifully, which matters for anyone listening to it. The hymn to Sredni Vashtar has a barbaric splendor, and the ending, with the distant screams and the slow, deliberate toasting, is a lesson in how to create suspense with almost no action.

Finally, readers admire the economy. A single short story manages to be a comedy, a horror tale and a satire in the span of a few pages. Writers have praised Saki's ability to say a great deal in a few words. He is a favorite of people who love sharp, concentrated prose, and this story is often named as his finest or one of his two or three finest. It rewards rereading, because once you know the ending, you see how much of the humor and menace was planted in the first paragraph.

There is also the thrill of the detail. The idea that a god might be worshipped only on Thursdays, or that a festival could be declared whenever a hated guardian suffers a toothache, is both funny and strangely moving. It tells us everything about a boy who has to find his triumphs where he can. Readers often say they can never forget the line about the nutmeg having to be stolen, a tiny rule of the religion that makes the whole invented faith feel real.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Not everyone has been comfortable with this story. The most obvious objection is moral. It presents the killing of an adult by an animal, at a child's wish, as a triumph. Some readers feel the story takes too much delight in the outcome and ignores the human cost. Mrs. De Ropp may be a bully, but she is a person, and the story's final image of a boy eating toast while she is carried in dead can feel callous.

A related criticism is that the story depends on a caricatured villain. Mrs. De Ropp is not shown acting from any inner life. She does not love, she does not doubt, and she does not seem to understand what she is doing. She is a type, the repressive guardian, and critics have said that her flatness makes the story's revenge too easy. Defenders reply that this is a fable and that fables work with types, and that the narrator makes a sly allowance for her when he says she would never admit to herself that she disliked the boy. Even so, the point stands.

Some critics have pointed to the story's darkness about childhood. The narrator treats the child's hatred and the fantasy of killing as natural and even healthy, which can be unsettling. A psychological reading might see Conradin's worship as a sign of a damaged child, not a triumphant one, and the story's cheerful tone as a refusal to admit this. Others note that the story is a product of Saki's own harsh upbringing and see it as personal score-settling.

There is also the usual charge against Saki: that his wit is cold, that his stories are too neat and that they lean on a cruelty that stands in for depth. Early reviewers of his collections praised his cleverness but sometimes found him heartless, and that reputation has followed him. Others found his world too narrow, a world of drawing rooms, aunts and clever young men, and his politics and attitudes, which now and then include the prejudices of his class and time, can sit awkwardly with modern readers. This particular story is mostly free of those problems, but it shares the tone that provoked them.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

In the big picture, Sredni Vashtar belongs to the Edwardian short story, a period when the form flourished in magazines and collections, and to the tradition of the comic macabre, in which wit and horror share a bed. Saki is often placed beside O. Henry, for his twist endings, and beside Oscar Wilde, for his epigrammatic wit. He shares with Wilde a taste for turning polite society's values upside down.

The story descends from the English fairy-tale and fable, with their small creatures that wreak big vengeance, and from the Victorian tradition of children's stories in which a child suffers under a stern guardian, as in Dickens and Charlotte Bronte's Jane Eyre. But where Dickens usually reforms or rescues his orphans through kindness, Saki rescues his through violence. That is the twist that makes the story modern. It also draws on the fashion for pagan and nature-worship themes in the years around 1900, when writers such as Kenneth Grahame, with his god Pan in The Wind in the Willows, and Arthur Machen were rediscovering older, wilder kinds of belief. Conradin's hutch is a small, comic cousin of those wild gods.

Its descendants include the tradition of dark children's literature that runs through Dahl, Edward Gorey and the stories of Lemony Snicket, and the horror-comedy of writers like Shirley Jackson and Muriel Spark, who share Saki's gift for the smiling menace. It also has relatives in the psychological tales of lonely children, from Henry James's The Turn of the Screw to modern novels of troubled young narrators.

Why read it today? Because it is very short and very good, and because it shows how a perfectly turned story can surprise even a reader who has read a great deal. It also still speaks to anyone who has been told what is good for them by someone who does not listen. Its ending refuses to moralize, and we are left to decide for ourselves whether to cheer or to shudder. Most readers do a little of both, and that mixed response is the best argument that the story is more than a joke.

It is also an easy story to carry around. It takes about ten minutes to read aloud, and it leaves a listener with something to argue about afterward. Anyone who wants to understand why short fiction can hit harder than a long novel could do worse than start here.
