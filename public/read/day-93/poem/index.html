Nightly Poem • Pastoral & Elegy

She Weeps Over Rahoon

James Joyce (1927) • 3 min read

As grey nettles and soft rain fall over Rahoon Cemetery in Galway, Nora Barnacle’s grief for her young dead suitor Michael Bodkin echoes the final pages of Joyce’s 'The Dead.'

Key Context & Why It Matters

This short, twelve-line poem by James Joyce is a quiet graveyard lyric that sits behind one of the most famous endings in English fiction. Joyce wrote it in 1913 while living in Trieste, the Adriatic port city where he taught English and wrote Dubliners. In 1912 he had visited Galway with his partner Nora Barnacle, and she took him to Rahoon cemetery, where a young man who had once loved her lay buried. Joyce was struck by the thought of a dead rival whose love had been untouched by time. It appeared in 1927 in Pomes Penyeach, a tiny book of thirteen poems published in Paris and sold for a shilling. Its feeling, a living woman haunted by someone who died for love of her, is the same one that closes Joyce's story "The Dead." The poem is about grief, memory and the way the dead stay younger than us, until we join them.

Rain on Rahoon falls softly, softly falling,

Where my dark lover lies.

Sad is his voice that calls me, sadly calling,

At grey moonrise.

Love, hear thou

How soft, how sad his voice is ever calling,

Ever unanswered, and the dark rain falling,

Then as now.

Dark too our hearts, O love, shall lie and cold

As his sad heart has lain

Under the moongrey nettles, the black mould

And muttering rain.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem begins in a graveyard, in rain. Rahoon is a cemetery near Galway, on the west coast of Ireland, and the first line, "Rain on Rahoon falls softly, softly falling," sets the mood at once. We then learn who is buried there: "my dark lover," a man the speaker loved or was loved by. The title tells us the speaker is a woman, and the biographical background tells us she is modeled on Nora Barnacle, Joyce's partner and later wife. She imagines his voice calling to her at "grey moonrise," a sad voice, "ever unanswered."

At the end of the first stanza the poem starts to pull us in two directions. In the second stanza she turns to a living companion with the words, "Love, hear thou / How soft, how sad his voice is ever calling." Who is this living love? It is, in the poem's imagined situation, the person walking beside her, the one she is speaking to. In real life it was Joyce himself. The poem is the portrait of someone listening to a dead man's voice while another man listens to her listening. Joyce is writing about his own jealousy and about the sort of love that cannot compete with someone who died young.

The last stanza is where the poem opens into something larger. "Dark too our hearts, O love, shall lie and cold / As his sad heart has lain / Under the moongrey nettles, the black mould / And muttering rain." The speaker now includes herself and her living lover in the grave. The dead man is not just a rival or a ghost. He shows what is coming to everyone. The tenderness of the poem lies in this shift: the living stop being envious of the dead and recognize themselves in him. The rain that falls on his grave "then as now" is falling on the whole human story.

Notice the repetition built into almost every line. "Softly, softly falling." "Sad is his voice that calls me, sadly calling." "How soft, how sad his voice is ever calling." Words and their cousins return again and again, so that the poem sounds like rain itself, or like a voice that keeps saying the same thing because it has nothing else to say. This is how grief works: it circles.

The best way to understand this poem is to read it alongside the closing pages of "The Dead," in which a woman named Gretta Conroy tells her husband about a boy named Michael Furey who died after standing in the rain outside her window, and her husband looks out at the snow falling across Ireland on "all the living and the dead." The poem is a smaller, more lyrical version of that same moment, the strange, humbling discovery that someone you love was once loved by someone who is gone.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The first thing to say is that this is a beautifully made, compact piece of music. The rhymes are interlaced in an unusual way, so that the ear keeps catching echoes. "Falling" rhymes with "calling," "lies" nearly rhymes with "moonrise," and the closing stanza brings back "cold" and "mould" with "lain" and "rain." The repeated "softly, softly" and "sadly calling" act like a refrain, and there is a hushed, slow music running all through it. Joyce had studied singing and wrote with a musician's sense of how vowels sound, and the dark, rounded vowels here, "moongrey," "mould," "mutter," suit the subject exactly.

The imagery is concentrated and effective. The final two lines, "Under the moongrey nettles, the black mould / And muttering rain," are among the most vivid in his poetry. Nettles and mould are not pretty things. They bring a rough, bodily reality into a poem that might otherwise be too soft. The word "muttering" is especially good, since it makes the rain sound like a murmuring voice, a crowd of the dead, speaking low.

The most impressive technical achievement is the emotional turn, which comes without announcement. The poem seems to be about a dead lover, then about a speaker and her present love, and then, almost in one breath, about the shared mortality of all. The movement happens in the word "too": "Dark too our hearts, O love, shall lie and cold." A single small word shifts the poem from private sorrow to universal fact.

Where does it fall short? Honest readers would point to a few things. The diction is old-fashioned even for 1913. "Thou," "O love," and the inverted word order, "Dark too our hearts," belong to the Victorian and Romantic traditions rather than the modern world Joyce was helping to create. A reader coming from his prose might feel the distance between this hushed, elegant voice and the dazzling, plain-spoken, comic voice of Ulysses. Some find the poem a little too composed and too tidy, its sadness arranged like flowers.

There is also a dependency on context. Without the biographical story, or the memory of "The Dead," the poem can feel generic. Its dark lover is barely sketched, and its feeling, while real, is expressed in fairly standard graveyard language of rain, moon and mould. The poem is moving, but it is not especially surprising. A cynical reader might say that it takes a very particular and unusual situation, a husband's jealousy of a dead boy, and files it down into a general elegy.

Still, those complaints are small. In twelve short lines, Joyce makes a graveyard sing, and the last line lingers. It is not his greatest work, but it is a very finished one.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be wrong to say this poem changed literature. It is a minor work by a major writer, and it appeared in a very small volume. But it has an impact of a particular and interesting kind: it illuminates one of the greatest short stories in the English language, and it shows how a writer's life passes into his work.

Pomes Penyeach was published in Paris in 1927 by Sylvia Beach's bookshop, Shakespeare and Company, the same shop that had published Ulysses. The title is a pun on the old price of apples, "penny each," and the volume contained thirteen poems, so you got a baker's dozen for a shilling. The poems were written over about twenty years. Joyce was by then famous and controversial, and the book was a small, modest surprise from a writer known for huge, difficult novels. It gave readers a glimpse of the lyric poet beneath.

The poem's most lasting influence has been on Joyce scholarship. Biographers, most famously Richard Ellmann in his great life of Joyce, have used it, along with the story of Nora's Galway past, to understand how deeply Joyce was affected by her earlier loves. It is one of the clearest bridges between his life and his fiction. When people teach "The Dead," they often bring up the poem, and when they teach the poem, they bring up the story. The two together make a case study in how personal feeling becomes art. It is worth noting, however, that biographers disagree about some details of the young man in Nora's past, including his name and age, and the poem is best read for its feeling rather than as a record of facts.

The poem has also been set to music, as Joyce's lyrics often have been. Composers have found in it the same drifting, rain-like quality that readers do. These settings have carried it to audiences who know the words as a song before they know them as a poem.

Beyond that, the poem has become a reference point in discussions of how Joyce wrote about the dead. Throughout his work, the dead are present: in Dubliners, in the buried father figures of Ulysses, in the dream-voices of Finnegans Wake. This poem is among the plainest statements of his sense that the dead are not gone but are a kind of weather, falling over the living. Readers who come to Joyce through his difficult novels often find in these short poems a surprising warmth. It's a gentler gateway than they expect, and a useful reminder that behind the experimental techniques was a man who felt things very simply and very deeply.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who love this poem tend to love its tenderness. It is not a loud poem, and it does not argue. It simply stands in the rain and listens. There is something deeply sympathetic in the way the speaker neither resents nor pities the dead man but sees herself in him. For many readers that generosity is the heart of it. A jealous living person looks at a grave and realizes that jealousy is pointless, because the grave is waiting for them too. It is a very humane idea, quietly stated.

They also love how the poem sounds. The soft repeated words, the rain image, the slow turning of the stanzas make a kind of lullaby for grief. It is the sort of poem that people memorize without trying. Even someone who knows nothing about Joyce can respond to "Rain on Rahoon falls softly, softly falling," a line that does what it says, moving as slowly as drizzle.

For devoted Joyceans, the poem is a treasure because it opens a door onto the man. Joyce's novels can be intimidating, packed with puns, parodies and encyclopedic reference. This poem is bare by comparison. It reveals that he was capable of simple, direct feeling, and it connects to the emotional core of "The Dead," which many readers consider the finest story he wrote. Knowing that Joyce wrote this poem and that story out of the same experience, the visit to a Galway graveyard with Nora, makes both richer. The reader begins to feel the person behind the work.

Another thing readers appreciate is the way it handles a very ordinary, very painful human situation. Most of us have felt, at some point, the odd pang of learning that someone we love once loved another person deeply, or that someone's attention is partly turned toward a past we cannot enter. The poem does not solve that problem. It simply gives it a place to stand, in a graveyard at dusk, and lets it become something larger and calmer.

Finally, there is its brevity. Twelve lines, three short stanzas, and the whole thing can be read in under a minute. Yet it holds a full emotional arc, from rain to grave to shared mortality. Readers who like their poems compact, musical and haunting find it ideal. It is a poem to read at the end of a long day, when you want something quiet, and a little sad, and true.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

As with much of Joyce's poetry, this poem has not attracted fierce controversy. It is a modest work in a modest book, and most criticism of it comes in the form of a lukewarm shrug rather than an attack. But there are real points of pushback worth knowing about.

The first is that the poetry of Pomes Penyeach is generally regarded as lesser Joyce. Critics have often said that his gifts as a novelist, his comic energy, his ear for speech, his boldness of form, are largely absent from these delicate, old-fashioned lyrics. Joyce seems to have held them lightly himself, and some of his admirers treat them as a sideline. Within that low expectation, this poem is usually ranked among the better ones. But some readers do feel that it is too conventional, that it relies on an older poetic vocabulary of moons, nettles and rain that had been used many times before.

A second criticism is that the poem leans too heavily on its backstory. Without "The Dead" and Joyce's biography, say these readers, it is a pleasant but somewhat vague graveyard poem. The emotional force depends on knowing who the "dark lover" is. That is a fair point: if a poem needs a footnote to land, it may not be fully self-sufficient. Others reply that the poem works well enough alone, as a general meditation on death, and that the biography simply adds depth.

A third area of debate concerns the biography itself. For a long time the story, as told by Ellmann, was that Nora's Galway lover was a boy who died young of illness, and that his death inspired both the poem and the story. More recent biographers have complicated this, noting that the details, who exactly the young man was, how old he was, and how much Joyce embellished, are less certain than the legend suggests. The poem should therefore be read as an imaginative work rather than a documentary one. Critics warn against treating it as a simple record.

There is also a feminist question that some scholars raise. The poem is called "She Weeps Over Rahoon," and it gives a woman's grief, but it is written by a man imagining her feelings and then putting his own jealousy into her mouth. Readers have asked whether Nora's real voice is heard at all, or whether she is turned into a figure in Joyce's drama. The poem does not answer that, and it is a worthwhile thing to hold in mind when reading.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This poem belongs to an old and rich family: the graveyard elegy, the poem that stands beside a grave and thinks about life. It descends from a long line of such works in English, from Thomas Gray's famous churchyard poem in the eighteenth century, with its owls and yew trees, through the Romantic and Victorian poets who loved a moonlit cemetery. Joyce's version is compressed and musical rather than expansive and moralizing, but it shares the central idea that a grave is a mirror for the living.

It also belongs to the tradition of the lyric as song, which runs through Irish poetry in particular. The Irish language poets and the songwriters and ballad-makers of the nineteenth century had long put grief and love into short, singable forms. Joyce, who loved Irish melodies and sang them himself, brought that quality into English. You can hear a kind of keening in the poem, the old Irish custom of lamenting the dead aloud.

In terms of Joyce's own work, the poem is a close sibling to "The Dead," the long final story of Dubliners, which Joyce had finished several years earlier, in 1907. The story gives us Gretta Conroy and the boy who died for her, and ends with one of the great passages in English prose, in which snow falls over the whole of Ireland, over the graveyard where Michael Furey lies, and "upon all the living and the dead." The poem is a lyric answer to that scene, offering the same revelation in miniature. If you have read the story, you will hear it in the poem. If you read the poem first, you will find the story waiting for you.

Other writers have also treated the dead as a presence among the living. Yeats wrote poems of ghostly voices, and the Irish tradition is full of them. In the wider world, you might think of Thomas Hardy's poems about his dead wife, which also combine plain feeling with a haunted setting, or the American poet Emily Dickinson, whose short, intense poems often look at death from very near.

Why read it now? It is short, quiet and beautiful, and it offers an experience that has not changed for centuries: standing in the rain by a grave, hearing a voice that cannot answer. It also teaches something important about humility. The living, the poem suggests, should not be jealous of the dead, because the dead are only ahead of us on a road we all take. That is an old thought, but Joyce says it in a way that feels fresh. For anyone curious about the man behind Ulysses, it is a small, moving key to his heart.
