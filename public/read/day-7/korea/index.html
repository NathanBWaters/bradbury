Jikji (1377) and the 81,258 Woodblocks of the Tripitaka Koreana

Korea — How Goryeo monks invented movable metal type seventy-eight years before Gutenberg and carved 52 million characters without a single error (Goryeo Dynasty (1236–1377)) • 11 min read

“Printed with cast metal type in the seventh month of the seventh year of Seongwang (1377) at Heungdeoksa Temple outside Cheongju.” — Colophon of Jikji Simche Yojeol

Ask almost anyone in the Western world who invented movable metal printing type, and they will name Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz, Germany, around 1455. Yet resting inside a climate-controlled vault at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris is a thirty-eight-sheet volume of Zen Buddhist teachings titled *Baegun Hwasang Chorok Buljo Jikji Simche Yojeol*—known simply as *Jikji* ('Anthology of Great Buddhist Priests' Zen Teachings')—whose final page bears a crisp, unmistakable colophon: it was printed with cast movable metal type at Heungdeoksa Temple in Cheongju, Korea, in July 1377, fully *seventy-eight years* before Gutenberg’s Bible.

In fact, *Jikji* was not even the first book printed with movable metal type in the Kingdom of Goryeo (918–1392)—it is simply the oldest surviving physical copy. In 1234, more than two centuries before Gutenberg, Goryeo civil minister Choe Yun-ui recorded printing twenty-eight copies of the fifty-volume civil code *Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun* ('Prescribed Ritual Texts of the Past and Present') using cast bronze type on Ganghwa Island after the royal library had burned during the Mongol invasions.

How did thirteenth- and fourteenth-century Korean metallurgists cast thousands of durable, uniform bronze characters when eleventh-century Chinese experiments by Bi Sheng had stopped at fragile baked-clay type? Drawing on Goryeo’s world-renowned bronze-casting mastery—already famous for temple bells and silver-inlaid bronze incense burners—artisan monks carved each mirror-image character into fine beeswax, packed a mold of fine river sand and clay mixed with paper pulp around the wax, melted the wax out in a kiln, and poured molten bronze alloy (copper, tin, and lead) into the cavity. Once cooled, the bronze letters were filed to uniform height, arranged in iron frames bedded with beeswax and pine resin, inked with pine-soot water-based ink (*meok*), and pressed by hand with a horsehair pad onto resilient mulberry bark paper (*hanji*).

Yet during the exact same era that Goryeo pioneered movable metal type for rapid, small-run editions, the kingdom also undertook the most stupendous woodblock-carving project in human history: the *Palman Daejanggyeong* (팔만대장경, literally the 'Eighty-Thousand Tripitaka'), known globally as the *Tripitaka Koreana*. In 1232, invading Mongol armies burned the first eleventh-century woodblock Buddhist canon housed at Buinsa Temple near Daegu. Refusing to surrender from their island refuge on Ganghwa, King Gojong and the Goryeo court vowed in 1236 to re-carve the entire Buddhist canon from scratch as a nationwide act of spiritual devotion and cultural preservation.

Consider the sheer material and human scale of what unfolded over the next fifteen years (1236–1251). Teams of foresters harvested thousands of mature silver magnolia (*hubaek*), wild cherry (*sanbeot*), and white birch trees from the southern islands of Geoje, Namhae, and 완도 (Wando). To prevent the wood from cracking, warping, or rotting over centuries, logs were submerged in seawater for three years, cut into planks, boiled in saltwater mixed with bitter plant extracts to repel wood-boring insects, and then air-dried in the shade for another three years before a single chisel touched the grain.

Once seasoned, master calligraphers wrote out 52,382,960 Chinese characters across 81,258 double-sided wooden blocks—each measuring 69.6 centimeters wide, 24 centimeters high, 3.8 centimeters thick, and weighing roughly 3.2 kilograms—framed with copper-plated corners to prevent warping. Every time a monk-carver raised his chisel to cut a character in reverse relief, he bowed completely to the floor in prayer (*ilja ilbae*—'one character, one prostration'). Across more than fifty-two million characters—equivalent to roughly four hundred full-length novels—modern scholars have found not a single erratum or typographical omission.

In 1398, early in the Joseon Dynasty, the 81,258 woodblocks were carried on the backs of thousands of monks and soldiers from Ganghwa Island up the misty valleys of Mount Gayasan to *Haeinsa Temple*, where they were housed in a purpose-built four-hall complex called the *Janggyeong Panjeon* (장경판전). Here, fifteenth-century Korean architects achieved a passive climate-control miracle that still baffles modern HVAC engineers. While modern concrete conservation vaults with electronic humidifiers built at Haeinsa in the 1970s developed mold during tests and had to be abandoned, the fifteenth-century wooden *Janggyeong Panjeon* has kept all 81,258 blocks free of rot, mold, and insects for over six hundred years without electricity.

How does the *Janggyeong Panjeon* work? The halls sit at 655 meters elevation on a southwest-facing mountain slope where valley breezes rise naturally. Beneath the earthen floors, builders dug deep trenches layered from bottom to top with charcoal, lime, salt, and sand: during humid summer monsoons, the charcoal and salt absorb excess moisture from the air; during bone-dry winters, they release stored moisture back into the room. Most brilliantly, the slatted wooden windows (*salchang*) have different sizes and heights on the front and back walls—large low windows at the bottom and small high windows at the top on the south side, and the exact reverse on the north side—creating a constant, gentle thermal convection vortex that circulates air evenly around every single woodblock on the five-tier racks.

When *Jikji*—collected in Seoul in the 1880s by French diplomat Victor Collin de Plancy and donated to the Bibliothèque nationale in 1950—was rediscovered in Paris in 1972 by visionary Korean librarian Dr. Park Byeong-seon after years of solitary research in the French archives, the world was forced to rewrite the history of information technology. Both *Jikji* and the *Tripitaka Koreana* now stand on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register: twin monuments to a nation that responded to invasion not merely with walls, but by casting bronze type and carving fifty-two million words into immortal wood.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Movable Metal Type vs. Woodblocks: Why Korea Used Both

Historians often ask why Goryeo and Joseon continued carving woodblocks even after inventing movable bronze type (such as the 1377 Jikji type and King Sejong's hyper-precise 1434 Gabinja bronze type). The answer is economic and bibliographic: Classical Chinese required thousands of distinct character matrices. When the state needed 100 or 200 copies of a new history, law code, or medical manual, movable metal type was vastly faster and cheaper because the bronze letters could be melted down or re-sorted immediately. When a text needed to be reprinted identically across centuries—such as the Buddhist Tripitaka or Confucian classics—carved woodblocks functioned as permanent 'hard drives' ready to print on demand.

2. Dr. Park Byeong-seon and the 1972 Rediscovery of Jikji

For decades, rumors circulated that an ancient Korean metal-type book sat somewhere in Europe, buried among millions of uncatalogued volumes. Working at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, Korean historian Dr. Park Byeong-seon (1923–2011) combed through dusty storage reserves until she located the lower volume of Jikji, proving through microscopic analysis of the ink squeeze marks, misaligned corner characters, and the 1377 Heungdeoksa colophon that it had been printed from cast metal type. Displayed at the 1972 International Book Year exhibition in Paris, Jikji inspired UNESCO to establish the UNESCO/Jikji Memory of the World Prize in 2004. 3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Master three essential terms of Korean print heritage: first, Jikji (직지), shorthand for Baegun Hwasang Chorok Buljo Jikji Simche Yojeol ('Pointing Directly to the Mind of the Buddha'), printed in Cheongju in 1377; second, Palman Daejanggyeong (팔만대장경, Tripitaka Koreana), the 81,258 birch-and-magnolia woodblocks containing 52.3 million characters without a single error; and third, Hanji (한지), traditional Korean handmade paper made from the inner bark of paper mulberry (daknamu), whose long, alkaline fibers last over a thousand years whereas nineteenth-century Western wood-pulp paper turns brittle and acidic in decades.
