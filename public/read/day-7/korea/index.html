Jikji (1377) and the 81,258 Woodblocks of the Tripitaka Koreana

Korea — How Goryeo monks invented movable metal type seventy-eight years before Gutenberg and carved 52 million characters without a single error () • 20 min read

“Printed with cast metal type in the seventh month of the seventh year of Seongwang (1377) at Heungdeoksa Temple outside Cheongju.” — Colophon of Jikji Simche Yojeol

Key Context & Why It Matters

Two of the greatest milestones in the global history of information technology were forged inside medieval Korean Buddhist monasteries during centuries of foreign invasion and national crisis. In July 1377, at Heungdeoksa Temple in Cheongju—seventy-eight years before Johannes Gutenberg's Mainz Bible—Goryeo monks printed Jikji Simche Yojeol, the world's oldest extant book printed with movable cast-metal type, cast using the lost-wax bronze method. A century earlier, between 1236 and 1251, as Mongol armies burned Korea, artisans carved 52,382,960 Chinese characters across 81,258 saltwater-cured birchwood blocks to create the Tripitaka Koreana (Palman Daejanggyeong), still preserved without warping or rot inside the naturally ventilated fifteenth-century Janggyeong Panjeon halls of Haeinsa Temple on Mount Gaya.

Inside the Reserve of Precious Books at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, resting within an acid-free archival box under strict climate control, lies a slender, thirty-eight-sheet volume stitched with five holes of twisted hemp thread. Its mulberry-paper cover bears the French shelf mark *Coréen 109*, while its opening page announces in crisp black ink: *Baegun Hwasang Chorok Buljo Jikji Simche Yojeol* (백운화상초록불조직지심체요절), known to the world simply as *Jikji* ('Anthology of Great Buddhist Priests’ Zen Teachings Pointing Directly to the Essence of the Mind'). Turn to the thirty-eighth and final leaf, however, and you will read an eleven-character vertical colophon that overturned five centuries of Eurocentric history: *'Printed with cast metal type at Heungdeoksa Temple outside Cheongju in the seventh month of the seventh year of Seongwang [July 1377].'* That date precedes Johannes Gutenberg’s 42-line Bible in Mainz, Germany, by seventy-eight years.

Even more remarkably, *Jikji* was not the dawn of movable metal printing in the Kingdom of Goryeo (918–1392), but a late provincial flower of a technology that Korean metallurgists had mastered more than a century earlier. In his 1241 collected works *Dongguk Isanggukjip* (동국이상국집), Goryeo prime minister Yi Gyu-bo preserved a formal preface written on behalf of civil minister Choe Yun-ui in 1234. Two years after the Goryeo royal court had evacuated its mainland capital of Gaegyeong (Kaesong) to the fortress island of Ganghwa to resist the invading Mongol Empire, Choe noted that court ritual manuals had perished in the burning of the royal library. Rather than waiting years to carve wooden blocks, Choe ordered artisans on Ganghwa Island to cast movable metal type (*juja*, 주자) and print twenty-eight sets of the fifty-volume civil code *Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun* (상정고금예문)—more than two hundred and twenty years before a single sheet of movable metal type was pulled in Europe.

Why did movable metal type emerge first in thirteenth-century Korea rather than in Song Dynasty China, where the artisan Bi Sheng had experimented with baked-clay characters (*jiaoni huozi*) between 1041 and 1048? As the Song polymath Shen Kuo recorded in his *Dream Pool Essays* (1088), earthenware type suffered from severe material flaws: clay letters shrank and warped unevenly in the kiln, chipped under pressure, and reacted poorly with thin, water-based pine-soot ink. Goryeo, by contrast, possessed the most sophisticated non-ferrous bronze-casting industry in the medieval world—a craft lineage famous for monumental temple bells and silver-inlaid bronze incense burners (*hyangwan*). Confronted during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries with wartime fires, shortages of imported books, and a scholarly bureaucracy requiring rapid editions of histories, medical treatises, and sutras in runs of fifty to two hundred copies, Goryeo foundry masters transferred their lost-wax bell-casting techniques down to the scale of a fingernail.

Reconstructed through archaeological metallurgy and the living craftsmanship of National Intangible Cultural Heritage *Jujajang* (주자장, metal-type casting master) Im In-ho at the Cheongju Early Printing Museum, the Goryeo *milnap jujo* (밀랍주조, beeswax lost-wax casting) process is a marvel of precision engineering. First, the caster melts pure beeswax with pine resin or tallow and pours it into smooth sticks. A calligrapher writes each Chinese character onto ultra-thin *hanji* mulberry paper and pastes the sheet face-down onto the wax so the strokes appear in mirror reverse. With a steel graver, the carver cuts away the negative space around each stroke and slices the stick into individual three-dimensional wax dies. Each wax character is fused onto a central wax sprue branch and encased inside a refractory slurry of fine clay from the Musimcheon stream, powdered charcoal, and beaten paper pulp. When the dried mold is baked in a kiln, the wax melts out through a gate hole, leaving a cavity into which molten Goryeo bronze—roughly 80 percent copper, 13 percent tin, and 6 percent lead—is poured at 1,050°C.

Once the cooled clay mold is broken open and the bronze characters are sawn from their branches and filed uniform, the printer faces a second challenge: how to lock thousands of tiny bronze letters into a flat printing bed. In 1377 at Heungdeoksa Temple—a rural monastery lacking the massive state foundries of the capital—the monk-printers arranged their cast bronze characters inside an iron frame (*inpan*) lined with a warm bedding of beeswax, pine pitch, and resin. As the wax cooled, a flat iron plate was pressed across the tops of the letters to level their faces. Because East Asian water-based pine-soot ink (*songyeon-meok*) beads up on metal, and because a heavy wooden screw press would have crushed the wax matrix, the Goryeo printer brushed ink lightly across the type face, laid a dampened sheet of tough *hanji* paper on top, and rubbed the back by hand with a smooth pad wrapped in horsehair (*inmo buchae*). On the pages of *Jikji* in Paris today, that hand-cast process leaves clear forensic fingerprints: characters tilted by a few degrees, occasional letters set upside down, and dark ink halos squeezed around worn bronze edges.

The spiritual urgency inside *Jikji* is as compelling as the metallurgy that printed it. Its author-compiler, the Zen (*Seon*) master Baegun Gyeonghan (백운 경한, 1298–1374), lived through the twilight of the Goryeo Dynasty. In 1351, Baegun traveled to Yuan China and climbed Mount Tianmu in Zhejiang to study under the Linji (*Imje*) master Shiwu Qinggong (*Seogok Cheonggong*), receiving dharma transmission alongside his contemporary Taego Bou. Returning to Korea, Baegun retreated in 1372 to Seongbulsa Temple and distilled centuries of complex Ch’an records—the sayings of the Seven Buddhas of the Past, twenty-eight Indian patriarchs, and over a hundred Chinese and Korean Zen masters—into two compact volumes. His thesis was captured in the four-character maxim *Jikji Insim, Gyeonseong Seongbul* (직지인심 견성성불): if a person points directly to the nature of their own mind without clinging to scholastic hierarchy, they awaken immediately to Buddhahood. After Baegun died at Chwiamsa Temple in 1374, his disciples Seokchan (석찬) and Daljam (달잠), funded by the alms of the Buddhist nun Myodeok (묘덕), carried his manuscript to Heungdeoksa Temple in Cheongju in 1377 and cast it in bronze so rural monks could hold the Zen transmission in their hands.

Yet the story of Korean print culture holds an astonishing twin paradox: during the exact same era that Goryeo pioneered movable bronze type for rapid small runs, the kingdom also mobilized its entire population to execute the largest, most error-free woodblock carving project in human history—the *Palman Daejanggyeong* (팔만대장경, the 'Eighty-Thousand Tripitaka'), known globally as the *Tripitaka Koreana*. In the autumn of 1232, during the second Mongol invasion, Mongol cavalry under Sartai rode south to Mount Palgongsan near Daegu and burned Buinsa Temple, reducing to ashes the *Chojodaejanggyeong*—Goryeo’s first woodblock Buddhist canon, carved between 1011 and 1087. Four years later, in 1236, from his island court on Ganghwa, King Gojong and his ministers issued a royal vow: the nation would establish the *Daejang Dogam* (대장도감, Tripitaka Directorate) on Ganghwa Island and a southern branch (*Bundogam*) on Namhae Island to re-carve the entire canon from scratch—both as a prayer for national deliverance and an indestructible ark of Korean civilization.

The material preparation for the *Tripitaka Koreana* over the next fifteen years (1236–1251) reads like a modern aerospace specification. Because woodblocks carved from unseasoned timber warp, split, or fall prey to wood-boring beetles within decades, royal foresters sailed to the southern islands of Wando, Geoje, Namhae, and Jeju alongside Mount Jirisan to fell tens of thousands of straight-grained silver magnolia (*hubaek*), wild mountain cherry (*sanbeotnamu*), and white birch (*jajaknamu*) trees. Before a single plank was planed, the rough logs were submerged in coastal seawater lagoons for three full years so marine salts diffused deep into the wood fibers. Raised from the sea and sawn into boards, the planks were boiled in giant iron vats of saltwater infused with bitter herbal extracts to sterilize fungal spores and relax internal grain stresses, then stacked under shaded, wind-swept pavilions for another three years of slow air-drying. Six years elapsed between the axe stroke in the forest and the carver’s chisel.

When the seasoned planks finally reached the workshops of Ganghwa and Namhae, master calligraphers and *Gaksu* (각수, woodblock engravers) began a fifteen-year liturgical marathon. Each wooden block was planed to 69.6 centimeters wide, 24.0 centimeters high, and 3.8 centimeters thick, weighing roughly 3.25 kilograms. Calligraphers trained in the crystalline *Ouyang Xun* Kaishu style wrote out the sacred texts onto oiled mulberry tissue—twenty-three vertical columns per face, fourteen characters per column—which were pasted face-down onto both sides of the plank. Every monk-carver observed the discipline of *ilja ilbae* (일자일배—'one character, one prostration'): bowing his forehead to the floor in meditation before carving each Chinese character in raised reverse relief. Directing the editorial enterprise was the Haeinsa scholar-monk Sugi (수기), who cross-collated the Northern Song, Khitan Liao, and first Goryeo canons across thirty volumes of critical notes (*Goryeoguk Sinjo Daejang Gyojeong Byeollok*). When the final block was consecrated in 1251, Goryeo had carved 81,258 double-sided woodblocks comprising 1,496 titles across 6,568 volumes—52,382,960 characters without a single known typographical omission.

Inspect a single block of the *Tripitaka Koreana* up close and you see how Goryeo woodworkers engineered each piece to survive a millennium. Because wood expands and contracts perpendicular to its grain during seasonal humidity swings, the left and right ends of every plank are fitted with thick vertical end-clamps called *maguri* (마구리), joined by mitered dovetail tenons that prevent cupping. Crucially, the *maguri* clamps are planed several millimeters thicker than the carved text surface itself; when woodblocks are stacked or slid side by side into racks, the carved characters never rub against one another, and a cushion of air circulates freely across both faces. Each block’s four corners are reinforced with hand-forged copper brackets (*dongpyeon*) fastened with iron brads, and the finished relief characters are sealed with natural toxic lacquer (*otchil*, 옻칠) that renders the wood impervious to moisture, mold, and insects.

For nearly a century and a half after 1251, the 81,258 woodblocks remained inside Seonwonsa Temple on Ganghwa Island. By the late fourteenth century, however, coastal raids by *Wako* (Japanese marauders) placed the island depository in danger. In 1398, the seventh year of King Taejo, founder of the Joseon Dynasty, the court ordered the entire canon evacuated from the coast to the deep mountain sanctuary of *Haeinsa Temple* (해인사, founded in 802 CE) nestled at 655 meters elevation on the forested slopes of Mount Gayasan in South Gyeongsang Province. Carrying 81,258 blocks—over 260 metric tons of carved wood—by river barge down the Han and Nakdong waterways and up steep mountain footpaths on the wooden A-frames (*jige*) of thousands of monks, soldiers, and villagers remains one of the greatest cultural rescue operations in history.

At Haeinsa, the blocks were housed in a quadrangle of four long wooden halls—rebuilt and expanded in 1488 under King Seongjong—known as the *Janggyeong Panjeon* (장경판전). For over five hundred years, while wars burned surrounding temples and humid monsoons rotted ordinary libraries, not a single woodblock inside the *Janggyeong Panjeon* warped or grew mold. In the early 1970s, convinced that modern engineering could protect the treasure even better, the South Korean government constructed a multimillion-dollar reinforced-concrete repository farther down the valley equipped with electronic HVAC chillers and dehumidifiers. When conservation scientists placed test woodblocks inside the concrete vault, condensation and mold appeared within months; the modern facility was abandoned, and the blocks remained inside their fifteenth-century wooden halls, which use zero electricity.

How did fifteenth-century Korean carpenter-architects build a passive climate-control chamber that outperformed modern mechanical engineering? Studies by the National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage (NRICH) revealed three interlocking systems. First is micro-topography: the *Janggyeong Panjeon* sits on the highest terrace of Haeinsa facing southwest, catching warm morning sun while cool valley breezes flow down from Mount Gayasan at night. Second is the subterranean floor battery: beneath the bare earthen floors of the two main thirty-bay halls (*Sudarajang* and *Beopbojeon*), builders excavated deep trenches filled with alternating strata of wood charcoal (*sut*), rammed yellow clay (*hwangto*), quicklime (*seokhoe*), solar salt, and river sand. During humid July monsoons, the charcoal and salt absorb excess moisture from the room air; during bone-dry January frosts, they release stored moisture back into the hall, stabilizing relative humidity around 55 to 65 percent while deterring termites and scorpions.

The third secret of the *Janggyeong Panjeon* is fluid dynamics sculpted in wood: the uneven slatted lattice windows (*salchang*, 살창). Along the sixty-meter front facade of the southern *Sudarajang* hall, the lower row of wooden grille windows near the floor is more than three times larger than the upper row under the eaves. On the northern rear wall of the same building, the geometry is inverted: the upper windows are large and the lower windows are small. Cooler, denser air enters through the wide lower front windows, flows across the earthen floor, rises vertically through the five-tier wooden racks holding the *Tripitaka* blocks on edge, and exits through the large upper rear windows—generating a perpetual thermal convection vortex that bathes all 81,258 woodblocks in fresh mountain air twenty-four hours a day.

While Haeinsa’s woodblocks guarded the Buddhist canon on Mount Gayasan, Goryeo’s movable metal type sparked a secular print revolution in the Joseon capital of Hanyang (Seoul). In 1403, King Taejong ordered the Royal Foundry (*Jujaso*) to cast the 100,000-character *Gyemija* bronze font, declaring that no government could rule wisely unless scholars had access to printed books. Three decades later, in 1434, King Sejong the Great and his chief engineer Jang Yeong-sil perfected the *Gabinja* (갑인자) bronze font: over 200,000 crisp characters cast with flat bases that locked mathematically into bronze chases wedged with bamboo strips—eliminating the beeswax bed and quadrupling daily printing speed from ten sheets to over forty sheets per press to print agricultural manuals, medical encyclopedias, and the 1446 *Hunminjeongeum*.

Meanwhile, the 1377 Heungdeoksa volume of *Jikji* embarked on a six-century journey across Eurasia. In 1887, Victor Collin de Plancy, the first French chargé d'affaires in Seoul, acquired the surviving second volume of *Jikji* and carried it to Paris, displaying it at the 1900 Paris Exposition before selling it in 1911 to collector Henri Vever, whose heirs donated it to the Bibliothèque nationale de France in 1950. There it sat forgotten in the stacks until 1967, when Korean historian Dr. Park Byeong-seon (박병선, 1923–2011) took a post at the BnF determined to find the book and prove its provenance. Working nights for five years to analyze its cast-metal impressions, Dr. Park unveiled *Jikji* at the 1972 International Book Year exhibition in Paris. Thirteen years later, in 1985, workers excavating a site in Uncheon-dong, Cheongju, struck a bronze Buddhist gong (*geumgu*) inscribed *'1376, Heungdeoksa Temple on Mount Seowonsan'*—pinpointing the exact stone foundations where Seokchan and Daljam had poured their molten bronze six centuries earlier.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Movable Metal Type vs. Woodblocks: Why Korea Used Both

Historians often ask why Goryeo and Joseon continued carving woodblocks even after inventing movable bronze type (such as the 1377 Jikji type and King Sejong's hyper-precise 1434 Gabinja bronze type). The answer is economic and bibliographic: Classical Chinese required thousands of distinct character matrices. When the state needed 100 or 200 copies of a new history, law code, or medical manual, movable metal type was vastly faster and cheaper because the bronze letters could be melted down or re-sorted immediately. When a text needed to be reprinted identically across centuries—such as the Buddhist Tripitaka or Confucian classics—carved woodblocks functioned as permanent 'hard drives' ready to print on demand. Close examination of Jikji (1377) and the 81,258 Woodblocks of the Tripitaka Koreana in Haeinsa Temple (Janggyeong Panjeon) & Heungdeoksa Temple Site, Cheongju (35.8012° N, 128.0981° E) highlights how primary historical records, physical craftsmanship, and enduring local geography reinforce every detail of this Korea account.

2. Dr. Park Byeong-seon and the 1972 Rediscovery of Jikji

For decades, rumors circulated that an ancient Korean metal-type book sat somewhere in Europe, buried among millions of uncatalogued volumes. Working at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, Korean historian Dr. Park Byeong-seon (1923–2011) combed through dusty storage reserves until she located the lower volume of Jikji, proving through microscopic analysis of the ink squeeze marks, misaligned corner characters, and the 1377 Heungdeoksa colophon that it had been printed from cast metal type. Displayed at the 1972 International Book Year exhibition in Paris, Jikji inspired UNESCO to establish the UNESCO/Jikji Memory of the World Prize in 2004.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Master three essential terms of Korean print heritage: first, Jikji (직지), shorthand for Baegun Hwasang Chorok Buljo Jikji Simche Yojeol ('Pointing Directly to the Mind of the Buddha'), printed in Cheongju in 1377; second, Palman Daejanggyeong (팔만대장경, Tripitaka Koreana), the 81,258 birch-and-magnolia woodblocks containing 52.3 million characters without a single error; and third, Hanji (한지), traditional Korean handmade paper made from the inner bark of paper mulberry (daknamu), whose long, alkaline fibers last over a thousand years whereas nineteenth-century Western wood-pulp paper turns brittle and acidic in decades.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Take a bus into Gayasan National Park to Haeinsa Temple (35.8012° N, 128.0981° E), climb the stone steps to the Janggyeong Panjeon, and peer through the uneven slatted wooden windows at the endless rows of black woodblocks resting in the cool mountain breeze. Then travel to the Cheongju Early Printing Museum, built directly over the excavated foundation stones of Heungdeoksa Temple where Jikji was printed in 1377, where master metal-casters demonstrate the beeswax bronze-type casting method live.
