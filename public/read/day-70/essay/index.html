Christmas and the Aesthetes

G. K. Chesterton (1905) • 12 min read

Anything that is truly ritual and human must be tied to a concrete hour and a concrete hearth, when the cold wind blows outside.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This is the sixth chapter of G. K. Chesterton's Heretics, published in 1905, and despite its title it begins nowhere near Christmas. It starts with two Victorian attempts to replace old religion with something modern. One is the Salvation Army, with its brass bands and street parades, which respectable people tolerated for its charity while sneering at its noise. The other is Positivism, a French philosopher's religion of humanity, complete with its own calendar and holidays, which its English followers embarrassedly kept only for its ideas. Chesterton reverses the usual verdict on both. The Army's loud methods, he says, are the best thing about it, and the Positivists' silly ceremonies were the only sensible part of theirs. Joy, he argues, needs rituals, and rituals need belief. He ends by teasing fashionable aesthetes who dream of revived maypoles but look down on the one great old festival still alive, Christmas. It is a funny, argumentative defense of noise, pudding and faith.

The world is round, so round that the schools of optimism and pessimism have been arguing from the beginning whether it is the right way up. The difficulty does not arise so much from the mere fact that good and evil are mingled in roughly equal proportions; it arises chiefly from the fact that men always differ about what parts are good and what evil. Hence the difficulty which besets “undenominational religions.” They profess to include what is beautiful in all creeds, but they appear to many to have collected all that is dull in them. All the colours mixed together in purity ought to make a perfect white. Mixed together on any human paintbox, they make a thing like mud, and a thing very like many new religions. Such a blend is often something much worse than any one creed taken separately, even the creed of the Thugs. The error arises from the difficulty of detecting what is really the good part and what is really the bad part of any given religion. And this pathos falls rather heavily on those persons who have the misfortune to think of some religion or other, that the parts commonly counted good are bad, and the parts commonly counted bad are good.

It is tragic to admire and honestly admire a human group, but to admire it in a photographic negative. It is difficult to congratulate all their whites on being black and all their blacks on their whiteness. This will often happen to us in connection with human religions. Take two institutions which bear witness to the religious energy of the nineteenth century. Take the Salvation Army and the philosophy of Auguste Comte.

The usual verdict of educated people on the Salvation Army is expressed in some such words as these: “I have no doubt they do a great deal of good, but they do it in a vulgar and profane style; their aims are excellent, but their methods are wrong.” To me, unfortunately, the precise reverse of this appears to be the truth. I do not know whether the aims of the Salvation Army are excellent, but I am quite sure their methods are admirable. Their methods are the methods of all intense and hearty religions; they are popular like all religion, military like all religion, public and sensational like all religion. They are not reverent any more than Roman Catholics are reverent, for reverence in the sad and delicate meaning of the term reverence is a thing only possible to infidels. That beautiful twilight you will find in Euripides, in Renan, in Matthew Arnold; but in men who believe you will not find it⁠—you will find only laughter and war. A man cannot pay that kind of reverence to truth solid as marble; they can only be reverent towards a beautiful lie. And the Salvation Army, though their voice has broken out in a mean environment and an ugly shape, are really the old voice of glad and angry faith, hot as the riots of Dionysus, wild as the gargoyles of Catholicism, not to be mistaken for a philosophy. Professor Huxley, in one of his clever phrases, called the Salvation Army “corybantic Christianity.” Huxley was the last and noblest of those Stoics who have never understood the Cross. If he had understood Christianity he would have known that there never has been, and never can be, any Christianity that is not corybantic.

And there is this difference between the matter of aims and the matter of methods, that to judge of the aims of a thing like the Salvation Army is very difficult, to judge of their ritual and atmosphere very easy. No one, perhaps, but a sociologist can see whether General Booth’s housing scheme is right. But any healthy person can see that banging brass cymbals together must be right. A page of statistics, a plan of model dwellings, anything which is rational, is always difficult for the lay mind. But the thing which is irrational anyone can understand. That is why religion came so early into the world and spread so far, while science came so late into the world and has not spread at all. History unanimously attests the fact that it is only mysticism which stands the smallest chance of being understanded of the people. Common sense has to be kept as an esoteric secret in the dark temple of culture. And so while the philanthropy of the Salvationists and its genuineness may be a reasonable matter for the discussion of the doctors, there can be no doubt about the genuineness of their brass bands, for a brass band is purely spiritual, and seeks only to quicken the internal life. The object of philanthropy is to do good; the object of religion is to be good, if only for a moment, amid a crash of brass.

And the same antithesis exists about another modern religion⁠—I mean the religion of Comte, generally known as Positivism, or the worship of humanity. Such men as Mr. Frederic Harrison, that brilliant and chivalrous philosopher, who still, by his mere personality, speaks for the creed, would tell us that he offers us the philosophy of Comte, but not all Comte’s fantastic proposals for pontiffs and ceremonials, the new calendar, the new holidays and saints’ days. He does not mean that we should dress ourselves up as priests of humanity or let off fireworks because it is Milton’s birthday. To the solid English Comtist all this appears, he confesses, to be a little absurd. To me it appears the only sensible part of Comtism. As a philosophy it is unsatisfactory. It is evidently impossible to worship humanity, just as it is impossible to worship the Savile Club; both are excellent institutions to which we may happen to belong. But we perceive clearly that the Savile Club did not make the stars and does not fill the universe. And it is surely unreasonable to attack the doctrine of the Trinity as a piece of bewildering mysticism, and then to ask men to worship a being who is ninety million persons in one God, neither confounding the persons nor dividing the substance.

But if the wisdom of Comte was insufficient, the folly of Comte was wisdom. In an age of dusty modernity, when beauty was thought of as something barbaric and ugliness as something sensible, he alone saw that men must always have the sacredness of mummery. He saw that while the brutes have all the useful things, the things that are truly human are the useless ones. He saw the falsehood of that almost universal notion of today, the notion that rites and forms are something artificial, additional, and corrupt. Ritual is really much older than thought; it is much simpler and much wilder than thought. A feeling touching the nature of things does not only make men feel that there are certain proper things to say; it makes them feel that there are certain proper things to do. The more agreeable of these consist of dancing, building temples, and shouting very loud; the less agreeable, of wearing green carnations and burning other philosophers alive. But everywhere the religious dance came before the religious hymn, and man was a ritualist before he could speak. If Comtism had spread the world would have been converted, not by the Comtist philosophy, but by the Comtist calendar. By discouraging what they conceive to be the weakness of their master, the English Positivists have broken the strength of their religion. A man who has faith must be prepared not only to be a martyr, but to be a fool. It is absurd to say that a man is ready to toil and die for his convictions when he is not even ready to wear a wreath round his head for them. I myself, to take a corpus vile, am very certain that I would not read the works of Comte through for any consideration whatever. But I can easily imagine myself with the greatest enthusiasm lighting a bonfire on Darwin Day.

That splendid effort failed, and nothing in the style of it has succeeded. There has been no rationalist festival, no rationalist ecstasy. Men are still in black for the death of God. When Christianity was heavily bombarded in the last century upon no point was it more persistently and brilliantly attacked than upon that of its alleged enmity to human joy. Shelley and Swinburne and all their armies have passed again and again over the ground, but they have not altered it. They have not set up a single new trophy or ensign for the world’s merriment to rally to. They have not given a name or a new occasion of gaiety. Mr. Swinburne does not hang up his stocking on the eve of the birthday of Victor Hugo. Mr. William Archer does not sing carols descriptive of the infancy of Ibsen outside people’s doors in the snow. In the round of our rational and mournful year one festival remains out of all those ancient gaieties that once covered the whole earth. Christmas remains to remind us of those ages, whether Pagan or Christian, when the many acted poetry instead of the few writing it. In all the winter in our woods there is no tree in glow but the holly.

The strange truth about the matter is told in the very word “holiday.” A bank holiday means presumably a day which bankers regard as holy. A half-holiday means, I suppose, a day on which a schoolboy is only partially holy. It is hard to see at first sight why so human a thing as leisure and larkiness should always have a religious origin. Rationally there appears no reason why we should not sing and give each other presents in honour of anything⁠—the birth of Michelangelo or the opening of Euston Station. But it does not work. As a fact, men only become greedily and gloriously material about something spiritualistic. Take away the Nicene Creed and similar things, and you do some strange wrong to the sellers of sausages. Take away the strange beauty of the saints, and what has remained to us is the far stranger ugliness of Wandsworth. Take away the supernatural, and what remains is the unnatural.

And now I have to touch upon a very sad matter. There are in the modern world an admirable class of persons who really make protest on behalf of that antiqua pulchritudo of which Augustine spoke, who do long for the old feasts and formalities of the childhood of the world. William Morris and his followers showed how much brighter were the dark ages than the age of Manchester. Mr. W. B. Yeats frames his steps in prehistoric dances, but no man knows and joins his voice to forgotten choruses that no one but he can hear. Mr. George Moore collects every fragment of Irish paganism that the forgetfulness of the Catholic Church has left or possibly her wisdom preserved. There are innumerable persons with eyeglasses and green garments who pray for the return of the maypole or the Olympian games. But there is about these people a haunting and alarming something which suggests that it is just possible that they do not keep Christmas. It is painful to regard human nature in such a light, but it seems somehow possible that Mr. George Moore does not wave his spoon and shout when the pudding is set alight. It is even possible that Mr. W. B. Yeats never pulls crackers. If so, where is the sense of all their dreams of festive traditions? Here is a solid and ancient festive tradition still plying a roaring trade in the streets, and they think it vulgar. If this is so, let them be very certain of this, that they are the kind of people who in the time of the maypole would have thought the maypole vulgar; who in the time of the Canterbury pilgrimage would have thought the Canterbury pilgrimage vulgar; who in the time of the Olympian games would have thought the Olympian games vulgar. Nor can there be any reasonable doubt that they were vulgar. Let no man deceive himself; if by vulgarity we mean coarseness of speech, rowdiness of behaviour, gossip, horseplay, and some heavy drinking, vulgarity there always was wherever there was joy, wherever there was faith in the gods. Wherever you have belief you will have hilarity, wherever you have hilarity you will have some dangers. And as creed and mythology produce this gross and vigorous life, so in its turn this gross and vigorous life will always produce creed and mythology. If we ever get the English back on to the English land they will become again a religious people, if all goes well, a superstitious people. The absence from modern life of both the higher and lower forms of faith is largely due to a divorce from nature and the trees and clouds. If we have no more turnip ghosts it is chiefly from the lack of turnips.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Chesterton begins with a thought about mixing. The world is so round, he says, that optimists and pessimists cannot even agree which way up it is, because people differ about which parts are good and which are bad. This is why "undenominational religions," which promise to blend the best of every creed, tend to collect the dullest parts of each. "All the colours mixed together in purity ought to make a perfect white. Mixed together on any human paintbox, they make a thing like mud." Worse, some people admire a religion in what he calls a photographic negative, praising what is bad in it and condemning what is good.

His first test case is the Salvation Army. The usual educated view is that they do a lot of good but in a vulgar style, that their aims are fine but their methods wrong. Chesterton says the opposite. He cannot say whether their aims are excellent, but their methods are admirable, because they are the methods of every hearty religion: popular, military, public and sensational. A famous scientist, Thomas Huxley, called the Army "corybantic Christianity," meaning frenzied and noisy, and Chesterton replies that there never has been, and never can be, any Christianity that is not corybantic. Judging aims, like General Booth's housing scheme, takes an expert. Judging a brass band is easy: it is "purely spiritual." The point is a clever one: "The object of philanthropy is to do good; the object of religion is to be good, if only for a moment, amid a crash of brass."

The second test case is Auguste Comte, the French thinker who founded Positivism, a religion that worshipped humanity and came with priests, a new calendar and saints' days for great men. English Positivists like Frederic Harrison kept the philosophy and dropped the ceremonies as absurd. Chesterton says the philosophy is the weak part, for you cannot worship humanity any more than you can worship your club, but the ceremonies were the wise part. Humans are ritual creatures, and "the religious dance came before the religious hymn." He jokes that he would never read Comte's works, but could happily light a bonfire on Darwin Day.

This leads to Christmas. The rationalists attacked Christianity as an enemy of human joy, yet they never set up one new festival. "Mr. Swinburne does not hang up his stocking on the eve of the birthday of Victor Hugo." Only Christmas survives of the old gaieties, and even the word "holiday" shows that leisure was once holy. His last target is the aesthetes, such as Yeats and George Moore, who long for ancient feasts yet may not pull crackers at Christmas. Wherever there is belief, there is hilarity, and with hilarity come dangers.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The essay's best quality is audacity. The Salvation Army argument reverses a commonplace so neatly that you cannot go back to the old version. The idea that we are comfortable judging the noise of a religion and uncomfortable judging its social program is a real insight, and it works as a comment on the way we all talk about charities, parties and causes. The line about philanthropy and religion, doing good versus being good "amid a crash of brass," is the kind of sentence that Chesterton alone seems to produce.

The material on ritual is also stronger than it first looks. The claim that ritual is older and wilder than thought was not just a joke. The Scottish scholar William Robertson Smith had argued in his 1889 lectures on the religion of the Semites that early religion was mostly something people did, not something they believed, and later scholars often agreed. Chesterton's version, that man was a ritualist before he could speak, anticipates a lot of later thinking about why shared ceremonies matter.

The comedy is dependable. The notion that a bank holiday must mean a day bankers regard as holy, and that a half-holiday is a day on which a schoolboy is only partially holy, is a graceful way to make a historical point. "Take away the supernatural, and what remains is the unnatural" is epigram at its best, and the closing joke about turnip ghosts and the lack of turnips ends the piece with a grin.

But the essay is flawed in ways worth admitting. Structurally, it is lopsided. The title promises Christmas and aesthetes, but Christmas does not really appear until two-thirds through, and the whole first half is a detour through the Army and Comte. The ending is abrupt, a joke and a shrug, and the essay does not so much conclude as stop. A reader may feel that Chesterton had three pieces in his notebook and decided to staple them together.

Fairness is another issue. To say that he does not know whether the Salvation Army's aims are excellent, when its work with the poor was one of the largest charitable efforts of the age, is flippant, and the claim that reverence is "only possible to infidels" is the kind of provocative sentence that falls apart when you press it. The teasing of Yeats and Moore is funny but also a bit unkind, since both were doing serious work reviving old culture, and guessing what they do at Christmas is unanswerable gossip. The claim that all holidays have a religious origin is also overstated, as his own phrase "whether Pagan or Christian" half concedes.

III. The Impact It Had

It is honest to say that this chapter is among the lesser-known parts of Heretics, and its direct influence is hard to trace. It was not a landmark that changed a field. What it did was strengthen a theme that would grow larger in Chesterton's work: the celebration of festivity, noise and the physical side of belief. He returned to Christmas often, in essays, in poems, and in the books on Dickens, and the defense of the loud, crowded, roast-and-pudding Christmas as a real spiritual event, not a degraded one, became part of his public identity. It also fed his larger case in Orthodoxy that Christianity is a joyful faith and not a gloomy one, answering the nineteenth-century charge that it was an enemy of human happiness.

The chapter's quotations have had a life of their own. "Take away the supernatural, and what remains is the unnatural" is widely quoted by religious writers, usually without the context of sausages and Wandsworth. The remark that the dance came before the hymn is a favourite of writers on liturgy and worship.

There are also some happy afterlives for its ideas. Comte's religion of humanity was not just a joke. It lived on in a Positivist Church in Brazil, and the motto of the Brazilian flag, Order and Progress, comes from his thought. The Salvation Army, meanwhile, grew into one of the largest charities in the world, and its brass bands and bell-ringers at Christmas have become part of the season in many countries, which would have delighted Chesterton. And Darwin Day, a celebration of Darwin's birthday on the twelfth of February, is today observed by scientific and humanist groups, as are some secular holidays in December, so the challenge that rationalists had no festivals was, in a small way, eventually taken up.

It is not usually taught in schools and has not been adapted. Its life is in anthologies, Christmas readings, and the reading lists of those who like Chesterton's playful side.

One more effect deserves mention, though it is hard to measure. The chapter taught a certain kind of reader to be unembarrassed. Many people raised among restrained, tasteful believers or tasteful unbelievers found in Chesterton a voice that said loudness and communal silliness were not signs of shallow faith but, often, of deep faith. That is a quiet kind of influence, passed along in conversations and Christmas toasts rather than in footnotes, but it may be the most real one the essay has.

IV. What Readers Loved

The easiest thing to say about what readers love is that this is Chesterton having a good time. The chapter is mostly jokes with a spine of conviction, and readers respond to that. A line like "Mr. William Archer does not sing carols descriptive of the infancy of Ibsen outside people's doors in the snow" makes the point about secular festivals better than any argument, because you picture it and laugh, and you realize he is right that it would be absurd.

Readers also love the permission it gives. The people who find Christmas too loud, too commercial, too sentimental, are told that its noisiness is the point and not the flaw. For anyone who has felt faintly embarrassed about enjoying brass bands, carols or an overcooked pudding, the essay is an affectionate defense. Chesterton makes the case that being a bit ridiculous in public, wearing a wreath or lighting a bonfire for what you believe, is a mark of sincerity. "A man who has faith must be prepared not only to be a martyr, but to be a fool" is the sort of line that readers of every belief, or none, have underlined.

There is also the pleasure of the Salvation Army argument. Many readers have felt uneasy about the way respectable people patronize noisy believers, and the essay turns the tables with relish. The Army, he says, has the old voice of "glad and angry faith, hot as the riots of Dionysus, wild as the gargoyles of Catholicism."

And readers with a taste for words enjoy the etymological play on the word holiday, a gentle demonstration that even our most ordinary leisure words carry old religion inside them.

Readers who are not religious sometimes say what they take from it is a question: if we want shared joy, what rituals do we actually have? The essay does not answer that, but it asks it in such a friendly way that people carry it with them. And for those who just love Christmas, it is a lovely thing to read in December, short, warm and a little mischievous.

There is also a gentler thing that readers mention. Chesterton never sounds as though he is lecturing from above. He talks about pudding and crackers as someone who has actually sat at the table, and the humour comes from a man who plainly likes the people he is teasing, including the aesthetes. That fondness is what keeps even his sharpest jokes from turning sour, and it is a good deal of why the essay still feels warm after more than a century.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most natural objection is from the people he is writing about. Salvationists took their charity seriously and might have been unhappy to hear that the brass band mattered more than the housing scheme. Social reformers would say that treating the aims of a poverty program as a matter for experts, while the music is for everyone, undersells the real work done to feed and shelter the poor. Chesterton's playful tone about whether the Army's aims are excellent is a joke that can look callous from a distance.

A second line of objection comes from secularists and humanists. They would argue that joy does not require creed. People celebrate birthdays, harvests, sports victories, seasons and weddings without any theology, and even those holidays with religious roots have often absorbed older, non-religious customs. Chesterton's claim that "men only become greedily and gloriously material about something spiritualistic" is a generalization that many anthropologists and historians would find too tidy. The theory that ritual precedes belief is also debated among scholars of religion, and nobody believes it is as simple as Chesterton makes it.

Third, there is the charge of nostalgia. Chesterton's English Christmas, with holly, pudding and crackers, is a comfortable, middle-class one, and critics note that it says nothing about people for whom December was a month of cold and hunger. Dickens at least put the Cratchits in the picture. The essay's fondness for the old Merry England is real but selective, and it does not ask who had the pudding.

Fourth, his treatment of Comte, Yeats and Moore has been seen as more dismissive than fair. Comte's attempt to build a humane, communal secular religion was a serious one, sustained by sincere people, and the aesthetes were doing important cultural work. To mock them by guessing that they do not pull crackers is an effective joke and a poor argument.

Fifth, and generally, there is the question of reputation. Chesterton's later writing on Jews has drawn serious criticism, and readers sometimes find it hard to enjoy his geniality without keeping that in mind. It does not appear in this chapter, but the complication is part of how he is read.

Finally, a plain structural complaint: the title promises more Christmas than it delivers.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The chapter belongs to a Victorian and Edwardian conversation about what could replace religion once it faded. Matthew Arnold heard the "melancholy, long, withdrawing roar" of the sea of faith, and offered poetry and culture as substitutes. Comte offered a church of humanity. The aesthetes of the 1890s offered beauty for its own sake. Others offered ethical societies and Fabian socialism. Chesterton's wry answer is that none of them could throw a party. They had ideas but not feasts, and a religion is a thing you do with your whole body and a crowd.

That intuition has a distinguished line of relatives. The French sociologist Émile Durkheim argued, in 1912, in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life that shared ritual produces a kind of collective excitement that binds a community, and that religion is at bottom about this. The Russian critic Mikhail Bakhtin wrote about carnival, the loud, grotesque festivity of the Middle Ages, as a form of freedom and renewal. Neither borrowed from Chesterton, but both are working on the same ground. In recent years the writer Alain de Botton has argued in Religion for Atheists that non-believers should learn from religions how to build communal rituals, an echo of Chesterton's complaint that the rationalists never set up a single trophy for the world's merriment.

Among Chesterton's own writing, it connects with his love of Dickens, whose Christmas books helped invent the modern English Christmas, and with the long essays in Orthodoxy about the gladness at the root of faith. It also connects, more distantly, to the distributist longing for a rooted, village life of feasts and customs, which he shared with William Morris, one of the figures he teases here.

Read next to its neighbours in Heretics, it is the lightest chapter. The Wells chapter is about giants, the Shaw chapter about wonder, the Kipling chapter about place. This one is about celebration, and it reminds you that Chesterton's real argument was always that life is a gift to be enjoyed noisily.

Why read it now? Because the question it asks, what do we do together that is joyful and shared, matters to a generation that has plenty of ideas and few communal rites. And because it is December somewhere, and a defense of the pudding is hard to resist.
