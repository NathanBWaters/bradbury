Misery

Anton Chekhov (1886) • 11 min read

“To Whom Shall I Tell My Grief?”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Anton Chekhov wrote Misery in 1886, when he was twenty-five or twenty-six, a young medical student and doctor turning out comic sketches for newspapers to make a living. It was one of the first stories in which he showed the quieter, sadder voice that would make him famous. It is short, only a few pages, and its situation could hardly be simpler. On a snowy night in Saint Petersburg, an old cab driver named Iona Potapov is grieving for his son, who died a few days ago, and he wants to tell someone. Nobody will listen. The story is a test of how much feeling a very small scene can carry. It asks what happens to grief that finds no one to receive it, and it suggests, without raising its voice, that the sorrow of an ordinary poor man deserves as much attention as any tragedy in a grand novel.

“To Whom Shall I Tell My Grief?”

The twilight of evening. Big flakes of wet snow are whirling lazily about the street lamps, which have just been lighted, and lying in a thin soft layer on roofs, horses’ backs, shoulders, caps. Iona Potapov, the sledge-driver, is all white like a ghost. He sits on the box without stirring, bent as double as the living body can be bent. If a regular snowdrift fell on him it seems as though even then he would not think it necessary to shake it off.⁠ ⁠… His little mare is white and motionless too. Her stillness, the angularity of her lines, and the stick-like straightness of her legs make her look like a halfpenny gingerbread horse. She is probably lost in thought. Anyone who has been torn away from the plough, from the familiar gray landscapes, and cast into this slough, full of monstrous lights, of unceasing uproar and hurrying people, is bound to think.

It is a long time since Iona and his nag have budged. They came out of the yard before dinnertime and not a single fare yet. But now the shades of evening are falling on the town. The pale light of the street lamps changes to a vivid color, and the bustle of the street grows noisier.

“Sledge to Vyborgskaya!” Iona hears. “Sledge!”

Iona starts, and through his snow-plastered eyelashes sees an officer in a military overcoat with a hood over his head.

“To Vyborgskaya,” repeats the officer. “Are you asleep? To Vyborgskaya!”

In token of assent Iona gives a tug at the reins which sends cakes of snow flying from the horse’s back and shoulders. The officer gets into the sledge. The sledge-driver clicks to the horse, cranes his neck like a swan, rises in his seat, and more from habit than necessity brandishes his whip. The mare cranes her neck, too, crooks her stick-like legs, and hesitatingly sets of.⁠ ⁠…

“Where are you shoving, you devil?” Iona immediately hears shouts from the dark mass shifting to and fro before him. “Where the devil are you going? Keep to the r-right!”

“You don’t know how to drive! Keep to the right,” says the officer angrily.

A coachman driving a carriage swears at him; a pedestrian crossing the road and brushing the horse’s nose with his shoulder looks at him angrily and shakes the snow off his sleeve. Iona fidgets on the box as though he were sitting on thorns, jerks his elbows, and turns his eyes about like one possessed as though he did not know where he was or why he was there.

“What rascals they all are!” says the officer jocosely. “They are simply doing their best to run up against you or fall under the horse’s feet. They must be doing it on purpose.”

Iona looks as his fare and moves his lips.⁠ ⁠… Apparently he means to say something, but nothing comes but a sniff.

“What?” inquires the officer.

Iona gives a wry smile, and straining his throat, brings out huskily: “My son⁠ ⁠… er⁠ ⁠… my son died this week, sir.”

“H’m! What did he die of?”

Iona turns his whole body round to his fare, and says:

“Who can tell! It must have been from fever.⁠ ⁠… He lay three days in the hospital and then he died.⁠ ⁠… God’s will.”

“Turn round, you devil!” comes out of the darkness. “Have you gone cracked, you old dog? Look where you are going!”

“Drive on! drive on!⁠ ⁠…” says the officer. “We shan’t get there till tomorrow going on like this. Hurry up!”

The sledge-driver cranes his neck again, rises in his seat, and with heavy grace swings his whip. Several times he looks round at the officer, but the latter keeps his eyes shut and is apparently disinclined to listen. Putting his fare down at Vyborgskaya, Iona stops by a restaurant, and again sits huddled up on the box.⁠ ⁠… Again the wet snow paints him and his horse white. One hour passes, and then another.⁠ ⁠…

Three young men, two tall and thin, one short and hunchbacked, come up, railing at each other and loudly stamping on the pavement with their goloshes.

“Cabby, to the Police Bridge!” the hunchback cries in a cracked voice. “The three of us,⁠ ⁠… twenty kopecks!”

Iona tugs at the reins and clicks to his horse. Twenty kopecks is not a fair price, but he has no thoughts for that. Whether it is a rouble or whether it is five kopecks does not matter to him now so long as he has a fare.⁠ ⁠… The three young men, shoving each other and using bad language, go up to the sledge, and all three try to sit down at once. The question remains to be settled: Which are to sit down and which one is to stand? After a long altercation, ill-temper, and abuse, they come to the conclusion that the hunchback must stand because he is the shortest.

“Well, drive on,” says the hunchback in his cracked voice, settling himself and breathing down Iona’s neck. “Cut along! What a cap you’ve got, my friend! You wouldn’t find a worse one in all Petersburg.⁠ ⁠…”

“He-he!⁠ ⁠… he-he!⁠ ⁠…” laughs Iona. “It’s nothing to boast of!”

“Well, then, nothing to boast of, drive on! Are you going to drive like this all the way? Eh? Shall I give you one in the neck?”

“My head aches,” says one of the tall ones. “At the Dukmasovs’ yesterday Vaska and I drank four bottles of brandy between us.”

“I can’t make out why you talk such stuff,” says the other tall one angrily. “You lie like a brute.”

“Strike me dead, it’s the truth!⁠ ⁠…”

“It’s about as true as that a louse coughs.”

“He-he!” grins Iona. “Me-er-ry gentlemen!”

“Tfoo! the devil take you!” cries the hunchback indignantly. “Will you get on, you old plague, or won’t you? Is that the way to drive? Give her one with the whip. Hang it all, give it her well.”

Iona feels behind his back the jolting person and quivering voice of the hunchback. He hears abuse addressed to him, he sees people, and the feeling of loneliness begins little by little to be less heavy on his heart. The hunchback swears at him, till he chokes over some elaborately whimsical string of epithets and is overpowered by his cough. His tall companions begin talking of a certain Nadyezhda Petrovna. Iona looks round at them. Waiting till there is a brief pause, he looks round once more and says:

“This week⁠ ⁠… er⁠ ⁠… my⁠ ⁠… er⁠ ⁠… son died!”

“We shall all die,⁠ ⁠…” says the hunchback with a sigh, wiping his lips after coughing. “Come, drive on! drive on! My friends, I simply cannot stand crawling like this! When will he get us there?”

“Well, you give him a little encouragement⁠ ⁠… one in the neck!”

“Do you hear, you old plague? I’ll make you smart. If one stands on ceremony with fellows like you one may as well walk. Do you hear, you old dragon? Or don’t you care a hang what we say?”

And Iona hears rather than feels a slap on the back of his neck.

“He-he!⁠ ⁠…” he laughs. “Merry gentlemen.⁠ ⁠… God give you health!”

“Cabman, are you married?” asks one of the tall ones.

“I? He he! Me-er-ry gentlemen. The only wife for me now is the damp earth.⁠ ⁠… He-ho-ho!⁠ ⁠… The grave that is!⁠ ⁠… Here my son’s dead and I am alive.⁠ ⁠… It’s a strange thing, death has come in at the wrong door.⁠ ⁠… Instead of coming for me it went for my son.⁠ ⁠…”

And Iona turns round to tell them how his son died, but at that point the hunchback gives a faint sigh and announces that, thank God! they have arrived at last. After taking his twenty kopecks, Iona gazes for a long while after the revelers, who disappear into a dark entry. Again he is alone and again there is silence for him.⁠ ⁠… The misery which has been for a brief space eased comes back again and tears his heart more cruelly than ever. With a look of anxiety and suffering Iona’s eyes stray restlessly among the crowds moving to and fro on both sides of the street: can he not find among those thousands someone who will listen to him? But the crowds flit by heedless of him and his misery.⁠ ⁠… His misery is immense, beyond all bounds. If Iona’s heart were to burst and his misery to flow out, it would flood the whole world, it seems, but yet it is not seen. It has found a hiding-place in such an insignificant shell that one would not have found it with a candle by daylight.⁠ ⁠…

Iona sees a house-porter with a parcel and makes up his mind to address him.

“What time will it be, friend?” he asks.

“Going on for ten.⁠ ⁠… Why have you stopped here? Drive on!”

Iona drives a few paces away, bends himself double, and gives himself up to his misery. He feels it is no good to appeal to people. But before five minutes have passed he draws himself up, shakes his head as though he feels a sharp pain, and tugs at the reins.⁠ ⁠… He can bear it no longer.

“Back to the yard!” he thinks. “To the yard!”

And his little mare, as though she knew his thoughts, falls to trotting. An hour and a half later Iona is sitting by a big dirty stove. On the stove, on the floor, and on the benches are people snoring. The air is full of smells and stuffiness. Iona looks at the sleeping figures, scratches himself, and regrets that he has come home so early.⁠ ⁠…

“I have not earned enough to pay for the oats, even,” he thinks. “That’s why I am so miserable. A man who knows how to do his work,⁠ ⁠… who has had enough to eat, and whose horse has had enough to eat, is always at ease.⁠ ⁠…”

In one of the corners a young cabman gets up, clears his throat sleepily, and makes for the water-bucket.

“Want a drink?” Iona asks him.

“Seems so.”

“May it do you good.⁠ ⁠… But my son is dead, mate.⁠ ⁠… Do you hear? This week in the hospital.⁠ ⁠… It’s a queer business.⁠ ⁠…”

Iona looks to see the effect produced by his words, but he sees nothing. The young man has covered his head over and is already asleep. The old man sighs and scratches himself.⁠ ⁠… Just as the young man had been thirsty for water, he thirsts for speech. His son will soon have been dead a week, and he has not really talked to anybody yet.⁠ ⁠… He wants to talk of it properly, with deliberation.⁠ ⁠… He wants to tell how his son was taken ill, how he suffered, what he said before he died, how he died.⁠ ⁠… He wants to describe the funeral, and how he went to the hospital to get his son’s clothes. He still has his daughter Anisya in the country.⁠ ⁠… And he wants to talk about her too.⁠ ⁠… Yes, he has plenty to talk about now. His listener ought to sigh and exclaim and lament.⁠ ⁠… It would be even better to talk to women. Though they are silly creatures, they blubber at the first word.

“Let’s go out and have a look at the mare,” Iona thinks. “There is always time for sleep.⁠ ⁠… You’ll have sleep enough, no fear.⁠ ⁠…”

He puts on his coat and goes into the stables where his mare is standing. He thinks about oats, about hay, about the weather.⁠ ⁠… He cannot think about his son when he is alone.⁠ ⁠… To talk about him with someone is possible, but to think of him and picture him is insufferable anguish.⁠ ⁠…

“Are you munching?” Iona asks his mare, seeing her shining eyes. “There, munch away, munch away.⁠ ⁠… Since we have not earned enough for oats, we will eat hay.⁠ ⁠… Yes,⁠ ⁠… I have grown too old to drive.⁠ ⁠… My son ought to be driving, not I.⁠ ⁠… He was a real cabman.⁠ ⁠… He ought to have lived.⁠ ⁠…”

Iona is silent for a while, and then he goes on:

“That’s how it is, old girl.⁠ ⁠… Kuzma Ionitch is gone.⁠ ⁠… He said goodbye to me.⁠ ⁠… He went and died for no reason.⁠ ⁠… Now, suppose you had a little colt, and you were own mother to that little colt.⁠ ⁠… And all at once that same little colt went and died.⁠ ⁠… You’d be sorry, wouldn’t you?⁠ ⁠…”

The little mare munches, listens, and breathes on her master’s hands. Iona is carried away and tells her all about it.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story begins in the twilight of a winter evening. Wet snow falls on the street lamps and settles on the roofs, on the backs of horses, and on the cab driver, Iona Potapov, who sits so still on his sledge that he is white like a ghost. His little mare is just as white and motionless. He has had no fare since before dinner. Then an officer climbs in and asks to be driven to the Vyborg district. As they go, Iona tries to speak. All he can say is that his son died this week. The officer asks idly what he died of, then tells him to hurry. Later, three young men, two tall and one short and hunchbacked, take his sledge, joking and swearing and arguing about a drinking party. Iona tries again to speak, and again he is mocked and brushed aside. They get out. Finally, in the stable yard late at night, a young driver wakes briefly to ask for water, and Iona tries once more. The younger man turns over and goes back to sleep. In the end, Iona goes out to the stable and tells the whole story to his mare.

On the surface, nothing happens. That is the point. The story is built around a series of failed conversations, each one a small refusal. The officer's brief question is a reflex; the young men are too busy with their own talk; the porter and the younger driver are too tired or indifferent. Each person is not cruel, exactly. They are simply absorbed in themselves, as most of us are most of the time, and they cannot make room for a stranger's grief. Chekhov shows how loneliness is not the absence of people, since Iona is surrounded by a roaring city, but the absence of anyone who will listen.

The original title in Russian, usually translated as Misery, is also rendered as Grief or Anguish, and the story also carries the phrase To Whom Shall I Tell My Grief? as a kind of subtitle That question is the story's whole subject. There is an echo here of a religious tradition, in which sorrow is brought to God, and the final scene has a quiet irony: the only one who listens is an animal.

Notice the details. The mare is compared to a gingerbread horse, a cheap toy, so that Iona and his animal seem like discarded figures in the city's lights. Notice also how Iona's attempts to talk grow more urgent as the story proceeds. The first is a sentence to an officer, the last a whole outpouring. Notice, too, how the story avoids describing the son in much detail. We know he was a cabman, that he died of fever after three days in the hospital, and that Iona thinks he ought to have lived. Chekhov lets the reader supply the rest. The story ends with Iona carried away, telling the mare everything while she munches and breathes on his hands. The tenderness of that last gesture is the only comfort the story allows, and it is both beautiful and almost unbearable.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The most impressive thing about Misery is its economy. In a very short space, Chekhov builds a complete emotional arc. The structure is a series of attempts, each slightly longer and more desperate than the last, with the climax coming not in a dramatic event but in a release. The repetition has the feel of a musical piece, with variations on a single theme, and the escalating rhythm makes the reader want Iona to succeed, which makes each rebuff hurt more. By the end, the reader is almost ready to be the listener that Iona needs, which is exactly what Chekhov wants.

His restraint is another strength. Chekhov never tells us that Iona's heart is breaking, and he never has Iona deliver a speech about the injustice of the world. The narrator keeps a calm, almost clinical distance, noting the snow, the lamp light and the slowness of the horse. This coolness does not weaken the feeling; it concentrates it. The famous opening image, in which a man is so bent under his sorrow that a snowdrift falling on him would make no difference, communicates the whole state of mind in a single picture. The cold detail of the officer shutting his eyes to avoid listening is another example of fact doing the work of emotion.

The story is also well observed in social terms. The sledge-drivers, the porters and the passengers are drawn with convincing humor and without condescension. The young men's bickering, the hunchback's cracked voice and the rude comments about Iona's cap make a lively, noisy contrast with the old man's silence. Chekhov also gives Iona small dignities, such as his wry laugh at the abuse and his steady care for the horse.

There are weaknesses, and they come mostly from the story's youth. The ending is potentially sentimental. A grieving man confiding in a faithful animal is a familiar device and could easily become mawkish. Chekhov largely avoids this by keeping Iona's speech plain and halting, and by letting the mare munch, but a stricter reader might feel that the last paragraph pushes slightly too hard, as though Chekhov wants to be sure we have felt it. The story is also quite schematic. The passengers are types, each designed to be a different kind of indifference. At times the structure feels a little too orderly, a little like a demonstration of a thesis.

And some of the shorthand about city life, with the monstrous lights and the horse torn from the plough, belongs to an older, more conventional style of social sympathy. It is not as subtle as the mature Chekhov, who would learn to leave even such comments out.

Even so, Misery is remarkable for a writer of his age. It shows what he could do with a few hundred words and a simple situation. If it is not as layered as The Lady with the Dog, it has an immediacy and a purity that the later stories only sometimes match. Its flaws are the flaws of a young master finding his voice, and its virtues are exactly the virtues that would define his mature art.

III. The Impact It Had

Misery was published in a Saint Petersburg newspaper in January 1886, when Chekhov was still known mainly as a humorist who wrote under a comic pseudonym for small magazines. He had begun to find a wider audience, and in that same year the established writer Dmitry Grigorovich wrote to urge him to take his talent seriously and to stop dashing off work for quick pay. Misery belongs to the group of stories from this time that showed a more serious side. It is often cited as one of the early signs that Chekhov was becoming the writer we know, one who could make a whole world from a slight incident.

The story's influence on later fiction has been wide. It is one of the clearest early examples of a type of story that Chekhov helped to define, in which the plot is minimal, the emotion is carried by small details and the ending is a quiet note rather than a twist. Writers of the modern short story, including Katherine Mansfield, James Joyce and Raymond Carver, are in debt to this method. The Irish writer Frank O'Connor, in his well-known study of the short story, argued that the form often gives voice to what he called submerged populations, people on the margins of society, and Chekhov's cab driver is a perfect example.

Misery also belongs to a Russian tradition of sympathy for the small man, descending from Pushkin's The Station Master and Gogol's The Overcoat. In The Overcoat, a poor clerk is robbed of his coat, ignored by the authorities and dies. Chekhov takes the same compassion and strips away the supernatural coda and the broad comedy, leaving only the truth of loneliness. This continuity of Russian humane realism is a favorite topic in courses on the literature.

In popular and educational life, the story has been a mainstay. It is widely anthologized, taught in schools as an introduction to Chekhov because of its brevity, and used in classes on point of view and on showing rather than telling. It is also a frequent choice for translators, with versions by Constance Garnett and many later hands. The image of the old man talking to his horse has become an emblem of unspoken grief.

Another form of impact is the way it has been used outside literature. Its central image, of a person who cannot find a listener, is often cited in discussions of loneliness, bereavement and the importance of simply listening. Though this reception is informal rather than scholarly, it shows how the story's meaning spills beyond its time and place.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved Misery, above all, for its emotional truth. Almost everyone has had the experience of needing to tell someone about a loss and sensing that the other person has no room to hear it. The officer's mild, absent-minded question about the cause of death, followed by an instant return to his own concerns, is painfully familiar. We have all been the officer at one time or another, and we have all been Iona. The story makes both recognitions possible, and readers often say they finish it wanting to be a better listener.

They also love the horse. There is something deeply touching in the old man's turning to the one creature that will stay with him, and in the small, solemn domestic details, such as the hay in place of oats and the mare's warm breath on his hands. The animal is neither a symbol nor a joke. She is simply present, and her presence is what Iona needs. Many readers find the last lines among the most moving in the whole of Chekhov, precisely because they refuse to tell us that it is enough.

The story's quietness is another source of admiration. It does not shout. It lets the snow fall and the lamps burn and the old man sit. In a literary culture that often prized dramatic effects, this modest approach felt fresh and true. Later writers have praised it for what it leaves out, for example the mother, the wife or the funeral, and for the dignity it grants a character who in life would be invisible. A cab driver in a threadbare cap, treated with such attention, became for many readers a proof that no life is too small for literature.

The humor, perhaps surprisingly, is something readers value too. The young men's banter, the hunchback's cracked voice and the clumsy argument over who will stand give the middle of the story a lively comic energy. This is the young Chekhov, the humorist, at work, and the contrast with Iona's silence heightens the sadness. The cruelty is never melodramatic. It is just the ordinary cruelty of busy, careless people, which is why it feels real.

Finally, readers like how short it is. It can be read in minutes, yet it stays in the mind for years. Many people remember the first time they read it. Teachers say students respond to it right away, even those who find older literature remote, because the situation, a person who wants to be heard, is immediate. That directness is rare and valuable.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Little detailed contemporary criticism of Misery has been recorded in English-language sources, and it is difficult to point to specific reviews of the 1886 publication. What can be said is that Chekhov's early work in general met a mixed reception in Russia. Critics of the time, especially those who expected literature to carry a clear social or political message, accused him of lacking convictions and of merely recording moods. A story like Misery, which shows suffering but proposes no cure and blames no one, was exactly the kind of work these critics found inadequate. The suggestion was that Chekhov's compassion was passive. A later generation of Soviet critics sometimes praised him as a witness to social injustice, while others found him insufficiently engaged.

The most common modern objection is sentimentality. Some readers feel that the device of a lonely man confiding in an animal is a stock sentimental image, and that the story tugs at the heartstrings more openly than the mature Chekhov would have done. The deliberate piling-up of rejections, officer, young men, porter, younger driver, can feel engineered, a bit like a machine for producing pathos. The tone of the opening, with its observations about the city's monstrous lights and the peasant torn from the plough, can seem a little too readily sympathetic. It is fair to say that Misery lacks the ambiguity of the later pieces.

Another line of criticism concerns its simplicity. Because the story has no twist and little psychological complexity, some readers find it slight, a sketch rather than a fully developed tale. Iona is a figure of pure suffering, with little else to him, and some critics have suggested that he is more an emblem than a person. The passengers, likewise, are types of indifference rather than rounded characters. This is a fair point, and it is why the story is often seen as a prelude to the greater stories.

There is also the question of whether the story's view of human indifference is too bleak or too accusatory. By presenting everyone but the horse as callous, Chekhov may seem to condemn his society wholesale, yet the story also acknowledges that these people are tired, cold and caught up in their own lives. Some readers feel this balance tips toward a slightly self-righteous pity. Others argue that the story's gentleness toward everyone, even the cruel hunchback, is what saves it.

None of these objections has diminished the story's standing. They mark it as an early work, strong in feeling and economy, and not yet the full subtlety of the later masterpieces.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

In the history of literature, Misery occupies the early stretch of Chekhov's career, before the great stories of the 1890s and the plays. It belongs to the broader movement of Russian realism, which sought to portray ordinary life truthfully, and to the more specific tradition of the humane story about the poor and powerless. Its ancestors include Pushkin's The Station Master, a tale of a lowly postmaster whose daughter is taken from him, and Gogol's The Overcoat, about a clerk whose life is destroyed by the loss of a garment. The Russian habit of looking at the small man with pity runs through Dostoevsky's Poor Folk and through Turgenev's sketches. Chekhov is distinctive in the lightness of his touch. He has none of Gogol's fantasy and none of Dostoevsky's feverish intensity; he offers instead a patient, clear-eyed attention.

Looking forward, the story is a step toward the modern short story. The idea that a story might consist of nothing more than a state of feeling, dramatized through a few encounters, and end without resolution, became a core principle of the form. It points toward Mansfield's quiet stories, Joyce's epiphanies, Hemingway's understatement and the contemporary tradition of Carver, Munro and William Trevor. Even outside fiction, its approach to showing suffering without commentary has parallels in documentary photography and film.

Comparison with Tolstoy is instructive. Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich also considers the loneliness of someone facing death surrounded by uncomprehending people. But Tolstoy's story is long, philosophical and intensely moral, while Chekhov's is tiny, unsermonizing and rooted in an ordinary working man. Chekhov himself, in his story Vanka from the same year, portrays a boy's letter to his grandfather that will never arrive, another instance of an unheard voice. These pieces share a central image of communication failing, and they rank among the most affecting expressions of this theme in the literature.

There is also a link to modern drama. The way characters in Chekhov's plays talk past one another, each locked in private concerns, is already visible here in miniature, and the hunger to be heard is a driving force in The Three Sisters and Uncle Vanya. In that sense, Misery is a seed of his theatre.

Why read it today? Because its subject is as pressing as ever. In a world of constant noise and instant messages, loneliness is still the experience of not being heard. The story takes only a few minutes, and it asks nothing of us except attention, which is exactly what Iona needs and cannot find. Reading it is a small practice of the thing it recommends. When we finish it, we might want to turn to the next person and say, go on, I am listening.
