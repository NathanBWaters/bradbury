Nightly Essay • Philosophy & Epistemology

Appearance and Reality

Bertrand Russell (1912) • 16 min read

Is there any knowledge in the world which is so certain that no reasonable man could doubt it?

Key Context & Why It Matters

This is the opening chapter of The Problems of Philosophy, a small book Bertrand Russell wrote in 1911 and published in 1912 as a gentle introduction for ordinary readers. Russell was then one of the sharpest logicians alive, deep in his enormous work Principia Mathematica with Alfred North Whitehead, yet here he chose to speak plainly about the oldest puzzles there are. The book became one of the most widely read philosophy primers ever written, and this chapter is the reason why. It starts with something as dull as a table, and by the end you are no longer sure the table is what you thought it was. Russell asks a simple question: is there any knowledge so certain that no reasonable person could doubt it? His answer begins with the difference between how things appear to us and how they might really be. As you listen, notice how he never lectures. He just keeps asking you to look more closely at what you thought you already knew.

Is there any knowledge in the world which is so certain that no reasonable man could doubt it? This question, which at first sight might not seem difficult, is really one of the most difficult that can be asked. When we have realized the obstacles in the way of a straightforward and confident answer, we shall be well launched on the study of philosophy⁠—for philosophy is merely the attempt to answer such ultimate questions, not carelessly and dogmatically, as we do in ordinary life and even in the sciences, but critically, after exploring all that makes such questions puzzling, and after realizing all the vagueness and confusion that underlie our ordinary ideas.

In daily life, we assume as certain many things which, on a closer scrutiny, are found to be so full of apparent contradictions that only a great amount of thought enables us to know what it is that we really may believe. In the search for certainty, it is natural to begin with our present experiences, and in some sense, no doubt, knowledge is to be derived from them. But any statement as to what it is that our immediate experiences make us know is very likely to be wrong. It seems to me that I am now sitting in a chair, at a table of a certain shape, on which I see sheets of paper with writing or print. By turning my head I see out of the window buildings and clouds and the sun. I believe that the sun is about ninety-three million miles from the earth; that it is a hot globe many times bigger than the earth; that, owing to the earth’s rotation, it rises every morning, and will continue to do so for an indefinite time in the future. I believe that, if any other normal person comes into my room, he will see the same chairs and tables and books and papers as I see, and that the table which I see is the same as the table which I feel pressing against my arm. All this seems to be so evident as to be hardly worth stating, except in answer to a man who doubts whether I know anything. Yet all this may be reasonably doubted, and all of it requires much careful discussion before we can be sure that we have stated it in a form that is wholly true.

To make our difficulties plain, let us concentrate attention on the table. To the eye it is oblong, brown and shiny, to the touch it is smooth and cool and hard; when I tap it, it gives out a wooden sound. Anyone else who sees and feels and hears the table will agree with this description, so that it might seem as if no difficulty would arise; but as soon as we try to be more precise our troubles begin. Although I believe that the table is “really” of the same colour all over, the parts that reflect the light look much brighter than the other parts, and some parts look white because of reflected light. I know that, if I move, the parts that reflect the light will be different, so that the apparent distribution of colours on the table will change. It follows that if several people are looking at the table at the same moment, no two of them will see exactly the same distribution of colours, because no two can see it from exactly the same point of view, and any change in the point of view makes some change in the way the light is reflected.

For most practical purposes these differences are unimportant, but to the painter they are all-important: the painter has to unlearn the habit of thinking that things seem to have the colour which common sense says they “really” have, and to learn the habit of seeing things as they appear. Here we have already the beginning of one of the distinctions that cause most trouble in philosophy⁠—the distinction between “appearance” and “reality,” between what things seem to be and what they are. The painter wants to know what things seem to be, the practical man and the philosopher want to know what they are; but the philosopher’s wish to know this is stronger than the practical man’s, and is more troubled by knowledge as to the difficulties of answering the question.

To return to the table. It is evident from what we have found, that there is no colour which preeminently appears to be the colour of the table, or even of any one particular part of the table⁠—it appears to be of different colours from different points of view, and there is no reason for regarding some of these as more really its colour than others. And we know that even from a given point of view the colour will seem different by artificial light, or to a colour-blind man, or to a man wearing blue spectacles, while in the dark there will be no colour at all, though to touch and hearing the table will be unchanged. This colour is not something which is inherent in the table, but something depending upon the table and the spectator and the way the light falls on the table. When, in ordinary life, we speak of the colour of the table, we only mean the sort of colour which it will seem to have to a normal spectator from an ordinary point of view under usual conditions of light. But the other colours which appear under other conditions have just as good a right to be considered real; and therefore, to avoid favouritism, we are compelled to deny that, in itself, the table has any one particular colour.

The same thing applies to the texture. With the naked eye one can see the grain, but otherwise the table looks smooth and even. If we looked at it through a microscope, we should see roughnesses and hills and valleys, and all sorts of differences that are imperceptible to the naked eye. Which of these is the “real” table? We are naturally tempted to say that what we see through the microscope is more real, but that in turn would be changed by a still more powerful microscope. If, then, we cannot trust what we see with the naked eye, why should we trust what we see through a microscope? Thus, again, the confidence in our senses with which we began deserts us.

The shape of the table is no better. We are all in the habit of judging as to the “real” shapes of things, and we do this so unreflectingly that we come to think we actually see the real shapes. But, in fact, as we all have to learn if we try to draw, a given thing looks different in shape from every different point of view. If our table is “really” rectangular, it will look, from almost all points of view, as if it had two acute angles and two obtuse angles. If opposite sides are parallel, they will look as if they converged to a point away from the spectator; if they are of equal length, they will look as if the nearer side were longer. All these things are not commonly noticed in looking at a table, because experience has taught us to construct the “real” shape from the apparent shape, and the “real” shape is what interests us as practical men. But the “real” shape is not what we see; it is something inferred from what we see. And what we see is constantly changing in shape as we move about the room; so that here again the senses seem not to give us the truth about the table itself, but only about the appearance of the table.

Similar difficulties arise when we consider the sense of touch. It is true that the table always gives us a sensation of hardness, and we feel that it resists pressure. But the sensation we obtain depends upon how hard we press the table and also upon what part of the body we press with; thus the various sensations due to various pressures or various parts of the body cannot be supposed to reveal directly any definite property of the table, but at most to be signs of some property which perhaps causes all the sensations, but is not actually apparent in any of them. And the same applies still more obviously to the sounds which can be elicited by rapping the table.

Thus it becomes evident that the real table, if there is one, is not the same as what we immediately experience by sight or touch or hearing. The real table, if there is one, is not immediately known to us at all, but must be an inference from what is immediately known. Hence, two very difficult questions at once arise; namely, (1) Is there a real table at all? (2) If so, what sort of object can it be?

It will help us in considering these questions to have a few simple terms of which the meaning is definite and clear. Let us give the name of “sense-data” to the things that are immediately known in sensation: such things as colours, sounds, smells, hardnesses, roughnesses, and so on. We shall give the name “sensation” to the experience of being immediately aware of these things. Thus, whenever we see a colour, we have a sensation of the colour, but the colour itself is a sense-datum, not a sensation. The colour is that of which we are immediately aware, and the awareness itself is the sensation. It is plain that if we are to know anything about the table, it must be by means of the sense-data⁠—brown colour, oblong shape, smoothness, etc.⁠—which we associate with the table; but, for the reasons which have been given, we cannot say that the table is the sense-data, or even that the sense-data are directly properties of the table. Thus a problem arises as to the relation of the sense-data to the real table, supposing there is such a thing.

The real table, if it exists, we will call a “physical object.” Thus we have to consider the relation of sense-data to physical objects. The collection of all physical objects is called “matter.” Thus our two questions may be restated as follows:

Is there any such thing as matter?

If so, what is its nature?

The philosopher who first brought prominently forward the reasons for regarding the immediate objects of our senses as not existing independently of us was Bishop Berkeley (1685⁠–⁠1753). His Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous, in Opposition to Sceptics and Atheists, undertake to prove that there is no such thing as matter at all, and that the world consists of nothing but minds and their ideas. Hylas has hitherto believed in matter, but he is no match for Philonous, who mercilessly drives him into contradictions and paradoxes, and makes his own denial of matter seem, in the end, as if it were almost common sense. The arguments employed are of very different value: some are important and sound, others are confused or quibbling. But Berkeley retains the merit of having shown that the existence of matter is capable of being denied without absurdity, and that if there are any things that exist independently of us they cannot be the immediate objects of our sensations.

There are two different questions involved when we ask whether matter exists, and it is important to keep them clear. We commonly mean by “matter” something which is opposed to “mind,” something which we think of as occupying space and as radically incapable of any sort of thought or consciousness. It is chiefly in this sense that Berkeley denies matter; that is to say, he does not deny that the sense-data which we commonly take as signs of the existence of the table are really signs of the existence of something independent of us, but he does deny that this something is non-mental, that it is neither mind nor ideas entertained by some mind. He admits that there must be something which continues to exist when we go out of the room or shut our eyes, and that what we call seeing the table does really give us reason for believing in something which persists even when we are not seeing it. But he thinks that this something cannot be radically different in nature from what we see, and cannot be independent of seeing altogether, though it must be independent of our seeing. He is thus led to regard the “real” table as an idea in the mind of God. Such an idea has the required permanence and independence of ourselves, without being⁠—as matter would otherwise be⁠—something quite unknowable, in the sense that we can only infer it, and can never be directly and immediately aware of it.

Other philosophers since Berkeley have also held that, although the table does not depend for its existence upon being seen by me, it does depend upon being seen (or otherwise apprehended in sensation) by some mind⁠—not necessarily the mind of God, but more often the whole collective mind of the universe. This they hold, as Berkeley does, chiefly because they think there can be nothing real⁠—or at any rate nothing known to be real except minds and their thoughts and feelings. We might state the argument by which they support their view in some such way as this: “Whatever can be thought of is an idea in the mind of the person thinking of it; therefore nothing can be thought of except ideas in minds; therefore anything else is inconceivable, and what is inconceivable cannot exist.”

Such an argument, in my opinion, is fallacious; and of course those who advance it do not put it so shortly or so crudely. But whether valid or not, the argument has been very widely advanced in one form or another; and very many philosophers, perhaps a majority, have held that there is nothing real except minds and their ideas. Such philosophers are called “idealists.” When they come to explaining matter, they either say, like Berkeley, that matter is really nothing but a collection of ideas, or they say, like Leibniz (1646⁠–⁠1716), that what appears as matter is really a collection of more or less rudimentary minds.

But these philosophers, though they deny matter as opposed to mind, nevertheless, in another sense, admit matter. It will be remembered that we asked two questions; namely, (1) Is there a real table at all? (2) If so, what sort of object can it be? Now both Berkeley and Leibniz admit that there is a real table, but Berkeley says it is certain ideas in the mind of God, and Leibniz says it is a colony of souls. Thus both of them answer our first question in the affirmative, and only diverge from the views of ordinary mortals in their answer to our second question. In fact, almost all philosophers seem to be agreed that there is a real table: they almost all agree that, however much our sense-data⁠—colour, shape, smoothness, etc.⁠—may depend upon us, yet their occurrence is a sign of something existing independently of us, something differing, perhaps, completely from our sense-data, and yet to be regarded as causing those sense-data whenever we are in a suitable relation to the real table.

Now obviously this point in which the philosophers are agreed⁠—the view that there is a real table, whatever its nature may be⁠—is vitally important, and it will be worth while to consider what reasons there are for accepting this view before we go on to the further question as to the nature of the real table. Our next chapter, therefore, will be concerned with the reasons for supposing that there is a real table at all.

Before we go farther it will be well to consider for a moment what it is that we have discovered so far. It has appeared that, if we take any common object of the sort that is supposed to be known by the senses, what the senses immediately tell us is not the truth about the object as it is apart from us, but only the truth about certain sense-data which, so far as we can see, depend upon the relations between us and the object. Thus what we directly see and feel is merely “appearance,” which we believe to be a sign of some “reality” behind. But if the reality is not what appears, have we any means of knowing whether there is any reality at all? And if so, have we any means of finding out what it is like?

Such questions are bewildering, and it is difficult to know that even the strangest hypotheses may not be true. Thus our familiar table, which has roused but the slightest thoughts in us hitherto, has become a problem full of surprising possibilities. The one thing we know about it is that it is not what it seems. Beyond this modest result, so far, we have the most complete liberty of conjecture. Leibniz tells us it is a community of souls; Berkeley tells us it is an idea in the mind of God; sober science, scarcely less wonderful, tells us it is a vast collection of electric charges in violent motion.

Among these surprising possibilities, doubt suggests that perhaps there is no table at all. Philosophy, if it cannot answer so many questions as we could wish, has at least the power of asking questions which increase the interest of the world, and show the strangeness and wonder lying just below the surface even in the commonest things of daily life.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Russell begins with a question that sounds easy and turns out to be brutal: is there anything we know so surely that no reasonable person could doubt it? He says that once you feel how hard this question is, you are already doing philosophy. For him philosophy is not a body of secret doctrines. It is the attempt to answer the biggest questions carefully, after looking at everything that makes them puzzling, instead of carelessly, as we do in daily life.

He then picks the humblest possible example. He is sitting in a chair at a table. He believes the sun is about ninety-three million miles away, that other people entering the room would see the same table, and that the table he sees is the one he feels pressing on his arm. All of this seems too obvious to say. Then he starts looking at the table. Where the light falls, it looks bright and white. From another angle, parts of it look dark. If you and I stand in different places, we see different colours and different shapes. A painter knows this and trains herself to draw what she actually sees, not what she knows. Touch tells a similar story. The table feels hard, but press harder and you feel only more pressure, and under a microscope the smooth grain is rough and full of hills and valleys. So what is the real colour, texture and shape of the table? Russell's answer is that nobody can say, because what we are directly aware of keeps changing with our position, the lighting and our own bodies.

This is where he introduces his key term, sense-data. These are the things we are immediately aware of in sensation, such as a patch of colour, a hardness, a sound. The experience of seeing is the sensation, but the colour itself is the sense-datum. And the key insight is that the sense-datum is not the table. It depends on us as much as on the table. So the table, whatever it is, is something we reach only by inference, a sign of something behind the appearance.

That leaves a dizzying set of possibilities, and the chapter ends by lining them up. Leibniz said the table is a community of souls. Berkeley said it is an idea in the mind of God. Science says it is a vast collection of electric charges in violent motion. Russell is not endorsing any of these. His point is that the one thing we know about our familiar table is that it is not what it seems, and beyond that we have, in his words, the most complete liberty of conjecture.

The layer a first-time reader may miss is how carefully he distinguishes doubt from denial. He is not saying there is no table. He is saying the table we see is not automatically the table that exists, and that the gap between appearance and reality is where all the interesting thinking begins. The last lines turn this into a defence of the whole subject: philosophy may not answer many questions, but it can show the strangeness and wonder lying just below the surface of the commonest things.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of teaching, this chapter is close to perfect, and it is worth saying exactly why. The central move is the choice of the table. Philosophers before Russell had used wax, dreams, and demons. A table is better because it is boring. The reader cannot say, I never worried about wax. Everyone has a table, and the argument arrives at the reader's own elbow. Russell's prose does the rest. Sentences are short, examples are concrete, there is no show of learning, and every term, like sense-datum, is defined the moment it appears. He also has the confidence to confess that his conclusions are modest. A weaker writer would have announced a system. Russell announces a puzzle and lets it do the work.

The structure is also elegant. He starts from what seems certain, shows that it is not, and ends on three rival pictures of the table that are each stranger than the last. That ending is rhetorical craft of a high order. You are left unsettled but not despairing, curious rather than defeated.

But the chapter has real weaknesses, and a fair assessment has to name them. The biggest is that the whole argument leans on the notion of sense-data, which he treats as obvious. In fact it is a substantial and contestable theory. Russell slides from the true observation that things look different from different angles to the stronger claim that what we are directly aware of is a private inner object, not the table itself. Those are not the same claim. A table that looks different from different angles may simply be a table seen from different angles. The step from perspective to a veil between us and the world is the very step a sceptical reader should refuse, and Russell makes it very smoothly.

A second weakness is the way he moves from the fact that the colour varies to the claim that there is no real colour. There are perfectly sensible ways of saying the table is brown under normal conditions in daylight, and Russell dismisses these a little quickly as favouring one set of circumstances without a reason. That is arguable, not decisive.

Third, the scientific picture is used as a kind of trump card against ordinary perception, as if talk of electric charges showed that the table of everyday life is somehow less real. Later philosophers, and many scientists, would say that the solid wooden table and the swarm of particles are two descriptions of one object at different scales, not rivals.

Finally, the chapter is very much a product of its moment. The physics is of 1911, the vocabulary of sensation is early Cambridge, and the sheer comfort with a split between mind and world feels dated. Still, being dated is not the same as being wrong, and these flaws are exactly what make it a good starting point for argument.

III. The Impact It Had

It is hard to measure the impact of one chapter, but this one belongs to a book that did enormous work. The Problems of Philosophy came out in 1912 in the Home University Library, a series of short, cheap volumes meant for general readers. It has hardly been out of print since. For generations of English-speaking students it has been the first philosophy book they ever opened, and the table passage is often the first thing they remember. Teachers still assign it, and the table of sense-data has become a kind of shared opening scene for introductory courses.

For Russell himself, the book marked a turn from technical logic toward public philosophy. He had spent years on Principia Mathematica, written with Whitehead and published between 1910 and 1913, which tried to derive mathematics from logic. The little book on philosophy showed he could write for anyone. That gift would carry through his famous History of Western Philosophy, his popular essays, and eventually the Nobel Prize in Literature he received in 1950. The clear, dry, witty voice you hear in this chapter is the voice that made him a public figure.

Intellectually, the chapter helped set the terms of a long debate in twentieth-century Anglo-American philosophy. Sense-data became the standard vocabulary for discussing perception. G. E. Moore, C. D. Broad, H. H. Price and A. J. Ayer all wrote about them, sometimes defending and sometimes refining the idea. Russell himself pushed it further in his 1914 book Our Knowledge of the External World, where he tried to treat physical objects as logical constructions out of sense-data. The question he raises here, how perception connects us to a world beyond it, is now called the problem of perception, and it still organises a whole branch of the field.

The impact also runs backwards through a clear picture of Berkeley. Russell's brief summary of the three dialogues between Hylas and Philonous introduced many readers to the idea that matter might not exist, and presented it as a serious position instead of a joke.

The book has also seeped into general culture. Whenever someone asks whether your red is my red, or whether a tree falling in an empty forest makes a sound, they are standing on ground Russell helped make familiar. And the last paragraph, the line about the wonder lying just below the surface, has been quoted so often in defence of studying philosophy that it has almost become a motto. In that sense the chapter did what its author most wanted: it made doubt feel like a pleasure.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved most is the sense of being addressed as an intelligent adult. Russell does not talk down, and he does not show off. He gives you a table, a few observations, and the freedom to be puzzled, and the effect is a little like being let into a secret. Many people who later became philosophers say that this opening was the moment they first felt the vertigo of the subject, the moment the ordinary world went slightly strange. That feeling of the familiar turning unfamiliar is a rare thing for an argument to produce.

Readers also admire the plainness of the writing. There is hardly a sentence in the chapter that needs rereading. The paragraphs are built on observations anyone can check by moving their head or pressing a thumb into wood, so the reader is not asked to take anything on trust. Russell was famous for prose with an Enlightenment clarity, a dry wit, and a refusal of jargon, and this chapter is a good place to see why admirers praised his style as much as his logic. The final paragraph, with the Leibniz, Berkeley and science trio, is especially often cited. Russell sets out three astonishing pictures in a few lines and lets the reader feel their absurdity and their seriousness at once.

There is also the pleasure of honesty. Russell admits again and again that he cannot settle the matter yet. He does not pretend. For readers used to textbooks that bury the difficulty, this willingness to say, here is a real problem and I do not know the answer, is refreshing and, oddly, trust-building.

Another source of affection is the way the chapter turns an apparently useless activity into something worth the effort. Many people feel suspicious of philosophy as idle word-play. Russell answers that suspicion not by promising practical results but by arguing that the subject enlarges the world. A mind that has seriously asked whether the table exists, he suggests, will never look at the ordinary again quite as before. That promise, that the reward of thinking is wonder, speaks to a lot of readers, including those who never go on to read any more philosophy.

Lastly, there is the sheer usefulness of the vocabulary. Terms like appearance and reality, sense-datum and physical object give a beginner handles for thoughts that were previously foggy. Readers feel smarter afterwards, and that is a real gift from a book this short.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most serious criticism is aimed at the idea of sense-data itself. In the middle of the twentieth century, the Oxford philosopher J. L. Austin attacked the whole picture in lectures later published as Sense and Sensibilia. Austin argued that philosophers had been bewitched by a few misleading examples, such as a stick that looks bent in water, and had drawn from them a sweeping conclusion that we never really see material things. Ordinary language, he said, is more subtle: we do not say we are deceived every time the light changes. Many later thinkers agreed that the argument from variation, which Russell uses in this chapter, proves far less than it seems to.

A related complaint comes from direct realists, who hold that in normal perception we see the table itself, not a mental stand-in. They say Russell invents a gap between us and the world and then worries about how to cross it. Phenomenologists such as Husserl and Merleau-Ponty, working in another tradition, also objected that Russell starts from isolated, passive snapshots of experience, when real perceiving is an active, bodily engagement with a world that is already there. On this view the problem of how to get from inner data to outer objects is an artefact of a bad starting point.

Some critics have pointed to the way Russell talks about common sense. He treats the everyday assumptions of the first paragraph as naive and in need of repair, which can seem condescending. Defenders of common sense, following G. E. Moore, held that we are more certain of everyday facts, like having hands, than we could ever be of any philosophical premise, so any argument that leads to doubt about them should be suspected, not the facts.

Others complain about the tone of the popular approach. Because the book is so readable, some argue it makes hard questions look easy, giving beginners the impression that sense-data are a settled fact and that the central issue is the choice between three or four neat theories. Professional philosophers sometimes say that the book is brilliant but compresses a lot of controversy into a few confident turns.

There is also a more historical note. Russell's own views changed, and within a few years he moved away from some of the picture presented here. He later became sceptical of sense-data as private mental items and drifted toward a view in which mind and matter are made of the same neutral stuff. So even the author did not regard this chapter as the last word. Most of the objections, in other words, are not that the chapter is careless, but that its tidy starting assumptions carry heavier loads than they admit.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter belongs to the old tradition of epistemology, the study of knowledge, which stretches back through Descartes, Locke, Berkeley and Hume to the ancient sceptics. The method of beginning with doubt and asking what survives is Cartesian. The idea that the immediate objects of experience are ideas or impressions rather than things is British empiricism. Russell's special talent was to take these inherited puzzles and restate them in cleaner modern language, with logic as his tool.

Its closest relatives are Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities, Berkeley's Three Dialogues, and Hume's discussions of perception. Berkeley in particular is a companion here. Russell credits him with showing that the existence of matter can be denied without absurdity, and that if there are things independent of us they cannot be the immediate objects of our senses. Where Berkeley moved from this to the claim that only minds and ideas exist, Russell stays cautious and, in the next chapter, defends the view that there is matter behind our sense-data.

Looking forward, the chapter is a founding text for the analytic tradition's treatment of perception. Sense-data theory dominated English-language philosophy for roughly half a century, and its collapse under attack from Austin, Wittgenstein, Ryle and others became one of the great stories of the discipline. Modern debates still revolve around the questions Russell sets up, even when the vocabulary has changed. Contemporary philosophers of perception talk about representationalism, naive realism and disjunctivism, but they are in effect arguing about whether and how a gap like Russell's table gap exists.

Interestingly, the chapter also anticipates themes in physics and cognitive science. The point that our experience depends on our position and our bodies is echoed in modern theories of perception as an active construction of the brain. Russell's glance at the scientific table, a mass of electric charges, anticipates the later puzzle about how the manifest world of colour and solidity relates to the scientific image of the world, a puzzle philosophers such as Wilfrid Sellars made famous.

Compare it with other popular entry points: Plato's cave offers a similar shock, that the shadows we take for reality are not the whole story, and Descartes' first Meditation uses dreaming for a similar purpose. Russell's table is the plain, daylight version of these, with no story about a cave or an evil demon.

Why read it today? Because it still works. In under three thousand words you get a real philosophical problem, a vocabulary for it, and the sense that careful thinking can be exciting. Whether or not you accept sense-data, you will understand much better what the arguments are about, and you will have enjoyed the company of a writer who plainly loved the questions.
