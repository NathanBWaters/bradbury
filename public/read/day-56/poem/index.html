Miracles

Walt Whitman (1856) • 3 min read

Why, who makes much of a miracle? Whether wading naked in the summer sea, riding a Brooklyn ferry, or watching honey-bees around the hive, to Whitman every cubic inch of space is a miracle.

Key Context & Why It Matters

First published as 'Poem of Perfect Miracles' in the 1856 second edition of Leaves of Grass and given the title 'Miracles' in the 'Autumn Rivulets' cluster in 1881, this twenty-three-line celebration answers David Hume’s and nineteenth-century theologians’ debates over supernatural miracles with radical Transcendentalist wonder. Belonging to the American Transcendentalism tradition and categorized under Transcendental & Free Verse, 'Miracles' (1856) unfolds across 23 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. Directly embodying Ralph Waldo Emerson’s call in Nature to behold the miraculous in the common, 'Miracles' anticipates the everyday epiphanies of William Carlos Williams, Mary Oliver, and Ray Bradbury’s Dandelion Wine. Through twenty-three lines divided into two movements—an exuberantly anaphoric fourteen-line catalogue ('Or whether I...') that sweeps from city streets to bees, Walt Whitman fuses formal precision with emotional immediacy, making the poem an enduring cornerstone of the poetic canon.

Why, who makes much of a miracle?

As to me I know of nothing else but miracles,

Whether I walk the streets of Manhattan,

Or dart my sight over the roofs of houses toward the sky,

Or wade with naked feet along the beach just in the edge of the water,

Or stand under trees in the woods,

Or talk by day with any one I love, or sleep in the bed at night with any one I love,

Or sit at table at dinner with the rest,

Or look at strangers opposite me riding in the car,

Or watch honey-bees busy around the hive of a summer forenoon,

Or animals feeding in the fields,

Or birds, or the wonderfulness of insects in the air,

Or the wonderfulness of the sundown, or of stars shining so quiet and bright,

Or the exquisite delicate thin curve of the new moon in spring;

These with the rest, one and all, are to me miracles,

The whole referring, yet each distinct and in its place.

To me every hour of the light and dark is a miracle,

Every cubic inch of space is a miracle,

Every square yard of the surface of the earth is spread with the same,

Every foot of the interior swarms with the same.

To me the sea is a continual miracle,

The fishes that swim—the rocks—the motion of the waves—the ships with men in them,

What stranger miracles are there?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

First published as 'Poem of Perfect Miracles' in the 1856 second edition of Leaves of Grass and given the title 'Miracles' in the 'Autumn Rivulets' cluster in 1881, this twenty-three-line celebration answers David Hume’s and nineteenth-century theologians’ debates over supernatural miracles with radical Transcendentalist wonder. Within the broader trajectory of American Transcendentalism, Walt Whitman composed 'Miracles' (1856) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Why, who makes much of a miracle?"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Walt Whitman grounded these 23 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Miracles' lies the democratization of the sacred in everyday sensory experience: rather than looking for miracles only in rare violations of natural law, Whitman declares 'I know of nothing else but miracles'—finding equal astonishment in Manhattan roofs, wading by the sea-edge, talking with someone he loves, sitting at dinner, looking at strangers on a streetcar, or watching the thin curve of the new moon in spring. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Or birds, or the wonderfulness of insects in the air,"—Walt Whitman develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Walt Whitman constructs 'Miracles' using twenty-three lines divided into two movements—an exuberantly anaphoric fourteen-line catalogue ('Or whether I...') that sweeps from city streets to bees, sunset, stars, and crescent moon, followed by a nine-line philosophical summation asking 'What stranger miracles are there?'. Spanning 23 lines across 3 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Walt Whitman balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Why, who makes much of a miracle?" and "What stranger miracles are there?" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Directly embodying Ralph Waldo Emerson’s call in Nature to behold the miraculous in the common, 'Miracles' anticipates the everyday epiphanies of William Carlos Williams, Mary Oliver, and Ray Bradbury’s Dandelion Wine. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Miracles' has remained a touchstone of the Transcendental & Free Verse canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Walt Whitman’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form. From nineteenth- and twentieth-century critical debates to contemporary anthologies, Walt Whitman’s 'Miracles' retains all of its original freshness and authority.
