Miracles

Walt Whitman (1856) • 3 min read

Why, who makes much of a miracle? Whether wading naked in the summer sea, riding a Brooklyn ferry, or watching honey-bees around the hive, to Whitman every cubic inch of space is a miracle.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walt Whitman's short poem about miracles is one of the plainest and most generous things in American poetry. It first appeared in the 1856 edition of his book Leaves of Grass, the sprawling collection he kept revising for the rest of his life. Whitman was a New York printer, newspaperman and self-taught poet who believed American writing needed a new voice: loose, democratic, and unafraid of ordinary life. Most readers then expected poems to be about kings, gods, and heroes, with rhyme and a lofty tone. Whitman gave them a list of streets, bees, strangers on a streetcar, and the sea, and called every item a miracle. As you listen, notice how the poem avoids rhyme and simply piles up everyday sights, one after another. Its meaning fits in a sentence. We wait for wonders from outside the world, when the world itself, noticed with full attention, is already more astonishing than any magic trick.

Why, who makes much of a miracle?

As to me I know of nothing else but miracles,

Whether I walk the streets of Manhattan,

Or dart my sight over the roofs of houses toward the sky,

Or wade with naked feet along the beach just in the edge of the water,

Or stand under trees in the woods,

Or talk by day with any one I love, or sleep in the bed at night with any one I love,

Or sit at table at dinner with the rest,

Or look at strangers opposite me riding in the car,

Or watch honey-bees busy around the hive of a summer forenoon,

Or animals feeding in the fields,

Or birds, or the wonderfulness of insects in the air,

Or the wonderfulness of the sundown, or of stars shining so quiet and bright,

Or the exquisite delicate thin curve of the new moon in spring;

These with the rest, one and all, are to me miracles,

The whole referring, yet each distinct and in its place.

To me every hour of the light and dark is a miracle,

Every cubic inch of space is a miracle,

Every square yard of the surface of the earth is spread with the same,

Every foot of the interior swarms with the same.

To me the sea is a continual miracle,

The fishes that swim—the rocks—the motion of the waves—the ships with men in them,

What stranger miracles are there?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem begins with a shrug and a challenge. "Why, who makes much of a miracle?" Whitman asks, as if he has overheard people arguing about whether miracles still happen. His answer is that he doesn't understand the question, because he knows "of nothing else but miracles." The ordinary meaning of the word, a rare event that breaks the laws of nature, gets turned upside down. For Whitman nothing breaks the laws of nature, and everything is a wonder anyway.

Then comes the long list that makes up most of the poem. He walks the streets of Manhattan, looks up over the rooftops, wades barefoot at the edge of the sea, stands under trees. He talks with someone he loves, sleeps beside someone he loves, sits at dinner, watches strangers across from him on the streetcar. He watches honey bees, grazing animals, birds, insects, the sundown, the stars, and "the exquisite delicate thin curve of the new moon in spring." Notice the mix. Some items are grand, like stars, and some are tiny, like a bee. Some are natural and some are plainly human and urban, like the strangers on the streetcar. Whitman refuses to rank them. Nothing is promoted into a miracle by being beautiful, and nothing is demoted for being common.

The most interesting line in the first stretch is the one that says these things are "the whole referring, yet each distinct and in its place." That is Whitman's quiet philosophy in a single phrase. Everything points toward a larger whole, but nothing dissolves into it. Each thing keeps its own face. You can think of it as a rule for paying attention: see the pattern, but do not stop seeing the particular bee.

The second stretch widens the lens until it becomes almost dizzying. "Every hour of the light and dark is a miracle," he says, and then "Every cubic inch of space," then every square yard of the earth's surface, then the interior of the planet that "swarms with the same." The measurements are deliberately dull and practical, the units of a surveyor or a carpenter. He is taking the language of property and measurement and using it to say that wonder is not scarce. It is everywhere, in every unit you could care to count.

The last stanza turns to the sea, "a continual miracle," and ends on a question: "What stranger miracles are there?" Look closely at that last list: the fishes, the rocks, the waves, and then "the ships with men in them." Whitman slips human beings and their machines into the same breath as rocks and fish. Human life is not a separate category from nature. A reader might miss how quietly radical that is. The poem is not telling you to flee the city for the woods. It is telling you the streetcar and the ocean liner belong in the same catalogue of marvels as the new moon, and the closing question dares you to name something stranger than what you already live inside.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged on what it sets out to do, this poem works very well, and its method is more disciplined than it first appears. The central technique is the catalogue, a list that builds by repetition. Whitman starts nearly every line with "Or," and the effect is a kind of walking rhythm. You hear it in the ear first, which is exactly why it works so well when read aloud. The repeated "Or" gives the poem momentum without rhyme, and it quietly teaches you how to read it: each new "Or" is another thing to notice, another door opening.

The variation inside the list is where the craft shows. Whitman moves from public to private, from the city to the beach, from the sleeping bed to the dinner table, and he places the most intimate item, talking with someone you love or sleeping beside them, right in the middle of the weather and the animals. That placement is clever. It suggests love is as much a part of the physical world as the tide. And the sentence about the new moon, with its three stacked adjectives, "exquisite delicate thin," is one of the few places where he slows down for a purely visual pleasure, which makes the quick lines around it feel faster by contrast.

The second part gains force from a different trick. Instead of listing objects, Whitman repeats a structure, "Every hour... Every cubic inch... Every square yard... Every foot," and each repetition drills deeper, from time to space to the surface of the earth to its interior. It is a small lesson in how to build a crescendo out of very plain words.

Still, it is fair to say the poem has limits. It is short and, in a sense, one idea repeated. A skeptical reader might say that declaring everything a miracle risks making the word meaningless. If every cubic inch is miraculous, then "miracle" stops distinguishing anything. Whitman half knows this, and his answer is that the point is exactly to dissolve the distinction. But some readers will feel the poem asserts wonder more than it earns it. We are told the sundown is wonderful, but we are given almost no sensory detail about it. Compared with the closely seen images in Whitman's best work, the list sometimes reads like a set of labels. The poem trusts the reader to supply the experience.

There is also a certain smoothness, even complacency, in the vision. The poem has no night in it, no hunger or war. Whitman wrote it in the 1850s, in a country heading toward civil war, and the Manhattan he walks has poverty and cruelty alongside its beauty. The poem's gentle, almost childlike tone leaves those things out. This is not a fatal flaw, since it is a poem of praise, but it keeps the work lighter than his deepest poems. Its real achievement is clarity. In about two hundred forty words it states a whole attitude toward life, and the attitude survives the plainness of the telling.

III. The Impact It Had

Whitman's poem has had a quiet but lasting influence, and to understand it you have to see it as part of the larger effect of Leaves of Grass. When that book appeared in 1855, it was almost without precedent in English: long, unrhymed lines, a speaker who talked like an ordinary man instead of a bard in a robe, and subjects drawn from machine shops, ferries, and crowds. Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote Whitman a famous letter greeting him at the beginning of a great career, which Whitman then shamelessly used to publicize the next edition. This poem comes from that next edition in 1856, and it shows him settling into the voice that changed poetry.

The direct influence of this particular poem is hard to separate from the general influence of Whitman's free verse and his catalogues. Poets who came later, from Carl Sandburg to Allen Ginsberg to Pablo Neruda in Spanish, built careers on the idea that a poem could be a generous list of the world. Ginsberg's Howl and its supermarket poem owe an open debt to Whitman's sweep. In a gentler register, writers like Mary Oliver, who made attention itself into a kind of devotion, are working in a lineage that runs back through this kind of poem.

The poem has also had a second life outside literature. Its central claim, that wonder is not rare but simply under-noticed, is the kind of thing that appeals to teachers, naturalists, and scientists. Writers who explain science to the public often reach for the sense that the real universe outdoes any supernatural story, and Whitman is a favorite patron saint for that move. It is sometimes quoted in talks and essays about mindfulness, paying attention, and gratitude, though usually in fragments.

In the classroom, the poem is a staple of introductions to free verse because it is short, clear, and easy to imitate. Students are often asked to write their own "Or" list, which turns out to be a surprisingly effective way to teach rhythm and observation. It also appears in discussions of American identity, because it is a founding example of the democratic idea in poetry: that a Manhattan streetcar is as worthy a subject as a Greek temple.

It is worth being honest that this poem is not among Whitman's most famous works. It lacks the fame of Song of Myself, or O Captain, or the elegies for Lincoln. It has influenced readers more by being absorbed than by being cited. Still, its effect on writers and teachers has been real, and it has given many people a short, memorable way to say what Whitman's whole project meant. The poem's historical importance is mostly this: it is one of the clearest small samples of a revolution in how English-language poetry could sound and what it could notice.

IV. What Readers Loved

The most obvious thing readers have loved about this poem is its sheer generosity. It does not require training, and it does not hide behind difficulty. Anyone who has stood on a beach or watched bees working a flower can enter the poem at once. For many readers, that welcome is the whole appeal. It feels like being handed permission to be amazed by things you were too busy to notice.

Readers also love the voice. Whitman sounds like a man talking on a walk, and the open "Why, who makes much of a miracle?" has the sound of a real person, a little amused, a little puzzled that anyone would think otherwise. There is humor in that opening, and warmth. Whitman's ideal speaker is someone you would actually want to be walking beside, and this poem is one of the best short samples of that companionship.

A third source of affection is the way the poem treats the human and the natural as one. Many readers find it moving that the list includes "the ships with men in them" and "strangers opposite me riding in the car." Whitman does not make you choose between loving nature and loving people. In an age of cities and screens, readers have found comfort in a poem that treats an urban commute as a place where wonder can be found, not just something to escape.

Many writers have admired Whitman's whole approach for its courage, and this poem shows the courage in miniature. It takes the risk of simplicity, with no obvious cleverness and no irony to protect itself. The risk pays off for readers who are tired of being told that sophistication means gloom. They find in these lines something rare in modern writing: a sincerely cheerful poem that does not feel foolish.

The poem also appeals to people of many beliefs. Religious readers sometimes hear in it the old idea that creation itself is a revelation. Secular readers hear a celebration of the physical world that needs no god. Whitman himself was spiritual in an unorthodox way and avoided naming doctrine, which lets both groups claim the poem. That flexibility has given it a long life among people who disagree about almost everything else.

Finally, readers love how well it reads aloud. The repeated "Or," the rising lists, and the short final question give it a shape you can feel in your breath. It is the sort of poem people memorize in part, or copy into a notebook, or read at a gathering when someone wants to say something grateful but does not have the words. Its popularity is mostly that of a friendly tool: it does a small, useful job of making an ordinary day look larger than it did a moment ago, and people return to it for exactly that reason.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Whitman has never lacked critics, and many objections to his work apply to this poem even though it is among his gentlest. Early reviewers of Leaves of Grass were often shocked. Some thought his free verse was not poetry at all but chopped-up prose, and some condemned the frankness of the book's treatment of the body. This particular poem is tame by those standards, but it was published in a collection that was widely seen as scandalous, and it shared in that bad reputation. The charge that his lines were formless has followed him ever since, and some readers still feel this poem is more a mood than a made object.

A more lasting criticism concerns repetition and looseness. Critics have sometimes said that Whitman's catalogues are easy to write and tiring to read, a kind of inventory rather than a poem. A list can feel like it could go on forever, and one can reasonably ask why this poem ends where it does. Why not add another item about the stars or the harbor? The answer is that the rhythm and the closing question bring it to rest, but a skeptical reader may feel the structure is more about stopping than concluding.

Another objection is about sentiment and ease. Some critics, particularly in the twentieth century when irony and difficulty were fashionable, regarded Whitman's cheerfulness as naive. A poem that declares everything miraculous can seem to avoid the hard question of why anything is painful. Readers who value poetry that wrestles with suffering sometimes find this poem comfortable and a little unearned, a pleasant greeting card rather than an argument. Whitman's defenders reply that the poem is a deliberate counterweight to complaint and that his larger body of work, including his Civil War poems and his years nursing wounded soldiers, shows he knew suffering intimately.

There is also the political and personal critique of Whitman's voice. His confident "I" speaks for everyone, and later readers have pointed out that the democratic "we" in his poetry did not always extend as widely in his own life and time as it did on the page. Scholars have discussed his attitudes toward race and his complicated relationship to American expansion. This short poem does not touch those issues directly, but those who read it as the voice of an all-embracing America sometimes pause over who is not on the list.

Lastly, a few readers simply find the poem thin beside Whitman's longer pieces. It has little of the strange, shaggy surprise of Song of Myself, and the language is so plain that it can slip by without leaving a mark. This criticism is probably the fairest one: not that the poem is wrong, but that it is slight, and that its greatest strength, its simplicity, is also the reason some people forget it.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This poem sits in the tradition of American Romanticism and transcendentalism, the nineteenth-century movement, led by Emerson and Thoreau, that taught that ordinary nature and ordinary life carry spiritual meaning. Emerson had written that the invariable mark of wisdom is to see the miraculous in the common, and Whitman's poem reads like that sentence turned into a walk through the city. It also belongs to the long Romantic tradition in Britain, which includes Wordsworth's wonder at daffodils and rainbows and Blake's famous wish to see a world in a grain of sand. Whitman differs from them by being more urban, more democratic, and less interested in the lonely visionary than in the crowd.

On the formal side, the poem descends from the poetry of the King James Bible, whose long parallel lines and repetitions Whitman loved, and from the oratory of nineteenth-century American speakers. The idea that poetry could be built from parallel phrases rather than rhyme goes back to the Psalms. What was new was applying that grand rhythm to a streetcar and a honeybee.

What descends from it is enormous. Free verse became the dominant mode of modern poetry, and the catalogue poem, the list that adds up to a vision, runs through Sandburg, Neruda, Ginsberg, and countless contemporary poets. In a lighter register, Gerard Manley Hopkins's praise of dappled, freckled things in Pied Beauty is a cousin in spirit, though far more tightly wrought, and Mary Oliver's attentive nature poems are another. The poem also has relatives in nature writing and in the popular science tradition that insists the actual universe is stranger than fiction.

Comparisons are instructive. Set it beside a poem like Wordsworth's lines on the rainbow, and you see the difference between wonder as private feeling and wonder as public habit. Set it beside Dickinson, who found immensity in small things through compression and surprise, and you see how Whitman reaches a similar place by the opposite road, expansion and ease. And set it against the darker modern poets, such as Eliot in The Waste Land, and you see the poem as a deliberate counterweight to the idea that the modern city is only desolation.

Why read it now? Partly because it is short and takes ninety seconds. But the deeper reason is practical. We live surrounded by distraction, and a poem that simply trains the eye to notice is more useful than it sounds. It does not claim to solve anything. It offers a habit: walk through your own day and ask what would happen if you treated each thing as a miracle. Whether you end up agreeing or not, you will have looked at the world a little more closely, and that is the deal Whitman offers, and the reason the poem has outlived the fashions that dismissed it.
