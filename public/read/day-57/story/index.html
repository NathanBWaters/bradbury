The Cats of Ulthar

H. P. Lovecraft (1920) • 7 min read

“It is said that in Ulthar, which lies beyond the river Skai, no man may kill a cat; and this I can verily believe as I gaze upon him who sitteth purring before the fire.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Cats of Ulthar is a tiny fable by H. P. Lovecraft, and it is a good place to meet him at his gentlest. Lovecraft was a reclusive writer from Providence, Rhode Island, now famous for cosmic horror, but in 1920, when he wrote this, he was deeply under the spell of an Irish fantasy author named Lord Dunsany, who wrote dreamy, invented-world tales in a grave, biblical voice. The result is not a story about tentacled gods. It is a short legend, told like something repeated by travelers, that explains why in the imaginary town of Ulthar it is against the law to kill a cat. It first appeared in a small amateur magazine called Tryout in late 1920. Lovecraft adored cats, and you can feel it. At heart it is a tale about cruelty getting its comeuppance, and about the quiet, mysterious power of the creatures we think of as simply pets. Listen for the old-fashioned storytelling voice, and for how little it explains.

It is said that in Ulthar, which lies beyond the river Skai, no man may kill a cat; and this I can verily believe as I gaze upon him who sitteth purring before the fire. For the cat is cryptic, and close to strange things which men can not see. He is the soul of antique Aegyptus, and bearer of tales from forgotten cities in Meroë and Ophir. He is the kin of the jungle’s lords, and heir to the secrets of hoary and sinister Africa. The Sphinx is his cousin, and he speaks her language; but he is more ancient than the Sphinx, and remembers that which she hath forgotten.

In Ulthar, before ever the burgesses forbade the killing of cats, there dwelt an old cotter and his wife who delighted to trap and slay the cats of their neighbors. Why they did this I know not; save that many hate the voice of the cat in the night, and take it ill that cats should run stealthily about yards and gardens at twilight. But whatever the reason, this old man and woman took pleasure in trapping and slaying every cat which came near to their hovel; and from some of the sounds heard after dark, many villagers fancied that the manner of slaying was exceedingly peculiar. But the villagers did not discuss such things with the old man and his wife; because of the habitual expression on the withered faces of the two, and because their cottage was so small and so darkly hidden under spreading oaks at the back of a neglected yard. In truth, much as the owners of cats hated these odd folk, they feared them more; and instead of berating them as brutal assassins, merely took care that no cherished pet or mouser should stray toward the remote hovel under the dark trees. When through some unavoidable oversight a cat was missed, and sounds heard after dark, the loser would lament impotently; or console himself by thanking Fate that it was not one of his children who had thus vanished. For the people of Ulthar were simple, and knew not whence it is that all cats first came.

One day a caravan of strange wanderers from the South entered the narrow cobbled streets of Ulthar. Dark wanderers they were, and unlike the other roving folk who passed through the village twice every year. In the marketplace they told fortunes for silver, and bought gay beads from the merchants. What was the land of these wanderers none could tell; but it was seen that they were given to strange prayers, and that they had painted on the sides of their wagons strange figures with human bodies and the heads of cats, hawks, rams, and lions. And the leader of the caravan wore a headdress with two horns and a curious disk betwixt the horns.

There was in this singular caravan a little boy with no father or mother, but only a tiny black kitten to cherish. The plague had not been kind to him, yet had left him this small furry thing to mitigate his sorrow; and when one is very young, one can find great relief in the lively antics of a black kitten. So the boy whom the dark people called Menes smiled more often than he wept as he sat playing with his graceful kitten on the steps of an oddly painted wagon.

On the third morning of the wanderers’ stay in Ulthar, Menes could not find his kitten; and as he sobbed aloud in the marketplace certain villagers told him of the old man and his wife, and of sounds heard in the night. And when he heard these things his sobbing gave place to meditation, and finally to prayer. He stretched out his arms toward the sun and prayed in a tongue no villager could understand; though indeed the villagers did not try very hard to understand, since their attention was mostly taken up by the sky and the odd shapes the clouds were assuming. It was very peculiar, but as the little boy uttered his petition there seemed to form overhead the shadowy, nebulous figures of exotic things; of hybrid creatures crowned with horn-flanked disks. Nature is full of such illusions to impress the imaginative.

That night the wanderers left Ulthar, and were never seen again. And the householders were troubled when they noticed that in all the village there was not a cat to be found. From each hearth the familiar cat had vanished; cats large and small, black, gray, striped, yellow and white. Old Kranon, the burgomaster, swore that the dark folk had taken the cats away in revenge for the killing of Menes’ kitten; and cursed the caravan and the little boy. But Nith, the lean notary, declared that the old cotter and his wife were more likely persons to suspect; for their hatred of cats was notorious and increasingly bold. Still, no one durst complain to the sinister couple; even when little Atal, the innkeeper’s son, vowed that he had at twilight seen all the cats of Ulthar in that accursed yard under the trees, pacing very slowly and solemnly in a circle around the cottage, two abreast, as if in performance of some unheard-of rite of beasts. The villagers did not know how much to believe from so small a boy; and though they feared that the evil pair had charmed the cats to their death, they preferred not to chide the old cotter till they met him outside his dark and repellent yard.

So Ulthar went to sleep in vain anger; and when the people awaked at dawn⁠—behold! every cat was back at his accustomed hearth! Large and small, black, gray, striped, yellow and white, none was missing. Very sleek and fat did the cats appear, and sonorous with purring content. The citizens talked with one another of the affair, and marveled not a little. Old Kranon again insisted that it was the dark folk who had taken them, since cats did not return alive from the cottage of the ancient man and his wife. But all agreed on one thing; that the refusal of all the cats to eat their portions of meat or drink their saucers of milk was exceedingly curious. And for two whole days the sleek, lazy cats of Ulthar would touch no food, but only doze by the fire or in the sun.

It was fully a week before the villagers noticed that no lights were appearing at dusk in the windows of the cottage under the trees. Then the lean Nith remarked that no one had seen the old man or his wife since the night the cats were away. In another week the burgomaster decided to overcome his fears and call at the strangely silent dwelling as a matter of duty, though in doing so he was careful to take with him Shang the blacksmith and Thul the cutter of stone as witnesses. And when they had broken down the frail door they found only this: two cleanly picked human skeletons on the earthen floor, and a number of singular beetles crawling in the shadowy corners.

There was subsequently much talk amongst the burgesses of Ulthar. Zath, the coroner, disputed at length with Nith, the lean notary; and Kranon and Shang and Thul were overwhelmed with questions. Even little Atal, the innkeeper’s son, was closely questioned and given a sweetmeat as reward. They talked of the old cotter and his wife, of the caravan of dark wanderers, of small Menes and his black kitten, of the prayer of Menes and of the sky during that prayer, of the doings of the cats on the night the caravan left, and of what was later found in the cottage under the dark trees in the repellent yard.

And in the end the burgesses passed that remarkable law which is told of by traders in Hatheg and discussed by travelers in Nir; namely, that in Ulthar no man may kill a cat.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, The Cats of Ulthar is a revenge story told as a legend. It opens with a narrator saying that in the town of Ulthar, no man may kill a cat, and that he can believe it as he watches his own cat purring by the fire. Then he tells us how the law came to be. An old man and his wife, who live in a dark hovel under oak trees, take pleasure in trapping and killing the cats of their neighbors. The villagers hate them but fear them even more, so no one confronts them. They simply keep their pets away and quietly accept the losses.

Then a caravan of dark wanderers arrives from the South. Among them is an orphaned boy named Menes, who has only a black kitten left to him after the plague took his family. When the kitten disappears, the villagers tell him about the old couple. Menes first weeps, then thinks, and then prays, stretching his arms to the sun in a tongue the villagers cannot understand. The caravan leaves the next morning, and that evening, strange things happen. The sky takes on unusual shapes, and every cat in the village vanishes. When the cats return the next day, they are sleek and well-fed, and they refuse their food for two days. Soon after, the villagers notice that the cottage under the trees is dark. When the burgomaster finally breaks in, he finds two cleanly picked human skeletons and some strange beetles. The town then passes its law.

The story never says exactly what happened, and that gap is the point. We are asked to infer that Menes's prayer, offered to some ancient power, summoned the cats to take vengeance, and that the cats ate the old couple. The strange figures painted on the caravan wagons, human bodies with the heads of cats, hawks, rams, and lions, and the leader's horned headdress with a disk between the horns, point toward ancient Egypt, where cats were sacred and animal-headed gods were worshipped. The boy's name, Menes, is the name traditionally given to the first pharaoh of a united Egypt. Lovecraft is telling us, without lecturing, that the wanderers carry an old faith, and that the cats are not just animals but servants of something older than the town.

The opening paragraph gives the key to the story's mood. Lovecraft says the cat is "cryptic, and close to strange things which men can not see," the soul of ancient Egypt and kin to the lords of the jungle. This sets up the idea that cats hold a secret knowledge.

There is also a quieter moral reading. The old couple are not punished because they are wicked in some dramatic way, but because they are casually cruel to those who cannot defend themselves, and because the community around them allows it. The villagers hate what the couple does, yet they merely lament and look away. When justice finally comes, it comes from outside, from a grieving child and the creatures he loved. Read this way, the story is about what happens when kindness is silent and cruelty goes unchecked, and about the fairy-tale idea that the small and powerless are never as helpless as they seem.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, The Cats of Ulthar is charming and uneven, and it is worth separating what works from what does not. Its greatest strength is tone. Lovecraft borrows the stately cadence of a very old tale, with phrases like "I know not" and "sitteth purring," and the voice does a lot of work. By telling the story as hearsay, something traders in Hatheg and travelers in Nir repeat, he makes it feel like folklore. The final sentence, which simply states the law, lands with the satisfaction of a proverb. That sense of an authentic legend is hard to fake, and he fakes it well.

The story is also admirably economical. In about thirteen hundred words we get a villain pair, a sympathetic child, a mystery, a climax, and a coda. The climactic episode is smartly handled by leaving it off the page. We never see what the cats do. We see only their sleek, full-bellied laziness afterward, the untouched milk, and the two clean skeletons. A more explicit writer would have staged a gory scene. Lovecraft lets the reader's imagination do it, and the quiet details, especially the cats refusing to eat, are more chilling than any description. The beetles in the corner are a perfectly odd final touch, suggesting something strange beyond the obvious.

On the other hand, it has real limitations. The characters are barely characters. The old couple are cruel because the story requires cruelty, and the text frankly admits it does not know why they do it. Menes is a sketch of a sad child, not a person. Readers wanting psychological depth will not find it. The story is also thin on suspense: because the narrator announces from the first line that cats are protected by law, and because the structure is a legend, there is little doubt about where it is going. It is more a mood piece than a thriller.

There is also the matter of ornament. The Dunsanian style, with its invented place names and its archaic grammar, is lovely in small doses but can feel precious. The quick tour of names at the end, Zath the coroner, Nith the notary, Kranon, Shang, Thul, and little Atal, is a deliberate imitation of Dunsany's fondness for exotic names, but it also exposes the story's thinness, since these characters exist only as sounds.

More troubling is the portrayal of the caravan. The "dark wanderers" are presented as mysterious, vaguely sinister foreigners, and the story relies on a stereotype of the exotic outsider, even if it grants them the moral high ground and a sympathetic child. Readers today often notice this, and it sits uneasily with an otherwise light tale. Overall, the story succeeds as a miniature, a clever, atmospheric, deliberately small piece, but it is not among Lovecraft's deepest work, and it was not meant to be.

III. The Impact It Had

The Cats of Ulthar was never a blockbuster, and its influence is gentle and specific rather than sweeping. Lovecraft wrote it in June 1920 and it appeared in the amateur journal Tryout in November of that year, a very small publication circulated among hobbyist writers. It was reprinted in Weird Tales in 1926, which gave it a far wider audience in the pulp world, and eventually it became part of the standard Lovecraft collections, so that generations of readers have met it early.

Its most important impact is on the shape of Lovecraft's own imagination. The story belongs to what scholars call his Dunsanian phase, a period in which he wrote dreamlike, myth-flavored tales in the manner of Lord Dunsany. The invented geography here, Ulthar, the river Skai, Nir, and Hatheg, would return in Lovecraft's longer fantasy, The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath, written in the 1920s, in which cats play an important and clever role and the town of Ulthar appears again. In that sense the story is a seed. It helped create the Dreamlands, a shared imaginary world that Lovecraft and later writers used for stories unlike his better-known cosmic horror. For readers who prefer wonder to dread, this strand of his work has stayed beloved.

The story also contributed to Lovecraft's lasting image as a cat person. He was well known in his circle for his affection for cats, and the story is often cited in discussions of his personality. A fan of Lovecraft will often point to it as proof that the author of so much cold, bleak fiction had a warm, almost sentimental side. It is routinely quoted, adapted, and referenced in cat-themed anthologies and in discussions of cats in fantasy, and the phrase that in Ulthar no man may kill a cat has become a kind of motto among Lovecraft fans.

In the larger culture, the story has been adapted into short films, audio readings, comics, and tabletop gaming material, and the town of Ulthar appears in role-playing games based on Lovecraft's Dreamlands. Fantasy writers have borrowed its premise of cats as keepers of old secrets, and the idea of the cat as a quiet, supernatural guardian pops up widely in fantasy fiction. A few later authors have written explicitly in its shadow, producing stories in which cats avenge abuse or guard humans from unseen dangers.

Finally, the story is taught and anthologized less as a masterpiece than as an accessible introduction. Teachers and fans like it because it is brief, readable, and carries many of the features of Lovecraft's craft without the heavy vocabulary or the hostility of his later work. It is a gentle doorway into a writer who is often approached with caution.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about The Cats of Ulthar? The first answer is the cats, of course. Anyone who has shared a home with a cat knows the mix of affection and mystery that animal can inspire, the sense that it is looking at something we cannot see. Lovecraft captures that feeling in the opening lines, and readers who love cats recognize themselves in his narrator watching his own pet by the fire. The story gives a literary form to a very common experience: the cat who sits and stares, apparently aware of things no one else notices.

The second answer is the satisfaction of justice. Readers like stories where cruelty is punished, and this one delivers its payoff with little fuss. There is something deeply pleasing about an orphan with nothing but a kitten calling on higher powers, and the powers answering. It is a revenge fantasy for people who love animals, and it has the tidy, fairy-tale morality of a story in which the bad are devoured and the good are left in peace. The fact that the vengeance is handled so calmly, with the cats simply returning and refusing their dinner, makes the justice feel both fierce and strangely elegant.

Third, readers enjoy its voice. The storyteller's tone is warm, dry, and slightly archaic, like an elder speaking by a fire. Many Lovecraft fans appreciate a break from his usual narrators, who sweat and shudder and gibber. Here, the narrator is calm and amused, and the sentences have a natural musical rhythm. Read aloud, which is how many people will hear it, the story has the pleasing shape of a bedtime legend, with its repeating names, its leisurely opening, and its crisp last line.

Fourth, the story is a treat for those who like creeping, understated horror. Because it never shows the killing, it leaves a delicious chill. The image of two clean skeletons on a dirt floor is stark enough to stay in the mind, and the beetles hint at something unsettling without overexplaining it. Readers have often noted that the story manages a lot of menace in a very small space.

Finally, it has the charm of discovery. Many readers meet it as an early, short Lovecraft, an easy path into the larger world of the Dreamlands. They find that this author who has a reputation for dark gloom can also write something light-footed, funny, and humane. That surprise is part of its affection. People often return to it when they want a short, atmospheric story that is more cozy than frightening, a story to be read before sleep without fear of nightmares, and perhaps with a cat nearby.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the story is slight and not widely reviewed in its own time, there is no famous body of contemporary criticism to cite. It appeared first in a tiny amateur magazine, and most of what has been said about it comes from later Lovecraft scholars and readers. So the criticisms that follow are the ones that have commonly been raised in later discussion, rather than a documented battle.

The most frequent complaint is that the story is minor. Even its admirers tend to call it a charming trifle. Compared with the great weight of Lovecraft's later fiction, such as The Call of Cthulhu or At the Mountains of Madness, it seems light, and critics sometimes say that it is pleasant but not memorable on its own. It lacks the cosmic scale and the philosophical edge that make his best work stand out. Some readers find the plot too thin, a single idea stretched into a short anecdote, and the lack of suspense a weakness.

A second complaint concerns its derivativeness. The Dunsany influence is so strong that some critics regard the story as pastiche, an exercise in borrowed style rather than an original work. The archaic diction, the invented names, and the legend structure all come directly from Dunsany's model, and Lovecraft himself later moved away from this manner toward his own kind of horror. Those who prefer his mature voice sometimes see this story as an apprenticeship piece.

A third and increasingly important criticism concerns race and stereotype. The caravan of dark wanderers from the South, with their strange prayers and exotic gods, belongs to a familiar pattern in which foreign people are presented as mysterious and faintly menacing. Lovecraft's racism is well documented, and readers who know that background tend to read his portrayals of outsiders with suspicion. In this story the foreigners are treated sympathetically, in that the boy is a victim and his cause is just, but they are still exoticized, and their power is described as strange rather than understood. Some critics argue that the story, for all its kindness, is shaped by the same habits of mind as his harsher work. Others contend that the story is generous to the wanderers and that its sympathies are clear.

Finally, a few readers have objected to the violence, finding the idea of cats devouring people unpleasant, or have questioned the moral logic of a punishment so extreme for what is, after all, cruelty to animals. That reaction is probably an overreading of a fable, in which exaggeration is part of the form, but it is real. The story invites us to cheer, and not everyone cheers.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Cats of Ulthar sits in a long tradition of the animal fable and the legend that explains a custom. Humans have told stories for ages about why a place has a strange rule, from tales of the origins of festivals to local folklore of cursed fields. Lovecraft's story is a literary version of this, with an ancient, mysterious explanation attached to a simple law. It also belongs to the old and widespread tradition of cats as magical or sacred beings, from the cat goddess Bastet of ancient Egypt to the many witch-and-cat tales of European folklore.

It also descends from a specific literary lineage: the fantasy of Lord Dunsany, whose collections such as The Gods of Pegana and Time and the Gods created invented worlds with their own gods, kings, and customs, told in a lofty, simple voice. Lovecraft discovered Dunsany around 1919 and was strongly influenced by him for years. The Cats of Ulthar is one of the clearest examples. Through Dunsany it also touches earlier traditions of the fairy tale and the prose poem.

Another interesting comparison is with Edgar Allan Poe's The Black Cat, which Lovecraft admired. Poe's story is about a man who cruelly harms a cat and is destroyed by his guilt and by the animal's return, and the cat there is a force of retribution. Lovecraft shifts the tone from guilt-ridden Gothic to cool fable, but the underlying idea, that cruelty to a cat invites a reckoning, is shared. Later fantasy and horror writers have returned to the same pattern, from stories of vengeful pets to modern fantasy in which cats are cunning and half-magical creatures. The pulp writer Fritz Leiber, who corresponded with Lovecraft and loved cats, wrote warm fantasy stories about them, and you can see a kinship.

Within Lovecraft's body of work, the story is an early outpost of the Dreamlands, the strand of his writing that stands apart from the Cthulhu stories. It shows another side of him, wonder and whimsy rather than dread, and it connects forward to the longer Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath and to many shorter dream tales.

Why read it now? First, it is short and graceful, a ten-minute story that leaves a distinct picture. Second, it offers an unusually human Lovecraft, one with a sense of humor and a real affection for animals. Third, it is a good example of how a story can be effective by implying rather than showing. And finally, it is a reminder that fantasy and horror can be playful. A reader who finishes it with a cat in the lap will understand why the old line still feels right: that the cat is close to strange things which men cannot see.
