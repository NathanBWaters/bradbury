Nightly Essay • Philosophy & Epistemology

The Existence of Matter

Bertrand Russell (1912) • 15 min read

Every principle of simplicity urges us to adopt the natural view, that there really are objects other than ourselves and our sense-data which have an existence not dependent upon our perceiving them.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This chapter comes from The Problems of Philosophy, the short book Bertrand Russell wrote in 1911 and published in 1912 for readers with no training in the subject. It picks up exactly where the first chapter left us. There we learned that what we directly see and touch is not the table itself but only a kind of appearance. So here Russell asks the natural next question: is there any real table at all? He was a master logician at the time, finishing Principia Mathematica with Alfred North Whitehead, but he writes here like a patient friend. The chapter is famous for its cat, and for a very practical idea about how to reason when certainty is out of reach. Its overall meaning is that we cannot prove the outside world exists, but we have excellent reasons to believe it, and philosophy's job is to organise our deepest instinctive beliefs, not to throw them away. Listen for how modest and clever that conclusion is.

In this chapter we have to ask ourselves whether, in any sense at all, there is such a thing as matter. Is there a table which has a certain intrinsic nature, and continues to exist when I am not looking, or is the table merely a product of my imagination, a dream-table in a very prolonged dream? This question is of the greatest importance. For if we cannot be sure of the independent existence of objects, we cannot be sure of the independent existence of other people’s bodies, and therefore still less of other people’s minds, since we have no grounds for believing in their minds except such as are derived from observing their bodies. Thus if we cannot be sure of the independent existence of objects, we shall be left alone in a desert⁠—it may be that the whole outer world is nothing but a dream, and that we alone exist. This is an uncomfortable possibility; but although it cannot be strictly proved to be false, there is not the slightest reason to suppose that it is true. In this chapter we have to see why this is the case.

Before we embark upon doubtful matters, let us try to find some more or less fixed point from which to start. Although we are doubting the physical existence of the table, we are not doubting the existence of the sense-data which made us think there was a table; we are not doubting that, while we look, a certain colour and shape appear to us, and while we press, a certain sensation of hardness is experienced by us. All this, which is psychological, we are not calling in question. In fact, whatever else may be doubtful, some at least of our immediate experiences seem absolutely certain.

Descartes (1596⁠–⁠1650), the founder of modern philosophy, invented a method which may still be used with profit⁠—the method of systematic doubt. He determined that he would believe nothing which he did not see quite clearly and distinctly to be true. Whatever he could bring himself to doubt, he would doubt, until he saw reason for not doubting it. By applying this method he gradually became convinced that the only existence of which he could be quite certain was his own. He imagined a deceitful demon, who presented unreal things to his senses in a perpetual phantasmagoria; it might be very improbable that such a demon existed, but still it was possible, and therefore doubt concerning things perceived by the senses was possible.

But doubt concerning his own existence was not possible, for if he did not exist, no demon could deceive him. If he doubted, he must exist; if he had any experiences whatever, he must exist. Thus his own existence was an absolute certainty to him. “I think, therefore I am,” he said (Cogito, ergo sum); and on the basis of this certainty he set to work to build up again the world of knowledge which his doubt had laid in ruins. By inventing the method of doubt, and by showing that subjective things are the most certain, Descartes performed a great service to philosophy, and one which makes him still useful to all students of the subject.

But some care is needed in using Descartes’ argument. “I think, therefore I am” says rather more than is strictly certain. It might seem as though we were quite sure of being the same person today as we were yesterday, and this is no doubt true in some sense. But the real Self is as hard to arrive at as the real table, and does not seem to have that absolute, convincing certainty that belongs to particular experiences. When I look at my table and see a certain brown colour, what is quite certain at once is not “I am seeing a brown colour,” but rather, “a brown colour is being seen.” This of course involves something (or somebody) which (or who) sees the brown colour; but it does not of itself involve that more or less permanent person whom we call “I.” So far as immediate certainty goes, it might be that the something which sees the brown colour is quite momentary, and not the same as the something which has some different experience the next moment.

Thus it is our particular thoughts and feelings that have primitive certainty. And this applies to dreams and hallucinations as well as to normal perceptions: when we dream or see a ghost, we certainly do have the sensations we think we have, but for various reasons it is held that no physical object corresponds to these sensations. Thus the certainty of our knowledge of our own experiences does not have to be limited in any way to allow for exceptional cases. Here, therefore, we have, for what it is worth, a solid basis from which to begin our pursuit of knowledge.

The problem we have to consider is this: Granted that we are certain of our own sense-data, have we any reason for regarding them as signs of the existence of something else, which we can call the physical object? When we have enumerated all the sense-data which we should naturally regard as connected with the table, have we said all there is to say about the table, or is there still something else⁠—something not a sense-datum, something which persists when we go out of the room? Common sense unhesitatingly answers that there is. What can be bought and sold and pushed about and have a cloth laid on it, and so on, cannot be a mere collection of sense-data. If the cloth completely hides the table, we shall derive no sense-data from the table, and therefore, if the table were merely sense-data, it would have ceased to exist, and the cloth would be suspended in empty air, resting, by a miracle, in the place where the table formerly was. This seems plainly absurd; but whoever wishes to become a philosopher must learn not to be frightened by absurdities.

One great reason why it is felt that we must secure a physical object in addition to the sense-data, is that we want the same object for different people. When ten people are sitting round a dinner-table, it seems preposterous to maintain that they are not seeing the same tablecloth, the same knives and forks and spoons and glasses. But the sense-data are private to each separate person; what is immediately present to the sight of one is not immediately present to the sight of another: they all see things from slightly different points of view, and therefore see them slightly differently. Thus, if there are to be public neutral objects, which can be in some sense known to many different people, there must be something over and above the private and particular sense-data which appear to various people. What reason, then, have we for believing that there are such public neutral objects?

The first answer that naturally occurs to one is that, although different people may see the table slightly differently, still they all see more or less similar things when they look at the table, and the variations in what they see follow the laws of perspective and reflection of light, so that it is easy to arrive at a permanent object underlying all the different people’s sense-data. I bought my table from the former occupant of my room; I could not buy his sense-data, which died when he went away, but I could and did buy the confident expectation of more or less similar sense-data. Thus it is the fact that different people have similar sense-data, and that one person in a given place at different times has similar sense-data, which makes us suppose that over and above the sense-data there is a permanent public object which underlies or causes the sense-data of various people at various times.

Now in so far as the above considerations depend upon supposing that there are other people besides ourselves, they beg the very question at issue. Other people are represented to me by certain sense-data, such as the sight of them or the sound of their voices, and if I had no reason to believe that there were physical objects independent of my sense-data, I should have no reason to believe that other people exist except as part of my dream. Thus, when we are trying to show that there must be objects independent of our own sense-data, we cannot appeal to the testimony of other people, since this testimony itself consists of sense-data, and does not reveal other people’s experiences unless our own sense-data are signs of things existing independently of us. We must therefore, if possible, find, in our own purely private experiences, characteristics which show, or tend to show, that there are in the world things other than ourselves and our private experiences.

In one sense it must be admitted that we can never prove the existence of things other than ourselves and our experiences. No logical absurdity results from the hypothesis that the world consists of myself and my thoughts and feelings and sensations, and that everything else is mere fancy. In dreams a very complicated world may seem to be present, and yet on waking we find it was a delusion; that is to say, we find that the sense-data in the dream do not appear to have corresponded with such physical objects as we should naturally infer from our sense-data. (It is true that, when the physical world is assumed, it is possible to find physical causes for the sense-data in dreams: a door banging, for instance, may cause us to dream of a naval engagement. But although, in this case, there is a physical cause for the sense-data, there is not a physical object corresponding to the sense-data in the way in which an actual naval battle would correspond.) There is no logical impossibility in the supposition that the whole of life is a dream, in which we ourselves create all the objects that come before us. But although this is not logically impossible, there is no reason whatever to suppose that it is true; and it is, in fact, a less simple hypothesis, viewed as a means of accounting for the facts of our own life, than the commonsense hypothesis that there really are objects independent of us, whose action on us causes our sensations.

The way in which simplicity comes in from supposing that there really are physical objects is easily seen. If the cat appears at one moment in one part of the room, and at another in another part, it is natural to suppose that it has moved from the one to the other, passing over a series of intermediate positions. But if it is merely a set of sense-data, it cannot have ever been in any place where I did not see it; thus we shall have to suppose that it did not exist at all while I was not looking, but suddenly sprang into being in a new place. If the cat exists whether I see it or not, we can understand from our own experience how it gets hungry between one meal and the next; but if it does not exist when I am not seeing it, it seems odd that appetite should grow during nonexistence as fast as during existence. And if the cat consists only of sense-data, it cannot be hungry, since no hunger but my own can be a sense-datum to me. Thus the behaviour of the sense-data which represent the cat to me, though it seems quite natural when regarded as an expression of hunger, becomes utterly inexplicable when regarded as mere movements and changes of patches of colour, which are as incapable of hunger as a triangle is of playing football.

But the difficulty in the case of the cat is nothing compared to the difficulty in the case of human beings. When human beings speak⁠—that is, when we hear certain noises which we associate with ideas, and simultaneously see certain motions of lips and expressions of face⁠—it is very difficult to suppose that what we hear is not the expression of a thought, as we know it would be if we emitted the same sounds. Of course similar things happen in dreams, where we are mistaken as to the existence of other people. But dreams are more or less suggested by what we call waking life, and are capable of being more or less accounted for on scientific principles if we assume that there really is a physical world. Thus every principle of simplicity urges us to adopt the natural view, that there really are objects other than ourselves and our sense-data which have an existence not dependent upon our perceiving them.

Of course it is not by argument that we originally come by our belief in an independent external world. We find this belief ready in ourselves as soon as we begin to reflect: it is what may be called an instinctive belief. We should never have been led to question this belief but for the fact that, at any rate in the case of sight, it seems as if the sense-datum itself were instinctively believed to be the independent object, whereas argument shows that the object cannot be identical with the sense-datum. This discovery, however⁠—which is not at all paradoxical in the case of taste and smell and sound, and only slightly so in the case of touch⁠—leaves undiminished our instinctive belief that there are objects corresponding to our sense-data. Since this belief does not lead to any difficulties, but on the contrary tends to simplify and systematize our account of our experiences, there seems no good reason for rejecting it. We may therefore admit⁠—though with a slight doubt derived from dreams⁠—that the external world does really exist, and is not wholly dependent for its existence upon our continuing to perceive it.

The argument which has led us to this conclusion is doubtless less strong than we could wish, but it is typical of many philosophical arguments, and it is therefore worth while to consider briefly its general character and validity. All knowledge, we find, must be built up upon our instinctive beliefs, and if these are rejected, nothing is left. But among our instinctive beliefs some are much stronger than others, while many have, by habit and association, become entangled with other beliefs, not really instinctive, but falsely supposed to be part of what is believed instinctively.

Philosophy should show us the hierarchy of our instinctive beliefs, beginning with those we hold most strongly, and presenting each as much isolated and as free from irrelevant additions as possible. It should take care to show that, in the form in which they are finally set forth, our instinctive beliefs do not clash, but form a harmonious system. There can never be any reason for rejecting one instinctive belief except that it clashes with others; thus, if they are found to harmonize, the whole system becomes worthy of acceptance.

It is of course possible that all or any of our beliefs may be mistaken, and therefore all ought to be held with at least some slight element of doubt. But we cannot have reason to reject a belief except on the ground of some other belief. Hence, by organizing our instinctive beliefs and their consequences, by considering which among them is most possible, if necessary, to modify or abandon, we can arrive, on the basis of accepting as our sole data what we instinctively believe, at an orderly systematic organization of our knowledge, in which, though the possibility of error remains, its likelihood is diminished by the interrelation of the parts and by the critical scrutiny which has preceded acquiescence.

This function, at least, philosophy can perform. Most philosophers, rightly or wrongly, believe that philosophy can do much more than this⁠—that it can give us knowledge, not otherwise attainable, concerning the universe as a whole, and concerning the nature of ultimate reality. Whether this be the case or not, the more modest function we have spoken of can certainly be performed by philosophy, and certainly suffices, for those who have once begun to doubt the adequacy of common sense, to justify the arduous and difficult labours that philosophical problems involve.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The chapter opens from the wreckage of the one before it. If what we immediately experience is only sense-data, the colours, shapes and hardnesses that appear to us, then we have no direct proof that anything exists apart from them. Russell sets out to see what, if anything, can be rescued. He makes a deliberate start with the harshest possible doubt. Following the method of Descartes, he asks what could survive if we doubted everything we could possibly doubt. The answer is that we are certain of our own present experience. I may not be sure there is a table, but I am sure that I am seeing a certain brown colour. Even that, he adds, is a more careful statement than the old formula that the self exists, because the thing we really know is the experience, not some lasting person behind it.

From that narrow ground, the problem is plain. How do we get from private sense-data to a public world of tables and trees? Russell considers two bad answers before settling on a good one. The first is simply to say that dreams show our senses can mislead us, so perhaps everything is a dream. He admits we cannot logically refute this. But he points out that dreams and waking life differ in how well they fit together, and that mere possibility is not a reason for belief.

His real reply comes through the cat. Suppose a cat is in one corner of the room, and later, in another. If cats were only bundles of sense-data, the cat would have to jump across the room without passing through the places between, and it would eat when it was not even seen to eat, while hunger arrived without any food. A cat that exists independently, moving about, eating and getting hungry, makes the whole thing easy to understand. The same goes for people: if my friend's words are only noises in my own mind, it becomes baffling that they answer my questions in ways I never expected.

So Russell's argument is not a proof but a comparison. The hypothesis that there is an external world simplifies and unifies our experience, while the rival hypothesis leaves it a chaotic jumble. He concludes that we may accept the real existence of the external world, with, as he puts it, a slight doubt derived from dreams.

The deeper layer is the final section, where he steps back to reflect on his own method. All knowledge, he says, must be built on our instinctive beliefs, and if we reject those, nothing is left. There can never be reason to reject one instinctive belief except that it clashes with others. So philosophy should sort these beliefs into a hierarchy, strip off the irrelevant additions, and check that they fit together. That, he says, is a modest but real function, and it justifies the effort.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The strength of this chapter is the final turn, where Russell reflects on what he has just done. Most introductions would have declared victory over the sceptic. Russell is more honest. He concedes that the argument is probably weaker than we would like, and then he makes a virtue of it by describing it as typical of philosophical argument. That is a rare piece of intellectual candour. It also yields a genuinely useful idea: if proof is impossible, the rational course is to start with the beliefs we cannot help holding, rank them by strength, and keep the ones that cohere. That view, a sort of coherence-minded common sense, has aged well.

The cat is also a charming bit of writing. It converts an abstract worry into a scene anyone can picture, and it has the advantage of making the sceptical alternative look comic without being dismissive. A reader laughs at the cat leaping about as disconnected appearances, and in laughing grasps what simplicity means as a criterion for belief.

But the chapter has weak points, and they are instructive. The first is that the reasoning is not as neat as it looks. Russell says the real-world hypothesis is simpler, but simple for whom, and by what standard? A determined idealist could say that a world of ideas ordered by God or by laws is no more complicated. He does not really answer this. Simplicity is doing a lot of silent work.

Second, the argument depends on the very point it is trying to prove. To say that a cat must eat when I am not looking presupposes that there are causal regularities in unobserved matters. But those regularities are exactly what a person without belief in an external world would not grant. The inference is therefore somewhat circular, and Russell half knows it, which is why he leans on the language of instinct instead of proof.

Third, the handling of the self is awkward. He moves quickly from the certainty of present experience toward discussion of a person who has it, and he seems to treat the existence of other people's minds as a matter of analogy, a topic that has troubled philosophers a great deal and that he treats in a couple of sentences.

Finally, the tone sometimes sounds more settled than the philosophy underneath it. The concluding paragraphs promise that a well-organised system of instinctive beliefs becomes worthy of acceptance, but he does not say what happens when two strong instincts really do conflict, or how to decide which one yields. That limit is real, though a reader is rarely troubled by it on a first reading because the prose moves so gracefully.

III. The Impact It Had

This chapter did not launch a school or produce a famous slogan, yet its method has quietly shaped how many philosophers argue. It is one of the clearest early statements of a mode of reasoning later called inference to the best explanation. When Russell says that the existence of the cat explains our experiences more simply than the alternative, he is doing what scientists and detectives do all the time: choosing the account that best makes sense of the evidence. Philosophers have since given that style of reasoning a proper name and a literature, and the cat is a good short example of it.

The chapter also fed into Russell's own next steps. In 1914 he published Our Knowledge of the External World, where he tried to build physical objects out of sense-data using logical tools, a project that went on to influence the logical positivists in Vienna and A. J. Ayer in Britain. The thought that external objects might be constructions from experience, instead of hidden causes, grows from questions raised here.

More broadly, the closing pages on instinctive beliefs became a model of what is now called a coherence-minded approach to justification. The idea that we cannot step outside all our beliefs, that we start somewhere and then repair the boat while at sea, has echoes in later thinkers. G. E. Moore defended common sense in a famous way, and Wittgenstein's late notes On Certainty explore the question of which beliefs can be doubted and which form the hinges on which doubt turns. Quine's picture of knowledge as a web of beliefs adjusting to experience is also in the same family. None of them borrowed directly from this chapter, but Russell's modest framing was a clear early version of the stance.

In teaching, the chapter has become standard. It is paired with the first chapter in introductory courses on knowledge and perception, and it is a favourite for first-year students because it shows a philosopher working through an objection step by step. The cat in particular has become a kind of shorthand, appearing in lecture notes and textbooks as the example of how we reason about unobserved objects.

Outside the academy, the chapter's real impact is cultural. It showed a general audience that one can take radical scepticism seriously without being paralysed by it, and that the proper response to the impossibility of proof is not despair but careful, humble organisation of what we already believe. That mixture of rigour and reassurance has been a part of Russell's lasting public appeal.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers tend to love this chapter for being reassuring without being cheap. After the unsettling first chapter, which left the table in pieces, many readers feel real relief on finding that Russell does not leave them stranded in doubt. He gives them a way to go on believing in the world with their eyes open. That emotional movement from vertigo to a steady, adult confidence is part of why the book as a whole has been so loved.

The cat is the most affectionately remembered part. It is a rare case where a philosophical example is both funny and exact. People who have forgotten the rest of the argument often remember that a cat's hunger makes little sense if the cat is only a string of appearances, since hunger needs a real stomach. It appeals because it takes the question seriously and answers with common sense dressed up in good logic.

Readers also admire the intellectual honesty. When Russell says that the argument is less strong than we could wish, he gives up the false confidence that often marks introductory books. People tend to trust a guide who points out where the ground is soft. Teachers report that students respond well to the admission that philosophical arguments rarely end in proof, and that this does not make them worthless. It makes philosophy feel like a real activity rather than a catalogue of answers.

There is as well the appeal of the concluding vision of philosophy's job. The idea that thinking should arrange our beliefs in a hierarchy, remove accretions and test for harmony has struck many readers as a humane, practical picture of reflection. It works well outside philosophy, for anyone trying to sort out what they really believe from what they have merely absorbed. People have borrowed the idea for ethics, politics and ordinary life.

Finally, readers like the manner. Russell's sentences are balanced and unhurried. He rarely strains for effect. In a few places his dry humour peeks through, as when he treats the hunger of an imaginary cat as evidence in a serious debate. That blend of seriousness and lightness is a hallmark of his best prose and is much of why people reread this book decades after first meeting it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The standard objection is that the chapter does not really answer the sceptic. Russell himself sees this. His argument shows that the real-world hypothesis is a better explanation than its rivals, but a determined sceptic can reply that preferring a simpler or tidier explanation is not the same as showing it is true. The world might be tidy because it is a dream or a simulation, and nothing in the cat example rules that out. Critics have often said that the argument has the shape of a proof without its force, and that Russell mildly oversells the strength of simplicity as a guide to truth.

A second line of criticism targets the starting point. The whole chapter assumes that we begin with private sense-data and have to work our way out. Philosophers of very different stripes have rejected that. Wittgenstein argued in his late work that radical doubt of this kind makes no sense, because doubting presupposes a shared world and language in which doubts have meaning. Heidegger and the phenomenologists said we are always already out in the world, so the picture of a mind first locked inside and then reaching out is a misdescription. For these critics Russell solves a problem that should never have been set.

Others, especially followers of ordinary-language philosophy, accused the sense-data approach of manufacturing a puzzle from a misuse of words, as J. L. Austin did in the 1950s. If the premise that we see only sense-data is wrong, then the careful rescue of the external world is a rescue from a danger that was never real.

Idealists of the older school thought Russell was not radical enough. Followers of Berkeley, or of the British idealists Russell had himself rebelled against, felt that he treated matter as an unexplained hidden cause when a more economical account was available. From the other side, some materialists and scientists found the whole preoccupation with sense-data bloated and unnecessary, since science can study perception directly without worrying whether the world exists.

A smaller complaint concerns the treatment of other minds. The cat is nice, but the passage on people is quick, and critics say the problem of how we know other people have minds, which is more difficult than the problem of matter, is passed over with an appeal to analogy.

As with much of Russell's popular writing, the book can be accused of making difficult debates look more settled than they are. Russell himself wrote later that he had changed his mind on several points, and some of the machinery here, such as the confident use of sense-data, is no longer where he stood.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter sits in the long conversation about scepticism, the worry that we cannot be sure the outside world exists. It belongs to a lineage that runs from the ancient sceptics through Descartes's first Meditation, with its dreaming and evil demon, to Berkeley's idealism and Hume's sceptical bite. Russell's twist is to treat the sceptic as someone to be answered with ordinary methods of explanation rather than with a fresh certainty, and that puts him in the company of those who thought philosophy should start from what we are already committed to.

The nearest cousin is Hume, who said that nature is too strong for sceptical principles. We cannot help believing in an external world, and philosophy cannot shift that. Russell takes up the same instinct but turns it into a positive method: since the belief is unavoidable and harmless, it counts as our data, and the task is to organise it. In this he anticipates the pragmatic attitude of later thinkers, and also the naturalism of Quine, who held that we work from within our current theory of the world and cannot step outside it. Moore's famous proof of an external world, from the simple claim that here is a hand, belongs to the same family, though it is far blunter than Russell's gentle argument.

Looking ahead, the modern literature on scepticism, with its brains in vats and simulation arguments, extends the cases Russell touches. Philosophers still debate whether the best explanation of our experience is the external world, a question treated with real technical tools in the philosophy of science. Contemporary epistemology includes whole programmes, such as reliabilism and contextualism, that address the same gap he opens.

For comparison, think of the Cartesian answer, which tries to prove the world from the certainty of the self and the goodness of God, and of Kant's response, which says the world of experience is partly constructed by the mind. Russell's answer is less ambitious than either, and in some ways more convincing for being so.

Why does it still matter? Partly because the central lesson is practical. In a time of conspiracy theories, simulated media and loud claims to certainty, the idea that we should believe what best fits together, hold our beliefs with a slight element of doubt, and test them against each other is useful far beyond philosophy. It is also a model of how to write about thought: honest, readable and generous. Read it for the cat, and stay for the calm account of what it means to know anything at all.
