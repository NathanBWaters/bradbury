Nightly Short Story • Modernist Fiction

In Another Country

Ernest Hemingway (1927) • 11 min read

“The doctor came up to the machine where I was sitting and said: “What did you like best to do before the war? Did you practise a sport?””

Key Context & Why It Matters

Ernest Hemingway published In Another Country in 1927, in a collection called Men Without Women, and it is one of the quietest and most devastating stories he ever wrote. Hemingway was badly wounded as a very young man in Italy in the First World War, and he spent months recovering in a Milan hospital. The story is not really about heroics. It is about what happens after the fighting, when wounded men sit in machines meant to repair their bodies, and try not to think about what cannot be repaired. The writing is spare and plain, with short sentences and almost no explanation of feelings. Hemingway believed that what you leave out can hit harder than what you say, and this story is a good test of that idea. The title echoes an old line from Christopher Marlowe about a distant country and a dead woman. Listen for the Italian major with the withered hand, and for what he says about marriage.

In the fall the war was always there, but we did not go to it any more. It was cold in the fall in Milan and the dark came very early. Then the electric lights came on, and it was pleasant along the streets looking in the windows. There was much game hanging outside the shops, and the snow powdered in the fur of the foxes and the wind blew their tails. The deer hung stiff and heavy and empty, and small birds blew in the wind and the wind turned their feathers. It was a cold fall and the wind came down from the mountains.

We were all at the hospital every afternoon, and there were different ways of walking across the town through the dusk to the hospital. Two of the ways were alongside canals, but they were long. Always, though, you crossed a bridge across a canal to enter the hospital. There was a choice of three bridges. On one of them a woman sold roasted chestnuts. It was warm, standing in front of her charcoal fire, and the chestnuts were warm afterward in your pocket. The hospital was very old and very beautiful, and you entered through a gate and walked across a courtyard and out a gate on the other side. There were usually funerals starting from the courtyard. Beyond the old hospital were the new brick pavilions, and there we met every afternoon and were all very polite and interested in what was the matter, and sat in the machines that were to make so much difference.

The doctor came up to the machine where I was sitting and said: “What did you like best to do before the war? Did you practise a sport?”

I said: “Yes, football.”

“Good,” he said. “You will be able to play football again better than ever.”

My knee did not bend and the leg dropped straight from the knee to the ankle without a calf, and the machine was to bend the knee and make it move as in riding a tricycle. But it did not bend yet, and instead the machine lurched when it came to the bending part. The doctor said: “That will all pass. You are a fortunate young man. You will play football again like a champion.”

In the next machine was a major who had a little hand like a baby’s. He winked at me when the doctor examined his hand, which was between two leather straps that bounced up and down and flapped the stiff fingers, and said: “And will I too play football, captain-doctor?” He had been a very great fencer, and before the war the greatest fencer in Italy.

The doctor went to his office in a back room and brought a photograph which showed a hand that had been withered almost as small as the major’s, before it had taken a machine course, and after was a little larger. The major held the photograph with his good hand and looked at it very carefully. “A wound?” he asked.

“An industrial accident,” the doctor said.

“Very interesting, very interesting,” the major said, and handed it back to the doctor.

“You have confidence?”

“No,” said the major.

There were three boys who came each day who were about the same age I was. They were all three from Milan, and one of them was to be a lawyer, and one was to be a painter, and one had intended to be a soldier, and after we were finished with the machines, sometimes we walked back together to the Café Cova, which was next door to the Scala. We walked the short way through the communist quarter because we were four together. The people hated us because we were officers, and from a wine-shop someone would call out, “A basso gli ufficiali!” as we passed. Another boy who walked with us sometimes and made us five wore a black silk handkerchief across his face because he had no nose then and his face was to be rebuilt. He had gone out to the front from the military academy and been wounded within an hour after he had gone into the front line for the first time. They rebuilt his face, but he came from a very old family and they could never get the nose exactly right. He went to South America and worked in a bank. But this was a long time ago, and then we did not any of us know how it was going to be afterward. We only knew then that there was always the war, but that we were not going to it any more.

We all had the same medals, except the boy with the black silk bandage across his face, and he had not been at the front long enough to get any medals. The tall boy with a very pale face who was to be a lawyer had been a lieutenant of Arditi and had three medals of the sort we each had only one of. He had lived a very long time with death and was a little detached. We were all a little detached, and there was nothing that held us together except that we met every afternoon at the hospital. Although, as we walked to the Cova through the tough part of town, walking in the dark, with light and singing coming out of the wine-shops, and sometimes having to walk into the street when the men and women would crowd together on the sidewalk so that we would have had to jostle them to get by, we felt held together by there being something that had happened that they, the people who disliked us, did not understand.

We ourselves all understood the Cova, where it was rich and warm and not too brightly lighted, and noisy and smoky at certain hours, and there were always girls at the tables and the illustrated papers on a rack on the wall. The girls at the Cova were very patriotic, and I found that the most patriotic people in Italy were the café girls⁠—and I believe they are still patriotic.

The boys at first were very polite about my medals and asked me what I had done to get them. I showed them the papers, which were written in very beautiful language and full of fratellanza and abnegazione, but which really said, with the adjectives removed, that I had been given the medals because I was an American. After that their manner changed a little toward me, although I was their friend against outsiders. I was a friend, but I was never really one of them after they had read the citations, because it had been different with them and they had done very different things to get their medals. I had been wounded, it was true; but we all knew that being wounded, after all, was really an accident. I was never ashamed of the ribbons, though, and sometimes, after the cocktail hour, I would imagine myself having done all the things they had done to get their medals; but walking home at night through the empty streets with the cold wind and all the shops closed, trying to keep near the street lights, I knew that I would never have done such things, and I was very much afraid to die, and often lay in bed at night by myself, afraid to die and wondering how I would be when I went back to the front again.

The three with the medals were like hunting-hawks; and I was not a hawk, although I might seem a hawk to those who had never hunted; they, the three, knew better and so we drifted apart. But I stayed good friends with the boy who had been wounded his first day at the front, because he would never know now how he would have turned out; so he could never be accepted either, and I liked him because I thought perhaps he would not have turned out to be a hawk either.

The major, who had been the great fencer, did not believe in bravery, and spent much time while we sat in the machines correcting my grammar. He had complimented me on how I spoke Italian, and we talked together very easily. One day I had said that Italian seemed such an easy language to me that I could not take a great interest in it; everything was so easy to say. “Ah, yes,” the major said. “Why, then, do you not take up the use of grammar?” So we took up the use of grammar, and soon Italian was such a difficult language that I was afraid to talk to him until I had the grammar straight in my mind.

The major came very regularly to the hospital. I do not think he ever missed a day, although I am sure he did not believe in the machines. There was a time when none of us believed in the machines, and one day the major said it was all nonsense. The machines were new then and it was we who were to prove them. It was an idiotic idea, he said, “a theory, like another.” I had not learned my grammar, and he said I was a stupid impossible disgrace, and he was a fool to have bothered with me. He was a small man and he sat straight up in his chair with his right hand thrust into the machine and looked straight ahead at the wall while the straps thumped up and down with his fingers in them.

“What will you do when the war is over if it is over?” he asked me. “Speak grammatically!”

“I will go to the States.”

“Are you married?”

“No, but I hope to be.”

“The more of a fool you are,” he said. He seemed very angry. “A man must not marry.”

“Why, Signor Maggiore?”

“Don’t call me ‘Signor Maggiore.’ ”

“Why must not a man marry?”

“He cannot marry. He cannot marry,” he said angrily. “If he is to lose everything, he should not place himself in a position to lose that. He should not place himself in a position to lose. He should find things he cannot lose.”

He spoke very angrily and bitterly, and looked straight ahead while he talked.

“But why should he necessarily lose it?”

“He’ll lose it,” the major said. He was looking at the wall. Then he looked down at the machine and jerked his little hand out from between the straps and slapped it hard against his thigh. “He’ll lose it,” he almost shouted. “Don’t argue with me!” Then he called to the attendant who ran the machines. “Come and turn this damned thing off.”

He went back into the other room for the light treatment and the massage. Then I heard him ask the doctor if he might use his telephone and he shut the door. When he came back into the room, I was sitting in another machine. He was wearing his cape and had his cap on, and he came directly toward my machine and put his arm on my shoulder.

“I am so sorry,” he said, and patted me on the shoulder with his good hand. “I would not be rude. My wife has just died. You must forgive me.”

“Oh⁠—” I said, feeling sick for him. “I am so sorry.”

He stood there biting his lower lip. “It is very difficult,” he said. “I cannot resign myself.”

He looked straight past me and out through the window. Then he began to cry. “I am utterly unable to resign myself,” he said and choked. And then crying, his head up looking at nothing, carrying himself straight and soldierly, with tears on both his cheeks and biting his lips, he walked past the machines and out the door.

The doctor told me that the major’s wife, who was very young and whom he had not married until he was definitely invalided out of the war, had died of pneumonia. She had been sick only a few days. No one expected her to die. The major did not come to the hospital for three days. Then he came at the usual hour, wearing a black band on the sleeve of his uniform. When he came back, there were large framed photographs around the wall, of all sorts of wounds before and after they had been cured by the machines. In front of the machine the major used were three photographs of hands like his that were completely restored. I do not know where the doctor got them. I always understood we were the first to use the machines. The photographs did not make much difference to the major because he only looked out of the window.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, almost nothing happens in this story. A young American officer, wounded in the knee, goes to a hospital in Milan every afternoon to sit in a machine that is supposed to bend his stiff leg. He walks there through the cold autumn dusk, past shops with game hanging in the windows, across bridges, through a courtyard where funerals often begin. He meets three young Italian officers who are also patients, and a major who used to be the finest fencer in Italy and now has a hand withered to the size of a baby's. The major corrects the narrator's grammar. And then, near the end, the major learns that his young wife has died of pneumonia, and falls apart in a stiff, upright, soldierly way.

The meaning lives in what the story refuses to say. Look at the opening. "In the fall the war was always there, but we did not go to it any more." The men are suspended between two worlds. The war is over for them, but they are not yet back in normal life, and the narrator keeps noticing death in the background: the funerals in the courtyard, the dead deer hanging in the shop windows, stiff and heavy and empty. The hospital's cheerful talk is a kind of lie. The doctor tells the narrator he will play football again like a champion, and tells the major that his hand will recover, and nobody in the room believes him. When the doctor asks the major, "You have confidence?" and the major answers with a flat "No," it is the most honest moment in the room.

The medals matter too. The narrator has one, but he is an American, and he knows his medal was given partly out of courtesy. The three Italian boys earned theirs by real bravery, and the narrator says they were like hunting hawks, while he was not a hawk, although he might seem like one to people who had never hunted. He is afraid to die, and he lies awake at night thinking about going back. This is not a story about brave men. It is about the gap between how courage looks from outside and how it feels from inside.

Then comes the major's speech about marriage. He is furious at the narrator for hoping to marry. "A man must not marry," he says, because a man who has already lost everything should not put himself in the position of losing anything else. He should find things he cannot lose. At that point the reader does not yet know why he is so angry. Then he takes a phone call, comes back in his cape, and tells the narrator that his wife has just died. The major's cruelty a minute earlier was grief he did not yet know about, or a dread he had always carried. Having lost his fencing and his hand, he put what remained of his hope into a young wife, and now she is gone.

The last image is the photographs. The doctor hangs framed pictures of healed hands on the wall, but the major simply looks out the window. Medicine can restore a hand, perhaps, but not a person, and the machines cannot fix what is actually broken. That is the whole story: a man who has been trained to endure everything, and finds the one thing he cannot endure.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

In Another Country is close to a perfect piece of short fiction, and its best qualities are also the ones that are easiest to take for granted. The first paragraph alone does a remarkable amount of work. The sentences are mostly plain and repetitive, built on the word "and," so the prose feels like a person walking: one step, another step, a bridge, a chestnut seller, a gate. Underneath that calm are the dead animals and the funerals, so that death is placed in the background of every pleasant detail. Hemingway never says "I am afraid" until the story is well underway, and by then the reader has felt it for pages.

The structure is just as smart. The story moves from general to specific. It begins with the narrator's habits and the whole group of wounded men, and only slowly narrows to the major, and then to a single afternoon. The announcement of the wife's death arrives almost offhandedly, with no musical build, and it is all the more powerful for that. The reversal is also fair. The major's rage about marriage is planted a few lines before the reader learns why, so that on a second reading his anger is heartbreaking rather than merely strange. The last two details, the black armband and the photographs he ignores, let the reader draw the conclusion without being told.

If the story has weaknesses, they are small and mostly matters of taste. Hemingway's narrator is deliberately blank. He is a camera with a conscience, but he is not much of a character, and readers who want more access to his inner life may find him thin. The story also leans on a few familiar Hemingway attitudes, such as the idea that real soldiers are quiet and tired of talk, and that the people who suffer most say least. That stoicism is moving here, but when imitated by lesser writers, and sometimes by Hemingway himself later on, it hardens into pose. The Italian dialogue is also simplified, with characters speaking in a kind of translated plainness that can sound stiff.

Still, these are the kind of objections one makes about a masterpiece. The story is short enough to read in fifteen minutes, yet it contains an entire theory of loss. Hemingway later described his method as showing only the tip of the iceberg while the rest stays beneath the surface. Here the iceberg is enormous, and the tip is a man looking out a window.

III. The Impact It Had

In Another Country was published in Scribner's Magazine in April 1927 and then in Men Without Women, Hemingway's second collection of short stories, which came out that October. By then he was already famous. The Sun Also Rises had appeared the year before, and critics were arguing about whether he was the voice of a whole disillusioned generation. The stories in Men Without Women, including The Killers and Hills Like White Elephants, confirmed that his short fiction was at least as influential as his novels.

The story's greatest effect has been on the way writers handle emotion. Hemingway's approach, which he later described as the iceberg theory, holds that a writer who knows his subject well can leave out most of what he knows, and the reader will feel it anyway. Generations of writers have learned from that. Short story writers such as Raymond Carver, with his stripped-down domestic scenes, and many writers of the so-called minimalist school took lessons from Hemingway's habit of letting a plain surface carry enormous pressure. You can hear echoes of it in war fiction, from Tim O'Brien to many younger writers who write about veterans who cannot explain what they carry.

The story also helped shape how we think about the aftermath of war. Much earlier war writing was about glory or about battles. Hemingway, along with other writers of the 1920s, turned attention to the hospital, the long recovery, and the men who return unable to say what happened. Here the war is almost absent. We never see a battle. We see a waiting room full of broken men and a doctor offering false hope. That shift was important, and it has remained a standard way to tell war stories.

Teachers use the story constantly. It is a favorite in high school and college courses because it is short, because its images are concrete, and because it rewards close reading without needing much background. Students are often asked to explain what the major's speech about marriage reveals, or to trace how the opening paragraph prepares the ending.

Within Hemingway's own career, the story belongs to a group of pieces that draw on his Italian experience. He was wounded in July 1918 while serving as a Red Cross ambulance driver near the Piave River, and he recovered in Milan, where he fell in love with a nurse. That experience later fed A Farewell to Arms in 1929, a novel that carries many of the same moods: cold weather, rain, hospitals, and the sense that the world breaks everyone. The story can be read as a small, early version of the same sorrow, written with far less plot.

The title borrows from a line in Christopher Marlowe's play The Jew of Malta, which T. S. Eliot had also used in a poem, so Hemingway was placing his story inside a literary conversation about distance, memory, and the dead.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this story first for its restraint. It is hard to write about grief without sentimentality, and Hemingway does it by showing almost nothing. When the major cries, he is described as carrying himself straight and soldierly, with tears on both cheeks and biting his lips, and that is all. Readers tend to remember that image for years because it refuses to ask for pity. The man's dignity and his collapse exist at the same time.

Many readers also admire the way the story captures the strange mood of recovery. Anyone who has spent time in a hospital, or sat in a waiting room full of people with the same problem, will recognize the false politeness, the small jokes, and the way strangers become companions only because they are in the same place every afternoon. The narrator says there was nothing that held them together except that they met each day at the hospital. That sentence is true of many friendships formed in difficult places, and it is part of why the story feels so humane.

The atmosphere is another favorite. Milan in the fall, with the early dark, the lighted shop windows, the wind coming down from the mountains, the chestnut seller warming her hands over charcoal, gives the story a physical reality that readers often cite. It is a rare piece of fiction that makes you feel cold and warm at once. The detail of the game hanging outside the shops, with snow powdering the foxes, is often admired as an example of how a single image can suggest the whole mood of a book.

Fellow writers have praised its economy. Hemingway's reputation among other authors has been mixed at times, but this story, along with a few others from the same period, is often named as proof that his method really works. Critics who dislike his later bluster and macho posing usually exempt the early stories, which they find more vulnerable and more precise.

Finally, readers love the moral sensitivity of the story. The narrator does not judge the major for his harshness. He listens, he apologizes, he feels sick for him. He also admits his own fear, which is a quiet kind of honesty about courage. The story suggests that bravery is not the absence of fear, and that grief is not a failure of strength, which is a message many readers, especially those who have lost someone, find comforting rather than bleak. There is no lesson announced, but the reader leaves with a sense that being gentle with a hurting person is the right response.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because In Another Country is so admired, it has received less direct criticism than many of Hemingway's longer works, but certain objections come up again and again. The first is the old complaint about his style. Some critics, since the 1920s, have found his short declarative sentences monotonous or mannered, and have accused him of mistaking blankness for depth. According to this view, a story that withholds feeling can sometimes simply withhold it, leaving the reader to supply what the author did not. Parodies of Hemingway's style, which have been popular for decades, make fun of exactly this habit of repeating the word "and" and describing simple actions with solemn seriousness.

Another line of criticism concerns the narrator. Because he is so reserved, some readers feel that the story never lets us into his mind in a way that earns the emotional payoff. We are told he is afraid to die, but we are not shown it dramatically. We are told he is feeling sick for the major, but the story prefers to report this in a sentence. Critics who prize interior life, as in Henry James or Virginia Woolf, have sometimes found the story distant for this reason.

There have also been broader complaints about Hemingway's view of the world. His fiction is often accused of treating suffering as something to be borne in silence, and of valuing a kind of stern stoicism that is not available to everyone. The major's advice that a man must not marry, or must find things he cannot lose, can be read as a bleak and self-protective philosophy, and some critics have wondered whether the story endorses it. The more usual reading is that the story pities the major for believing this, since the reader sees that his grief is exactly the price of love. Still, the ambiguity has bothered some.

Some critics have noted that women in the story are almost entirely absent. The major's wife never appears, and exists only as a loss. The chestnut seller is a background detail. This reflects a larger pattern in Hemingway's early work, in which women are often seen from outside, as objects of desire, comfort, or grief, rather than as people with their own inner lives. A story called Men Without Women, as the collection is titled, invites this criticism almost by design.

Finally, a few readers feel that the story's polish can seem too careful. The details of the walk, the symbolism of the funerals and the dead game, and the neat ending with the photographs can make the story feel designed. Even admirers sometimes admit that the effect is a little controlled. Still, most disagreements are about Hemingway's larger legacy, not about this particular piece.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

In Another Country belongs to the tradition of modernist fiction, the movement in the early twentieth century that broke away from long, explanatory Victorian storytelling in favor of compression, fragments, and hidden meaning. Hemingway learned a great deal in Paris from Gertrude Stein and Ezra Pound, who urged writers to cut the extra words and trust the concrete image. The story is a small triumph of that approach, and it sits alongside the poems of Pound and the early work of other modernists as an example of how little you need.

It also belongs to the great body of literature written after the First World War. That war shocked a generation of writers into new forms. Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front, Wilfred Owen's poems, and Hemingway's own A Farewell to Arms all describe the damage of the war, not its glory. Hemingway's version is distinct because it moves away from the battlefield entirely. In a sense, it is a story about the war's long shadow, the injuries that last, and the loss that follows the soldiers home. The phrase "Lost Generation," which Hemingway used as an epigraph in his novel, connects to this feeling of people who survived but cannot return to what they were.

Going back, the story descends from older traditions of storytelling about stoic endurance, such as the Anglo-Saxon poems about warriors, and from nineteenth century realists such as Anton Chekhov and Stephen Crane, who also let small events carry large feeling. Chekhov in particular is a good comparison. Both writers trust that readers will notice what has been left unsaid. The title's allusion to Marlowe turns a cold Renaissance joke about a dead woman into an elegy.

What descends from it is a whole school of American short fiction. Writers as different as Raymond Carver, Ann Beattie, and many contemporary authors of quiet, domestic stories use some version of its approach, with an unadorned surface and a heavy emotional undertow. War writers keep returning to its model of the hospital and the aftermath.

It matters still because its subject has not gone out of date. Every war produces men and women who come home with bodies and minds that cannot be easily repaired, and the cheerful promises of doctors and governments often ring hollow. The story also speaks to everyone who has lost something dear, because it knows that people respond to loss with anger, with dignity, or with silence, and that none of these is a failure. If you want to understand why Hemingway mattered, begin here.
