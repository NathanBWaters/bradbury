Science and the Savages

G. K. Chesterton (1905) • 13 min read

You cannot understand a ritual or a poem from the outside by counting pebbles; you can only understand it because you share the common human heart.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This essay by G. K. Chesterton appeared in 1905 in his book Heretics, where he took on the prominent ideas of his day. Here his target is the new science of folklore and anthropology, the study of myths, rituals, and so-called primitive peoples. This was the age of scholars who collected customs from around the world and explained them with confident theories about what early humans must have believed. Chesterton, a journalist with a gift for common sense and paradox, thought the scientists were making a basic mistake. They treated human beings like insects, studying them coldly from outside, and so they missed what any ordinary person already knows from the inside: that people do many things, from laying flowers on graves to dancing at balls, without any tidy reason at all. His argument is that to understand human origins, you should look at yourself and your own neighbors. As you listen, notice how funny and how serious he manages to be at once.

A permanent disadvantage of the study of folklore and kindred subjects is that the man of science can hardly be in the nature of things very frequently a man of the world. He is a student of nature; he is scarcely ever a student of human nature. And even where this difficulty is overcome, and he is in some sense a student of human nature, this is only a very faint beginning of the painful progress towards being human. For the study of primitive race and religion stands apart in one important respect from all, or nearly all, the ordinary scientific studies. A man can understand astronomy only by being an astronomer; he can understand entomology only by being an entomologist (or, perhaps, an insect); but he can understand a great deal of anthropology merely by being a man. He is himself the animal which he studies. Hence arises the fact which strikes the eye everywhere in the records of ethnology and folklore⁠—the fact that the same frigid and detached spirit which leads to success in the study of astronomy or botany leads to disaster in the study of mythology or human origins. It is necessary to cease to be a man in order to do justice to a microbe; it is not necessary to cease to be a man in order to do justice to men. That same suppression of sympathies, that same waving away of intuitions or guesswork which make a man preternaturally clever in dealing with the stomach of a spider, will make him preternaturally stupid in dealing with the heart of man. He is making himself inhuman in order to understand humanity. An ignorance of the other world is boasted by many men of science; but in this matter their defect arises, not from ignorance of the other world, but from ignorance of this world. For the secrets about which anthropologists concern themselves can be best learnt, not from books or voyages, but from the ordinary commerce of man with man. The secret of why some savage tribe worships monkeys or the moon is not to be found even by travelling among those savages and taking down their answers in a notebook, although the cleverest man may pursue this course. The answer to the riddle is in England; it is in London; nay, it is in his own heart. When a man has discovered why men in Bond Street wear black hats he will at the same moment have discovered why men in Timbuktu wear red feathers. The mystery in the heart of some savage war-dance should not be studied in books of scientific travel; it should be studied at a subscription ball. If a man desires to find out the origins of religions, let him not go to the Sandwich Islands; let him go to church. If a man wishes to know the origin of human society, to know what society, philosophically speaking, really is, let him not go into the British Museum; let him go into society.

This total misunderstanding of the real nature of ceremonial gives rise to the most awkward and dehumanized versions of the conduct of men in rude lands or ages. The man of science, not realizing that ceremonial is essentially a thing which is done without a reason, has to find a reason for every sort of ceremonial, and, as might be supposed, the reason is generally a very absurd one⁠—absurd because it originates not in the simple mind of the barbarian, but in the sophisticated mind of the professor. The teamed man will say, for instance, “The natives of Mumbojumbo Land believe that the dead man can eat and will require food upon his journey to the other world. This is attested by the fact that they place food in the grave, and that any family not complying with this rite is the object of the anger of the priests and the tribe.” To anyone acquainted with humanity this way of talking is topsy-turvy. It is like saying, “The English in the twentieth century believed that a dead man could smell. This is attested by the fact that they always covered his grave with lilies, violets, or other flowers. Some priestly and tribal terrors were evidently attached to the neglect of this action, as we have records of several old ladies who were very much disturbed in mind because their wreaths had not arrived in time for the funeral.” It may be of course that savages put food with a dead man because they think that a dead man can eat, or weapons with a dead man because they think that a dead man can fight. But personally I do not believe that they think anything of the kind. I believe they put food or weapons on the dead for the same reason that we put flowers, because it is an exceedingly natural and obvious thing to do. We do not understand, it is true, the emotion which makes us think it obvious and natural; but that is because, like all the important emotions of human existence it is essentially irrational. We do not understand the savage for the same reason that the savage does not understand himself. And the savage does not understand himself for the same reason that we do not understand ourselves either.

The obvious truth is that the moment any matter has passed through the human mind it is finally and forever spoilt for all purposes of science. It has become a thing incurably mysterious and infinite; this mortal has put on immortality. Even what we call our material desires are spiritual, because they are human. Science can analyse a pork-chop, and say how much of it is phosphorus and how much is protein; but science cannot analyse any man’s wish for a pork-chop, and say how much of it is hunger, how much custom, how much nervous fancy, how much a haunting love of the beautiful. The man’s desire for the pork-chop remains literally as mystical and ethereal as his desire for heaven. All attempts, therefore, at a science of any human things, at a science of history, a science of folklore, a science of sociology, are by their nature not merely hopeless, but crazy. You can no more be certain in economic history that a man’s desire for money was merely a desire for money than you can be certain in hagiology that a saint’s desire for God was merely a desire for God. And this kind of vagueness in the primary phenomena of the study is an absolutely final blow to anything in the nature of a science. Men can construct a science with very few instruments, or with very plain instruments; but no one on earth could construct a science with unreliable instruments. A man might work out the whole of mathematics with a handful of pebbles, but not with a handful of clay which was always falling apart into new fragments, and falling together into new combinations. A man might measure heaven and earth with a reed, but not with a growing reed.

As one of the enormous follies of folklore, let us take the case of the transmigration of stories, and the alleged unity of their source. Story after story the scientific mythologists have cut out of its place in history, and pinned side by side with similar stories in their museum of fables. The process is industrious, it is fascinating, and the whole of it rests on one of the plainest fallacies in the world. That a story has been told all over the place at some time or other, not only does not prove that it never really happened; it does not even faintly indicate or make slightly more probable that it never happened. That a large number of fishermen have falsely asserted that they have caught a pike two feet long, does not in the least affect the question of whether anyone ever really did so. That numberless journalists announce a Franco-German war merely for money is no evidence one way or the other upon the dark question of whether such a war ever occurred. Doubtless in a few hundred years the innumerable Franco-German wars that did not happen will have cleared the scientific mind of any belief in the legendary war of ’70 which did. But that will be because if folklore students remain at all, their nature will be unchanged; and their services to folklore will be still as they are at present, greater than they know. For in truth these men do something far more godlike than studying legends; they create them.

There are two kinds of stories which the scientists say cannot be true, because everybody tells them. The first class consists of the stories which are told everywhere, because they are somewhat odd or clever; there is nothing in the world to prevent their having happened to somebody as an adventure any more than there is anything to prevent their having occurred, as they certainly did occur, to somebody as an idea. But they are not likely to have happened to many people. The second class of their “myths” consist of the stories that are told everywhere for the simple reason that they happen everywhere. Of the first class, for instance, we might take such an example as the story of William Tell, now generally ranked among legends upon the sole ground that it is found in the tales of other peoples. Now, it is obvious that this was told everywhere because whether true or fictitious it is what is called “a good story;” it is odd, exciting, and it has a climax. But to suggest that some such eccentric incident can never have happened in the whole history of archery, or that it did not happen to any particular person of whom it is told, is stark impudence. The idea of shooting at a mark attached to some valuable or beloved person is an idea doubtless that might easily have occurred to any inventive poet. But it is also an idea that might easily occur to any boastful archer. It might be one of the fantastic caprices of some storyteller. It might equally well be one of the fantastic caprices of some tyrant. It might occur first in real life and afterwards occur in legends. Or it might just as well occur first in legends and afterwards occur in real life. If no apple has ever been shot off a boy’s head from the beginning of the world, it may be done tomorrow morning, and by somebody who has never heard of William Tell.

This type of tale, indeed, may be pretty fairly paralleled with the ordinary anecdote terminating in a repartee or an Irish bull. Such a retort as the famous “Je ne vois pas la necessité” we have all seen attributed to Talleyrand, to Voltaire, to Henri Quatre, to an anonymous judge, and so on. But this variety does not in any way make it more likely that the thing was never said at all. It is highly likely that it was really said by somebody unknown. It is highly likely that it was really said by Talleyrand. In any case, it is not any more difficult to believe that the mot might have occurred to a man in conversation than to a man writing memoirs. It might have occurred to any of the men I have mentioned. But there is this point of distinction about it, that it is not likely to have occurred to all of them. And this is where the first class of so-called myth differs from the second to which I have previously referred. For there is a second class of incident found to be common to the stories of five or six heroes, say to Sigurd, to Hercules, to Rustem, to the Cid, and so on. And the peculiarity of this myth is that not only is it highly reasonable to imagine that it really happened to one hero, but it is highly reasonable to imagine that it really happened to all of them. Such a story, for instance, is that of a great man having his strength swayed or thwarted by the mysterious weakness of a woman. The anecdotal story, the story of William Tell, is as I have said, popular, because it is peculiar. But this kind of story, the story of Samson and Delilah, of Arthur and Guinevere, is obviously popular because it is not peculiar. It is popular as good, quiet fiction is popular, because it tells the truth about people. If the ruin of Samson by a woman, and the ruin of Hercules by a woman, have a common legendary origin, it is gratifying to know that we can also explain, as a fable, the ruin of Nelson by a woman and the ruin of Parnell by a woman. And, indeed, I have no doubt whatever that, some centuries hence, the students of folklore will refuse altogether to believe that Elizabeth Barrett eloped with Robert Browning, and will prove their point up to the hilt by the unquestionable fact that the whole fiction of the period was full of such elopements from end to end.

Possibly the most pathetic of all the delusions of the modern students of primitive belief is the notion they have about the thing they call anthropomorphism. They believe that primitive men attributed phenomena to a god in human form in order to explain them, because his mind in its sullen limitation could not reach any further than his own clownish existence. The thunder was called the voice of a man, the lightning the eyes of a man, because by this explanation they were made more reasonable and comfortable. The final cure for all this kind of philosophy is to walk down a lane at night. Anyone who does so will discover very quickly that men pictured something semi-human at the back of all things, not because such a thought was natural, but because it was supernatural; not because it made things more comprehensible, but because it made them a hundred times more incomprehensible and mysterious. For a man walking down a lane at night can see the conspicuous fact that as long as nature keeps to her own course, she has no power with us at all. As long as a tree is a tree, it is a top-heavy monster with a hundred arms, a thousand tongues, and only one leg. But so long as a tree is a tree, it does not frighten us at all. It begins to be something alien, to be something strange, only when it looks like ourselves. When a tree really looks like a man our knees knock under us. And when the whole universe looks like a man we fall on our faces.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Chesterton begins with a puzzle about method. Most sciences require the scientist to stand apart from the thing studied. You do not need to feel like a star to understand astronomy. But anthropology and folklore are different, because the scientist is himself the animal under study. A man can understand a great deal about humanity merely by being human. So the cold, detached attitude that helps you dissect a spider actively harms you when you try to understand a person. As Chesterton puts it, the scholar is making himself inhuman in order to understand humanity.

From this he draws a famous consequence: the answer to the riddle of why a distant tribe does a strange thing is to be found not in Africa or the Pacific but in London, and in one's own heart. When you have discovered why men in Bond Street wear black hats, you will have discovered why men in Timbuktu wear red feathers. The secrets of a war dance are better studied at a subscription ball. If you want the origin of religion, go to church. If you want to understand society, go into society rather than the British Museum.

The essay's best illustration concerns ceremony. Learned men, he says, assume every ritual must have a stated reason, so they conclude that a tribe puts food in a grave because they believe the dead can eat. Chesterton imagines a future scholar reading that the English of the twentieth century believed a dead man could smell flowers, because they covered his grave with wreaths, and that old ladies were upset when the wreaths arrived late. The joke exposes the mistake. Ceremony is, in his phrase, something done without a reason. People put food or weapons or flowers with the dead because it is an obvious and natural thing to do, and the emotion behind it is deep and hard to explain, even to the person feeling it.

That leads to his boldest claim: that anything that has passed through the human mind becomes forever unsuitable for strict science. Science can analyze a pork chop, but not a man's wish for one. Desire is mystical whether it is for a chop or for heaven. A science of history or folklore, therefore, rests on unreliable instruments, like trying to measure the world with a growing reed.

Finally he attacks a favorite argument of the folklorists: that a story told in many places must be a myth. That many fishermen claim a two-foot pike does not prove no one ever caught one. Some stories spread because they are good, like William Tell, and some because they happen everywhere. Either way, widespread telling proves nothing about whether it happened. The layer a first-time reader may miss is that Chesterton is defending the dignity of ordinary people, savage and civilized alike, against being treated as specimens.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is vintage Chesterton, and it shows both his brilliance and his habit of overreach. Start with what is genuinely excellent. The core insight, that human behavior cannot be understood entirely from outside, is a real one, and anthropology itself eventually absorbed a version of it. The ideas of fieldwork, of taking participants' own understanding seriously, and of avoiding the arrogant assumption that other people's customs are mere mistakes, would all become central to the discipline in the generations after Chesterton wrote. His mockery of the scholar who finds a literal belief behind every gesture hits a genuine weakness of the intellectualist school of the time, which tended to treat ritual as bad science rather than as something else entirely, like expression or habit or feeling.

The craft is delightful. The flowers on the grave, parodied in the language of an ethnographer, are an almost perfect piece of comic argument. It does in a paragraph what a footnote-heavy academic rebuttal might take pages to do: it makes the reader feel the absurdity from the inside. The comparison of Bond Street hats to Timbuktu feathers and the idea of studying war dances at a subscription ball are the kind of lines that make an argument memorable. The pike and the Franco-German war are neat, plain examples that make a logical point without any jargon.

But the essay also has real weaknesses. First, Chesterton slides from a good point to a sweeping one. It is true that human motives are complex, but it does not follow that all attempts at a science of human things are, in his word, crazy. Psychology, economics, linguistics, and later anthropology have managed to say useful and checkable things about people despite the murkiness of motive. His argument about unreliable instruments proves too much: by his own logic, no one could ever learn anything about anyone.

Second, he is unfair to the scholars. The name he gives to the type, with the invented Mumbojumbo Land, is a caricature, and he does not name or quote any actual researcher. Real anthropologists of the period, including some he must have had in mind, were more varied and cautious than his composite. His own method, deciding what savages think by reflecting on how Englishmen feel, can slide into its own kind of armchair guesswork. Telling us confidently what a dead man's relatives really feel is exactly what he accuses the scientists of doing.

Third, the use of the word savages, normal for 1905, is jarring today, though the essay's actual thrust is to insist on a shared humanity. The strongest reading treats it as a funny, flawed, but largely generous polemic: right about the danger of cold method, too eager to wave away method altogether.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be an exaggeration to say this essay changed the course of anthropology. Chesterton was a journalist and a literary man, and professional scholars of the time were unlikely to alter their methods because of a witty chapter in a book of essays. But the essay is part of a larger story in which the intellectualist style of early anthropology, associated with figures like Edward Burnett Tylor and James Frazer, the author of The Golden Bough, was gradually replaced by approaches that paid more attention to practice, context, and the meaning of ritual to those who perform it. Chesterton's complaint belongs to the early rumblings of that shift, and later readers have sometimes pointed to it as a surprisingly prescient layman's objection.

The essay is also one chapter in the book Heretics, published in 1905, which had a very concrete effect on Chesterton's career. Heretics was a collection of attacks on fashionable thinkers, and it provoked a challenge to state his own beliefs. That challenge led to Orthodoxy, published in 1908, one of the most widely read works of Christian apologetics in English. In that sense, the folklore essay belongs to the foundations of Chesterton's mature thought. Its idea that ritual and ceremony are natural, and older than explanations, reappears in his writing on religion, and his insistence that old stories may preserve true memories fed his later book The Everlasting Man in 1925, which argued that the story of Christianity should be read as something more than one myth among many. C. S. Lewis later credited The Everlasting Man as an important step on his own road to faith.

His point that a story's popularity does not prove it false has been picked up by writers who defend the historical value of folk tradition and legend, and it remains a useful reminder in any discussion of whether widely repeated stories can have a factual core. Writers on myth and religion who wanted to resist reducing belief to primitive error have sometimes found him a lively ally.

In teaching, the essay appears in anthologies of Chesterton and in discussions of Edwardian intellectual life, sometimes alongside the work of the scholars he mocked, as a comic counterweight. Students of the history of social science may meet it as an example of how outsiders criticized the new disciplines. Its direct influence is modest, but its spirit, the insistence that human things must be understood with human sympathy, has become a commonplace of the humanities. There is a lasting echo in the modern phrase that you cannot study people as though they were rocks. Chesterton said it first in his own way, with hats and feathers and a funeral wreath that came too late.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers most enjoy about this essay is its sheer cleverness, and the delight of watching a very learned subject get gently deflated. Folklore and anthropology in 1905 carried an air of solemn authority. Chesterton punctures that authority not with rage but with a funeral wreath. The passage in which he rewrites an English burial as though it were a primitive custom, with old ladies distressed about wreaths that failed to arrive on time, is the part people remember. It is funny, but it also changes how you see the original claim. Once you have imagined an anthropologist from the far future treating your flowers as proof that you thought the dead could smell, you can no longer take such explanations at face value.

Readers also love the essay's warmth. It makes a case that people everywhere are in essential ways the same, and that what puzzles us about distant peoples is what puzzles us about ourselves. That is a generous, democratic idea, and it is stated with real affection. The line about studying the war dance at a subscription ball is almost tender: it invites us to see our own social rituals, the dances and hats and church services, as strange and meaningful rather than normal and obvious.

There is also a pleasure in the argument's plain logic. The pike story is one of the best small arguments in the essay. Anyone can follow it, and it exposes a real fallacy: that because many people tell a tale, it must be false. Readers who have no interest in folklore still appreciate a clean, memorable demonstration of a logical error.

Writers have admired Chesterton's gift for making abstract points concrete. Authors such as Borges and Marshall McLuhan admired his habit of turning ideas into pictures, and Chesterton's defenders often point to this essay as an example of a layman who sees something the experts have temporarily forgotten. Many modern readers, including those who find his politics or religion unattractive, still enjoy the shrewdness of his observation that experts can lose touch with what everyone knows.

Finally, there is the pleasure of an essay that trusts the reader. It does not ask us to master a theory. It asks us to consult our own experience of why we do the odd things we do. Anyone who has ever put flowers on a grave, or worn a ridiculous hat to a wedding, knows without being told that not all behavior has a reason. That sense of recognition is the essay's quiet reward.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most serious objection is that Chesterton attacks the idea of a human science far too broadly. Social scientists, historians, and philosophers of method have long pointed out that complexity of motive does not make study impossible. We can measure, compare, and test, even if we cannot reduce a person to a formula. His claim that any science of human things is crazy by its nature would rule out not only bad anthropology but good history, careful psychology, and the very comparisons of cultures that have revealed patterns he would no doubt find interesting. Many modern readers find this the weakest part of the essay: a witty rhetorical flourish presented as a logical conclusion.

A second objection is that he is unfair to the scholars, and that the unfairness is built into the method. By inventing a composite professor and a made-up tribe, Mumbojumbo Land, he can write the opponent's lines himself. Actual researchers of the time disagreed with one another, and some, such as those who emphasized fieldwork, would later make the same point he does. Critics have noted that his own account of what primitive people really think rests on introspection and common sense, which is exactly the kind of armchair confidence he mocks in others.

Third, there is the matter of his defense of folk stories. His argument that a widely told story is not made false by being widely told is logically sound, but he goes on to suggest that many such stories are simply true, or that scholars create legends rather than study them. Scholars of folklore have replied that comparison of tale types is not meant to prove falsehood but to trace how stories travel and change, and that Chesterton's treatment is a partial misreading of what they were doing. He is attacking a crude version of the comparative method that more careful scholars did not hold.

Fourth, the language of the essay raises problems for contemporary readers. Words like savage and primitive, standard in the period, now carry baggage. Though Chesterton's purpose is to stress shared humanity, some critics argue that he still treats distant peoples as a kind of mirror for English life rather than as subjects with their own histories. The essay uses the savage as a rhetorical device, a figure for whatever is irrational in all of us, rather than looking at specific societies.

Finally, his religious commitments sit behind the argument. Skeptical readers have suspected that part of his hostility to the science of religion is that it threatens belief. That suspicion does not refute his points, but it colors how the essay is read.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Chesterton's essay stands at a crossroads. On one side is the nineteenth-century project of comparative mythology and anthropology, with its grand evolutionary schemes in which societies passed from savagery through barbarism to civilization, and in which religion was a kind of early, mistaken science. Tylor and Frazer are the great names. Chesterton is in the loud, popular opposition. On the other side are the later schools that would transform the field: Franz Boas and his students, who stressed cultural context and fieldwork, and Bronisław Malinowski, who lived among the Trobriand Islanders and argued that customs had to be understood by how they functioned in people's lives. In a loose way, Chesterton's insistence that you must grasp the human meaning of a ritual, rather than hunting for a literal belief behind it, points toward those later developments, though he would not have recognized himself as their ancestor.

The essay is also an early entry in a long line of humanist complaints against scientism, the belief that scientific method can and should explain everything. Its relatives include Wilhelm Dilthey's distinction between explaining nature and understanding human life, and the later argument by philosophers of social science that interpreting meaning differs from measuring causes. Chesterton makes the point as a comic essayist rather than a philosopher, but it is the same family of ideas.

Within his own work, the essay anticipates The Everlasting Man, in which he argues that the human being is something strange and unique that cannot be fully explained by tracing animal origins, and that religion cannot be understood by treating it as a stage of development. It also fits with his love of tradition, since the instinct to leave flowers on a grave is a piece of inherited custom that he considers wiser than the theories explaining it away.

Comparisons are instructive. Where Frazer's The Golden Bough builds a majestic structure out of parallels across cultures, Chesterton is the voice insisting that parallels are not explanations. Where later anthropologists would adopt the language of cultural relativism, Chesterton's version is more like a defense of common human nature. You might also compare him to George Orwell, who in another register warned against clever intellectuals who lose touch with ordinary experience.

Why read it now? Because the underlying warning is still relevant. Whenever an expert explains away a human feeling with a neat theory, Chesterton's question applies: have you consulted the experience you are explaining? It is also simply a very entertaining piece of writing, and a reminder that the best way to understand strangers is to remember how strange we are to ourselves.
