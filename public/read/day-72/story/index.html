A Chameleon

Anton Chekhov (1884) • 6 min read

“So you bite, you damned brute?” Otchumyelov hears suddenly. “Lads, don’t let him go! Biting is prohibited nowadays! Hold him! ah⁠ ⁠… ah!”

Key Context & Why It Matters

A Chameleon is a comic sketch from 1884, written when Anton Chekhov was twenty-four, still a medical student and then a young doctor, writing funny pieces for humor magazines under the pen name Antosha Chekhonte. It runs only a few pages, but it has lasted because its target is so durable. In a dusty Russian market square, a police superintendent has to decide what to do about a dog that has bitten a man's finger. The facts never change, yet the verdict keeps changing, depending on a single question: who owns the dog? Russia in those years was ruled by a rigid ladder of rank, in which a petty official's judgment could depend less on right and wrong than on who stood above him. Chekhov turns that servile habit into a farce. Watch the superintendent's overcoat as you listen. The title tells you the animal that matters, and it is not the dog.

The police superintendent Otchumyelov is walking across the market square wearing a new overcoat and carrying a parcel under his arm. A red-haired policeman strides after him with a sieve full of confiscated gooseberries in his hands. There is silence all around. Not a soul in the square.⁠ ⁠… The open doors of the shops and taverns look out upon God’s world disconsolately, like hungry mouths; there is not even a beggar near them.

“So you bite, you damned brute?” Otchumyelov hears suddenly. “Lads, don’t let him go! Biting is prohibited nowadays! Hold him! ah⁠ ⁠… ah!”

There is the sound of a dog yelping. Otchumyelov looks in the direction of the sound and sees a dog, hopping on three legs and looking about her, run out of Pitchugin’s timber-yard. A man in a starched cotton shirt, with his waistcoat unbuttoned, is chasing her. He runs after her, and throwing his body forward falls down and seizes the dog by her hind legs. Once more there is a yelping and a shout of “Don’t let go!” Sleepy countenances are protruded from the shops, and soon a crowd, which seems to have sprung out of the earth, is gathered round the timber-yard.

“It looks like a row, your honour⁠ ⁠…” says the policeman.

Otchumyelov makes a half turn to the left and strides towards the crowd.

He sees the aforementioned man in the unbuttoned waistcoat standing close by the gate of the timber-yard, holding his right hand in the air and displaying a bleeding finger to the crowd. On his half-drunken face there is plainly written: “I’ll pay you out, you rogue!” and indeed the very finger has the look of a flag of victory. In this man Otchumyelov recognises Hryukin, the goldsmith. The culprit who has caused the sensation, a white borzoy puppy with a sharp muzzle and a yellow patch on her back, is sitting on the ground with her forepaws outstretched in the middle of the crowd, trembling all over. There is an expression of misery and terror in her tearful eyes.

“What’s it all about?” Otchumyelov inquires, pushing his way through the crowd. “What are you here for? Why are you waving your finger⁠ ⁠… ? Who was it shouted?”

“I was walking along here, not interfering with anyone, your honour,” Hryukin begins, coughing into his fist. “I was talking about firewood to Mitry Mitritch, when this low brute for no rhyme or reason bit my finger.⁠ ⁠… You must excuse me, I am a working man.⁠ ⁠… Mine is fine work. I must have damages, for I shan’t be able to use this finger for a week, may be.⁠ ⁠… It’s not even the law, your honour, that one should put up with it from a beast.⁠ ⁠… If everyone is going to be bitten, life won’t be worth living.⁠ ⁠…”

“H’m. Very good,” says Otchumyelov sternly, coughing and raising his eyebrows. “Very good. Whose dog is it? I won’t let this pass! I’ll teach them to let their dogs run all over the place! It’s time these gentry were looked after, if they won’t obey the regulations! When he’s fined, the blackguard, I’ll teach him what it means to keep dogs and such stray cattle! I’ll give him a lesson!⁠ ⁠… Yeldyrin,” cries the superintendent, addressing the policeman, “find out whose dog this is and draw up a report! And the dog must be strangled. Without delay! It’s sure to be mad.⁠ ⁠… Whose dog is it, I ask?”

“I fancy it’s General Zhigalov’s,” says someone in the crowd.

“General Zhigalov’s, h’m.⁠ ⁠… Help me off with my coat, Yeldyrin⁠ ⁠… it’s frightfully hot! It must be a sign of rain.⁠ ⁠… There’s one thing I can’t make out, how it came to bite you?” Otchumyelov turns to Hryukin. “Surely it couldn’t reach your finger. It’s a little dog, and you are a great hulking fellow! You must have scratched your finger with a nail, and then the idea struck you to get damages for it. We all know⁠ ⁠… your sort! I know you devils!”

“He put a cigarette in her face, your honour, for a joke, and she had the sense to snap at him.⁠ ⁠… He is a nonsensical fellow, your honour!”

“That’s a lie, Squinteye! You didn’t see, so why tell lies about it? His honour is a wise gentleman, and will see who is telling lies and who is telling the truth, as in God’s sight.⁠ ⁠… And if I am lying let the court decide. It’s written in the law.⁠ ⁠… We are all equal nowadays. My own brother is in the gendarmes⁠ ⁠… let me tell you.⁠ ⁠…”

“Don’t argue!”

“No, that’s not the General’s dog,” says the policeman, with profound conviction, “the General hasn’t got one like that. His are mostly setters.”

“Do you know that for a fact?”

“Yes, your honour.”

“I know it, too. The General has valuable dogs, thoroughbred, and this is goodness knows what! No coat, no shape.⁠ ⁠… A low creature. And to keep a dog like that!⁠ ⁠… where’s the sense of it. If a dog like that were to turn up in Petersburg or Moscow, do you know what would happen? They would not worry about the law, they would strangle it in a twinkling! You’ve been injured, Hryukin, and we can’t let the matter drop.⁠ ⁠… We must give them a lesson! It is high time.⁠ ⁠… !”

“Yet maybe it is the General’s,” says the policeman, thinking aloud. “It’s not written on its face.⁠ ⁠… I saw one like it the other day in his yard.”

“It is the General’s, that’s certain!” says a voice in the crowd.

“H’m, help me on with my overcoat, Yeldyrin, my lad⁠ ⁠… the wind’s getting up.⁠ ⁠… I am cold.⁠ ⁠… You take it to the General’s, and inquire there. Say I found it and sent it. And tell them not to let it out into the street.⁠ ⁠… It may be a valuable dog, and if every swine goes sticking a cigar in its mouth, it will soon be ruined. A dog is a delicate animal.⁠ ⁠… And you put your hand down, you blockhead. It’s no use your displaying your fool of a finger. It’s your own fault.⁠ ⁠…”

“Here comes the General’s cook, ask him⁠ ⁠… Hi, Prohor! Come here, my dear man! Look at this dog.⁠ ⁠… Is it one of yours?”

“What an idea! We have never had one like that!”

“There’s no need to waste time asking,” says Otchumyelov. “It’s a stray dog! There’s no need to waste time talking about it.⁠ ⁠… Since he says it’s a stray dog, a stray dog it is.⁠ ⁠… It must be destroyed, that’s all about it.”

“It is not our dog,” Prohor goes on. “It belongs to the General’s brother, who arrived the other day. Our master does not care for hounds. But his honour is fond of them.⁠ ⁠…”

“You don’t say his Excellency’s brother is here? Vladimir Ivanitch?” inquires Otchumyelov, and his whole face beams with an ecstatic smile. “Well, I never! And I didn’t know! Has he come on a visit?”

“Yes.”

“Well, I never.⁠ ⁠… He couldn’t stay away from his brother.⁠ ⁠… And there I didn’t know! So this is his honour’s dog? Delighted to hear it.⁠ ⁠… Take it. It’s not a bad pup.⁠ ⁠… A lively creature.⁠ ⁠… Snapped at this fellow’s finger! Ha-ha-ha.⁠ ⁠… Come, why are you shivering? Rrr⁠ ⁠… Rrrr.⁠ ⁠… The rogue’s angry⁠ ⁠… a nice little pup.”

Prohor calls the dog, and walks away from the timber-yard with her. The crowd laughs at Hryukin.

“I’ll make you smart yet!” Otchumyelov threatens him, and wrapping himself in his greatcoat, goes on his way across the square.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story takes place in a single scene, in a single place, in perhaps a quarter of an hour. Police Superintendent Otchumyelov crosses a quiet market square in a new overcoat. A policeman follows him carrying a sieve of confiscated gooseberries. Suddenly there is a commotion: a goldsmith named Hryukin is holding up a bleeding finger and shouting that a dog has bitten him. A crowd gathers. The superintendent takes charge, and at first his judgment is swift and stern. Whoever owns the dog will pay, and the dog, if it is a stray, will be destroyed.

Then a voice in the crowd says the dog looks like General Zhigalov's. The superintendent's tone reverses at once. Perhaps the man provoked the dog. Perhaps he stuck a cigar in its mouth. Look at the poor little creature, and look at that fool's finger. Then someone says no, the General has no such dog. Back swings the verdict: a wretched, mangy brute, to be destroyed. Then the policeman thinks he saw one like it in the General's yard. Around and around it goes, with the superintendent calling for his coat to be taken off when he feels hot with righteous anger toward the dog's owner, and put back on when the dog is revealed to belong to someone powerful, and he feels a chill of fear. At last the General's cook arrives and says it belongs to the General's brother, a visitor. At that, the superintendent is all smiles. A lively pup. A nice little thing. And the goldsmith, the actual victim, is threatened for his trouble.

The meaning is not about dogs. It is about how authority behaves when it has no principle of its own. Otchumyelov does not weigh evidence. He has an inner weathervane that points toward power. Right and wrong are whatever the ranks above him would prefer. The overcoat is the visible sign of this: the coat goes on and off like a mood, and in the final line he wraps himself in it again and walks on, unembarrassed. The man who, a moment before, threatened to have a dog strangled now melts into tenderness, and neither the crowd nor he himself seems troubled by the contradiction.

A layer worth noticing is the role of the crowd. The bystanders are not neutral. Each shout from the crowd is a small push on the weathervane, and each is delivered with the same confidence, whether it helps the victim or the dog. They laugh at the goldsmith at the end. The story hints that the superintendent is not a lone monster but the natural product of a society where everyone watches the person above and nobody asks what is fair. The name itself is a joke on this: Otchumyelov echoes a Russian word for being addled or going mad, and Hryukin's name echoes a grunting sound, as in a pig's. Chekhov names his characters like the comic types of old stage farce, and then lets them turn out to be disturbingly real.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As pure comic machinery, A Chameleon is superb. The whole story is built on a single repeated gesture, a reversal, and Chekhov is wise enough to repeat it exactly the right number of times: about five. Any fewer and the pattern would not register; any more and it would turn tiresome. Each flip is triggered by a fresh scrap of information, usually a throwaway line from a bystander or the policeman, which keeps the reversals believable and quick. The overcoat adds a physical rhythm to the verbal one, so the reader sees the switch as well as hears it. This is the work of a young writer who understood comedy as timing.

The prose is also remarkably lean. The opening sentences establish the empty square, the open shop doors that look "like hungry mouths," the sieve of gooseberries, and the silence, and then the first shout breaks it. There is almost no description after that, only dialogue and the superintendent's reactions. That economy is partly a product of the circumstances: Chekhov was writing for humor magazines that wanted very short pieces, and he learned to deliver a whole world in a few hundred words. He took the constraint and made it a style.

Where is the weakness? The characters are types. Otchumyelov is a satirical figure, a puffed-up tyrant who is also a coward, and we learn nothing of his life outside the incident. The goldsmith Hryukin is merely a victim, and the crowd is a chorus. The story has no interest in inwardness, and so it cannot offer the surprise that comes when a character turns out to contain more than we expected. For readers who love Chekhov for his psychological subtlety, this early work can feel like a clever sketch rather than a story.

The satire can also seem a little blunt. The dog is a nearly perfect device, since it cannot speak and is entirely at the mercy of the humans' shifting judgment, but the point is made so explicitly, with such constant shouting about rank and the General, that the sly reader may feel nudged. A subtler writer, which Chekhov would later become, might have let one reversal go unremarked. And the ending, with the superintendent threatening the victim after all, is a tidy button on the joke. It lands, but it is the kind of ending a comic writer reaches for, a final punishing twist.

Even so, the story pays back its small ambitions in full. It does exactly one thing, does it in about a thousand words, and does it so cleanly that the title has entered the language as a stock description of a person who changes colors to suit the room. There is real virtue in a perfectly made small thing, and this is one.

III. The Impact It Had

A Chameleon was published in 1884 in a humor magazine of the time, the St. Petersburg weekly Oskolki, which means Fragments, one of the outlets that paid Chekhov a few kopecks a line. He wrote hundreds of such pieces in his twenties. Most are forgotten, and a few, including this one, became classics. It belongs to the early period that made him a known name in Russia before he turned to his more serious stories and plays, and it demonstrates how much craft he had already acquired.

Its most lasting impact is linguistic and cultural. In Russian, the word for chameleon, and the story's title, became shorthand for a turncoat, a person who adapts shamelessly to whoever holds power. Russian readers use the word without needing to have read the story. Otchumyelov himself became a reference figure: a type of petty official who bends with the wind. In the Soviet period the story was widely taught in school, and its portrait of a servile bureaucrat could be read in more than one way, as a critique of the old tsarist order but also, for the attentive, as a description of timeservers under any regime.

The story also contributed to a particular strain of Russian satire, the tradition of mocking small officials and their absurd hierarchies, which runs from Gogol's The Government Inspector and The Overcoat through Saltykov-Shchedrin to Chekhov's own The Death of a Government Clerk, and onward to twentieth-century satirists such as Zoshchenko and, in a darker register, Bulgakov. It helped establish the idea that the real subject of satire is not villainy but obsequiousness, the small, constant, ordinary bending of the spine.

It has been adapted many times in Russia, for stage, film, and television, and is frequently performed as a short theatrical piece, because its single-scene structure and strong physical business, the overcoat on and off, are made for actors. It has also been animated, and the dog is a popular role. Outside Russia, it is a standard item in anthologies of Chekhov's early comic stories and is used in classrooms as a model of how a story can be built on a single repeated reversal.

For Chekhov's career, the story is a marker of his apprenticeship. He later said, in a number of letters, that he wrote his early comic pieces fast and sometimes carelessly. But A Chameleon shows him already in command of a method he would never abandon: take an ordinary event, strip away commentary, and let the behavior of the characters reveal everything. The humorous sketches taught him compression, and compression is the foundation of his mature style.

IV. What Readers Loved

The great pleasure of A Chameleon is recognition. Almost everyone has met an Otchumyelov: the manager who agrees with whoever spoke last, the official who is a bully to the weak and a pushover to the strong, the colleague whose opinion changes the moment the boss walks in. Readers across languages and generations have laughed at the story because they can attach a face to the type, often more than one. That is why the title has traveled so well. The word chameleon already hints at the idea, and the story fills it with a vivid example.

Readers also love how physical the comedy is. The superintendent's coat, which he wants off because he is hot with anger and wants on because he is suddenly cold with worry, is a perfect comic prop. It makes an invisible moral shift visible. Audiences who hear the story read aloud, which is how many Russians first met it, often laugh at each flip, because Chekhov arranges the rhythm so well. The bystanders' interjections feel like a chorus in a farce, and the final image of the crowd laughing at the poor goldsmith adds a bitter edge to the laughter that many readers find irresistible.

Another thing admirers prize is the story's tact. Chekhov never says that the system is corrupt or that servility is a national disease. He never has a narrator lecture. He simply sets up the situation and lets it run, trusting that the reader will recognize the truth. That trust is part of what fellow writers admire. Chekhov's later insistence that a writer should not be a judge but a witness is already visible here in embryo.

Younger readers often love it for a more basic reason: it is short, funny, and easy to follow. It makes a good introduction to Chekhov because there is no need for background about nineteenth-century Russia beyond the most basic idea of rank and deference. A reader who finishes it in five minutes has understood the whole thing and can still go back for the finer details, such as the way the overcoat motif frames the story or the careful placement of the gooseberries in the opening.

There is also the dog. Readers who care about animals often note, with some affection, that the little dog is the one character with entirely consistent behavior. She bites when bothered, she shivers when frightened, and she goes back to her owners. Every human in the story changes color except her. It is a small joke inside the large one, and it is part of why the story feels warm even as it mocks. The satire is aimed at human weakness, and it is delivered with a smile rather than with contempt.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because A Chameleon is a short comic sketch from the start of Chekhov's career, it has attracted less critical argument than his mature work, and it would be dishonest to pretend there is a large body of famous attacks on it. What exists is a set of reservations that readers and critics have voiced over time.

The first is that the story is thin. Compared with the great later works, it offers a single joke and a single target. Critics who prize Chekhov for his ambiguity, his refusal to settle on a verdict, sometimes treat his early humorous pieces as juvenilia, clever but lacking the weight that makes his later fiction astonishing. In the late nineteenth century, some Russian critics also complained, more generally, that Chekhov seemed to lack a clear social position or message, and that his stories were mere impressions. A Chameleon, with its sharp point, escaped that charge, but it did not escape the sense that such pieces were light entertainment.

The second reservation is about the satire's bluntness. The characters are broad. The superintendent is almost a cartoon, and the story's punch depends on that exaggeration. A reader who wants social satire to feel rooted in real, complicated people may find it too schematic. It makes its point by caricature, and caricature is a form some critics regard as limited, since it flatters the reader into feeling superior to the target. In the story, we are invited to laugh at Otchumyelov, but we are less often invited to see ourselves in him, though the story does gesture in that direction.

A third line of criticism involves the political readings that have been laid on the story. In the Soviet era it was sometimes taught as a straightforward denunciation of tsarist bureaucrats, and later commentators have pointed out that this reading flattens Chekhov's more general interest in human weakness. Chekhov did not write a protest piece. Reading him as a social crusader can miss the fact that servility is portrayed as a timeless human habit rather than the product of a single regime.

Finally, a few readers and translators have remarked on how much of the comedy depends on Russian ranks, titles, and forms of address, which can be hard to convey in English. Names like Otchumyelov and Hryukin carry comic sounds that do not survive translation, and the system of deference can seem remote. That is not a flaw in the story so much as a limit on how much of it travels, but it is a real difference between how Russian readers and foreign readers experience it.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

A Chameleon sits in the long tradition of satire about officialdom, and in Russia that tradition is unusually rich. Gogol's play The Government Inspector, from 1836, shows an entire town of officials groveling before a man they mistake for an important visitor. His story The Overcoat gives us a clerk crushed by the ranks above him. Chekhov borrows that sensibility but trims away the fantasy and the grandeur. Where Gogol builds elaborate comic worlds, Chekhov gives you a single square and a single dog. In this sense the story is a miniature, a distilled version of a tradition.

It also has a relative in Chekhov's own sketches of the same period, such as The Death of a Government Clerk, in which a man dies from the embarrassment of having sneezed on a general, and Fat and Thin, in which two old schoolmates' friendship collapses the moment one learns the other's rank. All of these treat the same disease: the way hierarchy distorts human feeling. A Chameleon is the purest example because it has no sentiment in it at all. There is no pathos, only the machinery.

Looking forward, the story's descendants are numerous. Any later fiction that mocks the petty tyrant who tailors his judgments to the powerful owes something to this model. You can see echoes in the satire of Zoshchenko, in the bureaucratic comedy of Czech and Polish writers, and in English-language works such as Orwell's depictions of servility. In a different register, the figure of the opportunist who changes sides to suit the hour is a staple of political fiction, and Otchumyelov is an early, memorable portrait.

The story also connects to the idea of the chameleon in literature generally, which reaches back to antiquity. The animal that changes color to suit its surroundings has long stood for flattery and inconstancy. Chekhov's achievement is to make the metaphor concrete by letting us watch a man do it in real time, and to attach it to a specific social disease, deference to power.

Why read it now? Because the behavior it describes is not historical. Anywhere there is a hierarchy, there is someone adjusting his judgment according to who owns the dog. The story is also a good lesson in how comedy can carry moral observation without sermonizing, and it takes only a few minutes. It reminds us that the question of who is wrong often gets replaced, in institutions and in ordinary life, by the question of who is important, and that the replacement is often invisible to the person making it. Chekhov makes it visible by giving him an overcoat.
