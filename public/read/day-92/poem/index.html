Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land

James Joyce (1907) • 3 min read

Thundering out of the sea in black armour with whirling whips and plunging horses, a nightmare army of abandonment leaves the sleeper crying out in despair.

Key Context & Why It Matters

James Joyce is famous for enormous, difficult novels, Ulysses and Finnegans Wake, but he began as a lyric poet, and this twelve-line poem is the best of his early verse. He wrote it in the first years of the twentieth century as a young man in Dublin and Paris. It was the final poem in his first book, Chamber Music, which came out in 1907. The collection is a sequence of delicate love songs in the manner of Elizabethan lute music and the Irish poets of the Celtic Revival, and most of it is gentle. This poem is the exception. It ends the book with a storm of horses and chariots out of a nightmare. In 1913 the American poet Ezra Pound, then gathering the new poets he thought mattered, chose it for his Imagist anthology, which brought Joyce to the attention of the modern literary world. Later the composer Samuel Barber set it as a song. It is a poem about fear, grief and loneliness, told through a sound you can almost hear.

I hear an army charging upon the land,

And the thunder of horses plunging, foam about their knees:

Arrogant, in black armour, behind them stand,

Disdaining the reins, with fluttering whips, the charioteers.

They cry unto the night their battle-name:

I moan in sleep when I hear afar their whirling laughter.

They cleave the gloom of dreams, a blinding flame,

Clanging, clanging upon the heart as upon an anvil.

They come shaking in triumph their long, green hair:

They come out of the sea and run shouting by the shore.

My heart, have you no wisdom thus to despair?

My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem begins with something enormous and frightening. The speaker hears an army charging across the land, with thundering horses "plunging, foam about their knees," and behind them proud charioteers in black armor, who ignore the reins and crack their whips. They shout their battle-name into the night. The speaker, who seems to be lying in bed, "moan[s] in sleep" at the sound of their "whirling laughter." The invasion cuts through "the gloom of dreams" and rings on the heart "as upon an anvil."

So what is this army? Joyce never says, and the poem gets its power from that silence. The most important clue comes in the third stanza. The riders come "out of the sea," with "long, green hair," and they run shouting along the shore. Horses, foam, sea, shaking hair: it is hard not to think of ocean waves, which in old poetry are often pictured as white horses. In that reading the army is the sea itself, rolling in at night, and the speaker is a person on a shore listening to it crash. Others read it as the invasion of grief or desire, the way a dream can overwhelm you. Still others read it as a literary echo of Irish legends about sea-riding gods and heroes. None of these readings cancels the others.

The final two lines are where the poem turns, and where the meaning becomes clear. After eleven lines of noise, the speaker suddenly addresses his own heart: "My heart, have you no wisdom thus to despair?" Then comes the real cry, "My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?" The charging army, we realize, is the outer form of an inner state. The speaker is not really afraid of soldiers. He is afraid of being alone. The roar of the world, in sleep and in the dark, is what loneliness sounds like once someone you love is gone.

It helps to know that this collection of poems is arranged as a kind of love story, moving from hopeful courtship to loss. This is the last poem, so the "love" who has left is the end point of the whole sequence. Joyce also lost his mother in 1903, around the time he was writing these early poems, and some readers hear personal grief behind the cry. That is a reasonable guess, though the poem itself never gives a name or an event.

What a first-time reader might miss is how much is going on in the sound. The poem is built to be heard: the repeated words, "clanging, clanging," the long, rolling lines, and the way the final line breaks the pattern by suddenly becoming simple. The army is loud, and the loneliness is plain and very quiet. The contrast is the poem's whole point.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a small poem with a big effect, and most of what makes it work is Joyce's ear. He was a trained singer with a fine tenor voice, and he writes here almost like a composer. The lines are long and sweeping, built on a heavy, galloping beat, with sounds that pile up as if the poem itself were charging at you. "And the thunder of horses plunging, foam about their knees" slows down and speeds up in a way that mimics the animals. "Clanging, clanging upon the heart as upon an anvil" repeats a word so that you hear the blows. Few early poems by anyone use sound this well to create a physical sensation.

The structure is smart too. Three four-line stanzas build in intensity, first the sight, then the sound and the dream, then the sea and the despair, and the poem saves its simplest line for last. After all the elaborate description, "My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?" arrives like a child's cry. The sudden drop from epic grandeur to bare feeling is very effective, and it recasts everything that came before. This is the kind of turn that makes a short poem feel larger than it is.

The rhyme is subtle. Joyce rhymes loosely, so that "land" answers "stand" and "name" answers "flame," while other lines, like "knees" and "charioteers," chime without matching exactly. This gives the poem an unsettled quality that fits a nightmare.

It is not flawless. A skeptical reader could point to some borrowed furniture. "Arrogant, in black armour," "charioteers," "whirling laughter," "long, green hair" are romantic props, and the whole thing has a slight stagey, theatrical feel. The language is a little stiff in places, with archaisms like "unto" that come from the Victorian and Celtic Revival poets Joyce was trying to move beyond. Some readers find the poem more impressive in its sound than in its thought, a gorgeous noise that does not quite say what it is about. And the final turn, however moving, is also a little conventional: a lover's abandonment is the oldest subject in lyric poetry.

Joyce himself seems to have thought less of his early verse as time went on. Later in life he wrote more playfully and bitterly about poetry, and Chamber Music is often treated as an apprenticeship. Even so, this one poem stands out from its fellows. It is the only one in the book that suggests the hunger and violence in his later work. Where the other poems are pretty, this one has danger. It succeeds because it breaks the book's own rules, and because the tension between its enormous noise and its tiny, aching last line feels honest. As a miniature, it is hard to improve.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's most important effect was on Joyce's career. In 1913, Ezra Pound, who was then championing new poetry in London, asked Joyce for a poem for a collection of the new Imagist poets. Pound picked this one. It appeared in the anthology Des Imagistes in 1914, alongside work by Pound himself, H.D., Richard Aldington and others. This put Joyce, then a struggling language teacher in Trieste with a manuscript of Dubliners that publishers kept refusing, in front of an international audience of the avant-garde. It also started a relationship that mattered enormously. Pound became one of Joyce's most important champions and helped get A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man serialized in a magazine and published.

The poem's link to Imagism is a little curious, since Joyce was not really an Imagist. The Imagists wanted hard, clear, concrete poems, free of ornament. This poem is more ornate and rhythmic than that. But Pound loved its musical drive and its vivid, direct picture, and it fit his broader campaign to find poets who cared about craft.

For the wider world, its biggest impact has been through music. The American composer Samuel Barber set it as a song for voice and piano in the 1930s, as part of a set of three Joyce settings. Barber's version, with its stormy piano, is one of the best-known American art songs, and it has been recorded by many singers. It has helped keep Joyce's lines alive among people who may never read Chamber Music. In fact, Joyce's lyrics have proved unusually attractive to composers; many have set poems from the book, and Joyce, a singer, would have been pleased.

Within Joyce's own work, the poem is something of a seed. The sea, which appears here as a threatening army, becomes one of the central images of Ulysses, where Stephen Dedalus meditates on the water as a force that holds history and death. The sense of a mind beset by voices and noise, unable to sleep, anticipates the dream-language of Finnegans Wake. The theme of abandonment and a voice calling across distance runs through his short story "The Dead."

Teachers use the poem as a gateway to Joyce, showing students that the man behind the notorious novels began as a careful, musical lyricist. It is also often used as an example of how sound can carry meaning in poetry, and of how a single closing line can transform what has come before. It does not have the fame of the great anthologies' best-known pieces, but among readers who know Joyce, it is the early poem they remember.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing admirers point to is the music. This is a poem you want to read aloud, and doing so is a physical pleasure. The pounding, galloping rhythm and the repeated, ringing words give it an almost hypnotic quality. Even people who find the imagery puzzling often say they can feel what the poem is doing before they can explain it. That is a high compliment for a short lyric.

Readers also love the way it handles fear. Many of us know the experience of lying awake while some unnamed dread seems to press in from outside. Joyce catches this perfectly: the sense of something huge, proud and cruel approaching in the dark, and the helplessness of being half asleep and unable to respond. The poem does not explain the dread, and that is its truthfulness. Fear in the night rarely comes with an explanation.

Then there is the ending, which many readers say is the reason they remember the poem at all. The speaker has been describing a spectacular, almost cinematic onslaught, and then simply asks why his love has left him alone. There is something deeply human in that. We recognize the way grief sometimes disguises itself as fear or anger or noise, and the way, beneath it all, the real feeling is smaller and more childlike than we expected. The repetition in "My love, my love, my love" is like a person speaking through tears.

Writers and critics have long admired how much Joyce packs into twelve lines. It has the force of an epic scene, the intimacy of a love poem, and the strangeness of a dream, and none of these crowd out the others. Fellow poets have valued the confidence of its construction, the way each stanza escalates and the way the whole thing lands on a single emotional beat.

Among general readers, the poem has also been loved through its musical setting. Singers and audiences are drawn to Barber's treatment, which seems to understand that the poem is already almost a song. For many people, this is how they first meet the poem, and it often sends them back to the words. It is a good example of how a short, intense piece of writing can travel a long way on the strength of its sound and its feeling, finding listeners far beyond the circle of people who care about Joyce's novels.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the poem is short and not especially famous outside Joyce circles, there is not a large body of famous attacks on it. Most criticism is aimed at the whole collection Chamber Music, and at how this poem sits within it. It is fair to say that the reception has been warm and the objections modest and mostly about style.

The most common reservation is that Joyce's early poetry is derivative. Chamber Music borrows heavily from the Elizabethan song writers, from the Irish Revival, and from the French Symbolists whom Joyce read, and critics have often said that you can hear the influences more clearly than you can hear Joyce. This poem is less guilty than most, but it still uses a stock of poetic props, such as black-armored charioteers and sea-people with green hair, that feel more like literature than like life. A tougher reader might say that Joyce is performing intensity rather than finding it.

A second criticism is about vagueness. The poem builds a vast, threatening image and then pulls the rug out by suggesting it was all a way of talking about heartbreak. Some readers feel this makes the army seem like a mere decoration, a grand costume on a conventional lament. If the real subject is the loss of a lover, they ask, why does the poem need so much armor? The answer, of course, is that the armor is the point, but not everyone is convinced.

There was also a mild complaint, from Joyce's more modern admirers, that the poem sounds too much like the Victorian and Edwardian verse he later mocked. In the age of Pound and Eliot, who wanted plain speech, a poem full of "unto" and "charioteers" could seem a little dated. Pound himself seems to have admired the poem for its rhythm and clarity of image, but his larger aim was to sweep away old-fashioned diction. That the poem still got included shows how strong its sound was.

And Joyce himself, as noted, was ambivalent about these early poems. He was critical of his youthful verse and sometimes dismissive of it as a minor thing compared with his prose. Later critics have sometimes echoed that, treating the poem as a charming footnote to the real Joyce rather than a great poem in its own right. That seems a bit harsh, but it is a point of view you will encounter. In summary: the poem has few enemies, but it also has a modest reputation, and the criticisms mostly come down to a question of originality.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

In literary history, this poem sits at a crossroads. It comes out of the late nineteenth-century world of Symbolism, which tried to suggest feeling through music and image rather than direct statement, and out of the Irish Literary Revival led by W. B. Yeats, which drew on Celtic myth. At the same time it looks forward to the modernist movement, in which the compression, intensity and imagery of a poem like this could be put to harder, more experimental use. Ezra Pound spotted that crossing point, which is why he picked it.

Joyce's strengths here are also those of the song tradition. English lyric poetry has always lived close to music, from the Elizabethan lute songs Joyce loved, to the ballads, to the songs of Thomas Moore. In Ireland, the line between poem and song has always been thin. Joyce was part of this inheritance, and the musical settings of his poems are not accidents. The poem also relates to a lineage of nightmare and dream-poems, from the Romantics through Poe and the Symbolists.

Compare it to Yeats, whose early poems also conjure Irish myth in a dreamy, musical manner, and whose later work is more hard and plain. Joyce never followed that path as a poet, but turned instead to prose, where he did something even more radical. You could also compare it to Pound's own short poems, or to the work of H.D., which share the clarity but not the stormy rhythm. A closer cousin might be Yeats's "The Wild Swans at Coole" in its ability to make an image into an emotion, though the moods are very different.

What descends from it? Strictly speaking, the poem is not a founding text of anything. Its main legacy lies in what it shows about Joyce. The themes here, the sea, the sleeping mind besieged by voices, loneliness, a call across distance, are the same ones that would organize his greatest books. In that sense, it is a small door into a very large house.

Why does it still matter? For one thing, it reminds us that great innovators often start by mastering tradition. Joyce's early verse is conventional, but it is conventional in a skilled and musical way, and you can hear the future writer testing his ear. For another, it is simply a good poem, short enough to read in a minute and strong enough to stay with you. If you read it out loud, the way it was meant to be read, you will feel the horses, and then you will feel the silence at the end. That combination of noise and stillness is something poetry can do better than almost any other art.
