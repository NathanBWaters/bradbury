I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land

James Joyce (1907) • 3 min read

Thundering out of the sea in black armour with whirling whips and plunging horses, a nightmare army of abandonment leaves the sleeper crying out in despair.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written around 1903–1904 and placed as the final, thirty-sixth poem (XXXVI) of James Joyce’s 1907 debut poetry collection Chamber Music, this explosive nightmare lyric was singled out by Ezra Pound in 1913 for inclusion in the landmark Des Imagistes anthology (1914), introducing Joyce to the international Modernist movement. Belonging to the Irish Modernism tradition and categorized under Modernist & Imagist, 'I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land' (1907) unfolds across 12 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. W. B. Yeats called it 'a technical and emotional masterwork,' Ezra Pound hailed it as the birth of modern Imagist rhythm, and Samuel Barber set it to music as one of his most famous twentieth-century art songs (Op. 10, No. 3).

I hear an army charging upon the land,

And the thunder of horses plunging, foam about their knees:

Arrogant, in black armour, behind them stand,

Disdaining the reins, with fluttering whips, the charioteers.

They cry unto the night their battle-name:

I moan in sleep when I hear afar their whirling laughter.

They cleave the gloom of dreams, a blinding flame,

Clanging, clanging upon the heart as upon an anvil.

They come shaking in triumph their long, green hair:

They come out of the sea and run shouting by the shore.

My heart, have you no wisdom thus to despair?

My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written around 1903–1904 and placed as the final, thirty-sixth poem (XXXVI) of James Joyce’s 1907 debut poetry collection Chamber Music, this explosive nightmare lyric was singled out by Ezra Pound in 1913 for inclusion in the landmark Des Imagistes anthology (1914), introducing Joyce to the international Modernist movement. Within the broader trajectory of Irish Modernism, James Joyce composed 'I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land' (1907) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"I hear an army charging upon the land,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, James Joyce grounded these 12 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land' lies the terrifying nocturnal onslaught of anxiety and romantic abandonment: after thirty-five delicate Elizabethan love songs in Chamber Music, the speaker’s sleep is shattered by an apocalyptic dream-cavalry rising out of the foam—arrogant charioteers in black armour with 'long, green hair' whose hoofs clang upon the heart like an anvil before leaving the forsaken lover crying 'My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"They cleave the gloom of dreams, a blinding flame,"—James Joyce develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, James Joyce constructs 'I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land' using three four-line ABAB quatrains written in surging hexameter/alexandrine lines filled with galloping anapests and dactyls, intense auditory consonance ('clang, ringing clear,' 'clanging, clanging upon the heart as upon an anvil'), and Yeatsian mythic nightmare imagery. Spanning 12 lines across 3 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how James Joyce balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "I hear an army charging upon the land," and "My love, my love, my love, why have you left me alone?" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

W. B. Yeats called it 'a technical and emotional masterwork,' Ezra Pound hailed it as the birth of modern Imagist rhythm, and Samuel Barber set it to music as one of his most famous twentieth-century art songs (Op. 10, No. 3). Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'I Hear an Army Charging Upon the Land' has remained a touchstone of the Modernist & Imagist canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to James Joyce’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
