Discourses I.2: How a Man on Every Occasion Can Maintain His Proper Character

Epictetus (c. 108 AD) • 7 min read

It is you who know yourself, how much you are worth to yourself, and at what price you sell yourself; for men sell themselves at various prices.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This chapter of the Discourses asks a question that sounds simple: how can a person stay true to who they are, whatever happens? It comes from Epictetus, a Greek teacher of Stoic philosophy who worked in the Roman Empire around the year 100. He was born a slave, became free, and ended up teaching ambitious young men, some of them future officials, in a school in northwestern Greece. A student named Arrian wrote down his talks, so we hear him roughly as he spoke. The backdrop matters. Under emperors like Nero and Vespasian, honest men could be ordered to stay silent, play along, or die. Epictetus tells the true stories of senators and philosophers who were given exactly those orders. His answer is that each of us has a character, and that the real question is not what is safe but what we are willing to sell ourselves for. As you listen, notice how he turns a price tag into a test of identity.

To the rational animal only is the irrational intolerable; but that which is rational is tolerable. Blows are not naturally intolerable. How is that? See how the Lacedaemonians endure whipping when they have learned that whipping is consistent with reason. To hang yourself is not intolerable. When then you have the opinion that it is rational, you go and hang yourself. In short, if we observe, we shall find that the animal man is pained by nothing so much as by that which is irrational; and, on the contrary, attracted to nothing so much as to that which is rational.

But the rational and the irrational appear such in a different way to different persons, just as the good and the bad, the profitable and the unprofitable. For this reason, particularly, we need discipline, in order to learn how to adapt the preconception of the rational and the irrational to the several things conformably to nature. But in order to determine the rational and the irrational, we use not only the estimates of external things, but we consider also what is appropriate to each person. For to one man it is consistent with reason to hold a chamber pot for another, and to look to this only: that if he does not hold it, he will receive stripes, and he will not receive his food; but if he shall hold the pot, he will not suffer anything hard or disagreeable. But to another man not only does the holding of a chamber pot appear intolerable for himself, but intolerable also for him to allow another to do this office for him. If then you ask me whether you should hold the chamber pot or not, I shall say to you that the receiving of food is worth more than the not receiving of it, and the being scourged is a greater indignity than not being scourged; so that if you measure your interests by these things, go and hold the chamber pot. “But this,” you say, “would not be worthy of me.” Well then, it is you who must introduce this consideration into the inquiry, not I; for it is you who know yourself, how much you are worth to yourself, and at what price you sell yourself; for men sell themselves at various prices.

For this reason, when Florus was deliberating whether he should go down to Nero’s spectacles, and also perform in them himself, Agrippinus said to him, “Go down:” and when Florus asked Agrippinus, “Why do not you go down?” Agrippinus replied, “Because I do not even deliberate about the matter. For he who has once brought himself to deliberate about such matters, and to calculate the value of external things, comes very near to those who have forgotten their own character. For why do you ask me the question, whether death is preferable or life? I say life. Pain or pleasure? I say pleasure. But if I do not take a part in the tragic acting, I shall have my head struck off.” “Go then and take a part, but I will not.” “Why?” “Because you consider yourself to be only one thread of those which are in the tunic. Well then it was fitting for you to take care how you should be like the rest of men, just as the thread has no design to be anything superior to the other threads. But I wish to be purple, that small part which is bright, and makes all the rest appear graceful and beautiful. Why then do you tell me to make myself like the many? and if I do, how shall I still be purple?”

Priscus Helvidius also saw this, and acted conformably. For when Vespasian sent and commanded him not to go into the senate, he replied, “It is in your power not to allow me to be a member of the senate, but so long as I am, I must go in.” “Well, go in then,” says the emperor, “but say nothing.” “Do not ask my opinion, and I will be silent.” “But I must ask your opinion.” “And I must say what I think right.” “But if you do, I shall put you to death.” “When then did I tell you that I am immortal? You will do your part, and I will do mine: it is your part to kill; it is mine to die, but not in fear: yours to banish me; mine to depart without sorrow.”

What good then did Priscus do, who was only a single person? And what good does the purple do for the toga? Why, what else than this, that it is conspicuous in the toga as purple, and is displayed also as a fine example to all other things? But in such circumstances another would have replied to Caesar who forbade him to enter the senate, “I thank you for sparing me.” But such a man Vespasian would not even have forbidden to enter the senate, for he knew that he would either sit there like an earthen vessel, or, if he spoke, he would say what Caesar wished, and add even more.

In this way an athlete also acted who was in danger of dying unless his private parts were amputated. His brother came to the athlete, who was a philosopher, and said, “Come, brother, what are you going to do? Shall we amputate this member and return to the gymnasium?” But the athlete persisted in his resolution and died. When someone asked Epictetus how he did this, as an athlete or a philosopher? As a man, Epictetus replied, and a man who had been proclaimed among the athletes at the Olympic games and had contended in them, a man who had been familiar with such a place, and not merely anointed in Baton’s school. Another would have allowed even his head to be cut off, if he could have lived without it. Such is that regard to character which is so strong in those who have been accustomed to introduce it of themselves and conjoined with other things into their deliberations.

“Come then, Epictetus, shave yourself.” If I am a philosopher, I answer, I will not shave myself. “But I will take off your head!” If that will do you any good, take it off.

Some person asked, “how then shall every man among us perceive what is suitable to his character?” How, he replied, does the bull alone, when the lion has attacked, discover his own powers and put himself forward in defense of the whole herd? It is plain that with the powers the perception of having them is immediately conjoined: and, therefore, whoever of us has such powers will not be ignorant of them. Now a bull is not made suddenly, nor a brave man; but we must discipline ourselves in the winter for the summer campaign, and not rashly run upon that which does not concern us.

Only consider at what price you sell your own will: if for no other reason, at least for this, that you sell it not for a small sum. But that which is great and superior perhaps belongs to Socrates and such as are like him. Why then, if we are naturally such, are not a very great number of us like him? Is it true then that all horses become swift, that all dogs are skilled in tracking footprints? What then, since I am naturally dull, shall I, for this reason, take no pains? I hope not. Epictetus is not superior to Socrates; but if he is not inferior, this is enough for me; for I shall never be a Milo, and yet I do not neglect my body; nor shall I be a Croesus, and yet I do not neglect my property; nor, in a word, do we neglect looking after anything because we despair of reaching the highest degree.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The chapter begins with a strange claim: only a rational animal finds the irrational intolerable. Epictetus means that suffering is rarely about the raw event. Blows, he says, are not naturally unbearable. The Spartans take a whipping calmly because they have learned that enduring it fits their idea of what is reasonable. A man who decides that hanging himself is reasonable goes and does it. What truly pains a human being, in other words, is whatever seems senseless to him, and what attracts him is whatever seems sensible. So everything depends on what you have taught yourself to count as sensible.

But people differ on this, and Epictetus faces that squarely with his chamber pot example. To one man, holding another man's chamber pot is perfectly reasonable if the alternative is a beating and no dinner. To another man it would be unthinkable. Epictetus does not scold either one. He says food is worth more than none and a beating is a worse indignity than no beating, so if those are your measures, go and hold the pot. But if you say "that would not be worthy of me," then you are the one bringing a new consideration into the question, a consideration about who you are. Men, he says, "sell themselves at various prices." The phrase is the key to the chapter. Everyone has a price, and what matters is knowing yours.

The stories that follow show what it looks like to have set the price so high that nothing is for sale. Florus wonders whether to appear in Nero's theatrical shows, which was a humiliating thing for a Roman of rank to do. Agrippinus tells him to go. Why will you not go yourself, asks Florus? Because, says Agrippinus, I do not even deliberate about it. Once you start weighing such things, you have already come close to forgetting your character. The image he uses is a robe. Most people are content to be one thread in the tunic. Agrippinus wants to be the purple stripe, the small bright band that makes the whole garment look fine.

Then comes Priscus Helvidius, ordered by the emperor Vespasian to stay out of the Senate. If I am a senator, I must go in, he says. Then say nothing. Then do not ask my opinion. But I must ask. Then I must say what I think is right. Then I will have you killed. "When then did I tell you that I am immortal?" Priscus answers. You do your part, which is to kill, and I will do mine, which is to die without fear.

What a first-time reader may miss is that Epictetus is not praising stubbornness. The point is that a settled sense of who you are makes some questions simply not open for discussion, and that this frees you. The bull senses its strength when the lion arrives. Character, like muscle, is built in the winter for the summer campaign. And he closes with a refusal to be crushed by comparison: he will never be the wrestler Milo or the rich king Croesus, yet he still looks after his body and his property. Falling short of the greatest does not excuse neglect.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is one of the most dramatic chapters in the Discourses, and its dramatic quality is a strength. Epictetus does not argue in the abstract that integrity matters. He puts real names and real confrontations on the table and lets the dialogue carry the point. The exchange between Priscus and Vespasian, with its neat pairs of "I must" and "I will," has the compression of a short play. The line about not being immortal is one of the best retorts in ancient prose, because it deflates the threat without denying it.

The chamber pot passage is also unusually honest. Many moralists would simply declare that a dignified person would never do such a thing. Epictetus, who as a slave surely knew the reality of the act, refuses to be so glib. He lets the person choose and says the choice reveals a price. That frankness makes the later demands feel earned. It is hard to dismiss a teacher who has not pretended that humiliation is imaginary.

The purple thread metaphor is exactly the kind of image that sticks. It is visual, brief and slightly flattering, which is why people remember it. It makes the case for being different without sounding vain.

Yet there are real flaws. The chapter can feel arbitrary about what counts as a point of honor. Why is appearing in a Nero show a line that cannot be crossed, while holding a chamber pot is fine for the right person? The answer is meant to be "whatever your own character says," but that makes the standard curiously private. A reader could ask whether a person with poor judgment would simply find their own vices to be part of their character. Epictetus gives no outside test beyond the example of the great, and the great are men like Socrates, whom he admits are rare.

The athlete anecdote is also puzzling. A philosopher-athlete dies rather than submit to an amputation, and Epictetus praises him for acting "as a man." Many readers will find this obscure and perhaps grotesque, since it is hard to see what principle was served by refusing medical treatment. It is the one story in the chapter where admiration seems to override judgment. The "shave yourself" retort, where Epictetus says that if he is a philosopher he will not shave, and that the emperor may take his head if it helps, is amusing but shows a certain swagger too. Philosophers in his day were known for beards, so it is partly about the costume of the profession, which makes the heroic gesture a little smaller.

There is also the oddly confident assertion that a person who has the powers will know it, like a bull. This leaves out the many people who discover their courage late, or fail it and are sorry. The chapter is better at celebrating the people who passed the test than helping the rest of us who are not sure.

Still, the final paragraph rescues the chapter from elitism. By saying that being dull by nature is no reason to give up effort, Epictetus turns an aristocratic ideal into a daily practice. It is a very good ending to a chapter that might otherwise have been only a gallery of heroes.

III. The Impact It Had

The chapter's influence is part of the larger story of the Discourses, which Arrian recorded and which survived in four of eight original books. Epictetus himself wrote nothing, and without Arrian we would not know this particular teacher at all. Arrian later served as a Roman governor and wrote a famous history of Alexander the Great, so his decision to preserve these classroom talks is itself a mark of how seriously a worldly, educated man took them.

The chapter's central question, how to stay one's self under pressure, ran through the late Stoic tradition. Marcus Aurelius, who read Epictetus closely, wrote repeatedly in his private notebook about keeping his own mind clear amid the demands of empire. The image of the person who refuses to bend even when the emperor commands has a long line of descendants, from accounts of martyrs in the early Christian centuries to later stories of conscience against power.

Helvidius Priscus and the other Stoic senators in these pages became a recurring symbol. Historians of Rome, especially Tacitus, describe a circle of Stoic opposition to tyrannical emperors, and writers in later centuries, when dealing with their own authoritarian governments, returned to these figures as models. The phrase "the Stoic opposition" is now standard in histories of the early empire, and whenever someone argues that philosophy has a role in public courage, Priscus's reply to Vespasian is likely to be cited.

In the twentieth century, Admiral James Stockdale, a navy officer who was held prisoner in North Vietnam for years, said he had Epictetus in mind during his captivity. He referred to the Discourses in speeches about leadership and integrity, and his example brought this kind of thinking into military education and business writing. The idea of a "price" for your will appears in modern discussions of corruption and ethics, where it is common to ask what a person would do under pressure and what would be enough to make them give in.

In modern therapy, the part about perceiving things as reasonable or unreasonable fits with cognitive approaches, which treat distress as dependent on beliefs about what is acceptable. Some therapists use the chamber pot passage as an example of how values shape what feels like humiliation.

The chapter is also taught in philosophy and classics courses as a primer on Stoic ethics and on the question of moral identity, sometimes called "integrity" in the sense of wholeness. Philosophers in the twentieth century, among them those interested in how our commitments define us, have written about the idea that some actions are unthinkable for a person, not because a calculation forbids them but because they are not options. Agrippinus's remark that he does not even deliberate is a classic ancient version of that idea.

Finally, the purple thread line has entered the language of ethics lectures and graduation speeches, where it is a popular way to encourage students to stand out for good reasons.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this chapter first for its courage, and for the fact that the courage is shown instead of preached. The scene of Priscus Helvidius walking toward the Senate house, knowing the emperor has forbidden it, and answering threats of death with calm logic has the power of a good scene in a film. People remember it. They remember that the answer to "I will have you put to death" is a kind of joke about mortality, and the combination of lightness and steel is deeply attractive.

They have also loved its lack of illusion about money and comfort. Epictetus does not pretend that principle is free. He admits that holding the pot gets you food and refusing gets you beaten, and he says so in plain terms. For readers tired of lofty moralizing, this realism is refreshing. The reader is treated as an adult who can decide for themselves, with the one condition that they know what they are paying.

The image of the purple stripe is a favorite. Readers like that it gives a graceful reason for individuality. Instead of saying "be different for the sake of it," it says that a small bright part makes the whole cloth look better. This has made it a popular quotation among people who feel pressure to blend in.

Another point of affection is the humane ending. After a series of near-heroic examples, Epictetus says that he is naturally dull and will still make an effort. The honesty here is disarming. It tells the reader that the goal is not to be Socrates but to do what one can, as one tends one's body and property without hoping to become a champion or a king. Readers often say they find this consoling, and it helps them take on the harder passages that came before.

Many people also love the line about the bull and the lion for its simplicity. It makes the case that courage is something that shows itself when needed, and that preparation in quiet seasons matters. Athletes, soldiers and teachers have all been drawn to the idea that you must "discipline ourselves in the winter for the summer campaign." It has the ring of a coaching maxim, and in a sense it is one, since Epictetus often borrows language from the gymnasium.

Finally, readers appreciate that the chapter takes the reader's inner life seriously. It does not assume that character is a given. It treats it as something you can examine, name and protect. For anyone who has been asked to compromise at work or in a community, and who felt a quiet sense that something would be lost, the chapter gives words for that feeling. This is why it is often recommended to young people starting careers, who are about to be offered their first prices.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critics of Epictetus, and of this chapter in particular, have raised several objections over the centuries. The most familiar is that his ideal is elitist and, in practice, available mostly to the strong. The stories involve senators, athletes and philosophers, people with standing, education and sometimes the safety of a famous name. A poor person with a family to feed, in a world with no protection, cannot easily say "I do not deliberate about this." Epictetus does acknowledge that men differ in price and that he will not condemn the man who holds the pot, but the heroes he praises are the ones who refuse.

A related objection is that the chapter is weak on how a person actually comes to know their own character. It assumes that you will know, the way the bull does. Critics note that people often deceive themselves about what they are made of, and that a standard that rests on self-knowledge is only as good as the self-knowledge. History includes many men who were certain of their principles until the moment they were tested.

Others have criticized the Stoic readiness to die. The philosopher-athlete story strikes many modern readers as pointlessly stubborn. More broadly, some have said that treating death as simply "my part" can shade into a kind of fatalism that makes resistance less practical. Priscus did not reform Vespasian. Within a few years, according to the historical accounts, he was exiled and then killed. A skeptic can ask what the gesture achieved, and Epictetus's own answer, that the purple makes the garment look fine, is more aesthetic than political.

The Stoics also faced the charge, going back to antiquity, that their ideal was too severe to be human. Pascal, in the seventeenth century, said that Epictetus understood duty but flattered human pride by suggesting we could meet it alone. The English-speaking world of the eighteenth century, which loved Stoic writing, still often found it cold.

There is also a more recent unease about how this kind of thinking can be used. Corporate and self-help writers have turned the language of character and price into a motivational vocabulary, which some critics think drains it of its context. In its original setting the "price" in question was one's life, one's freedom and one's honor in front of an emperor, not the cost of a promotion.

Finally, scholars point out that Arrian's notes give us a single, selective record, and that the speeches attributed to people like Priscus come to us through a teacher's retelling and not from neutral sources. Historians who have compared these stories with Tacitus find some agreement but also a heightened, teaching quality. The stories are probably true in outline, but they are polished to make a point, and readers should keep that in mind.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter sits in the tradition of Stoicism, the school founded in Athens by Zeno around 300 BC and carried to Rome by thinkers like Seneca and Musonius Rufus. Its special interest is in what scholars call the "roles" or "personae" a person plays. Earlier Stoics, notably Panaetius, taught that each person has several roles: as a human being, as a member of a family or city, as an individual with particular talents and as someone with a chosen path. Epictetus draws on this when he speaks of being appropriate "to each person." The chapter is a vivid example of Roman Stoicism applied to politics and daily conduct.

It descends, too, from Socrates, who in Plato's account preferred to die rather than give up his philosophical work. Epictetus mentions him at the end, with a modest comparison, and the whole chapter has a Socratic cast: the claim that goodness is a matter of what you value, and that the person who values the right things cannot be truly harmed by those who value the wrong ones.

In literature, the idea of the person who will not cooperate with tyranny echoes in many later works. Thomas More, who refused to approve Henry VIII's divorce and was executed, was described by admirers in language that recalls Priscus. Sophocles had already given the world Antigone, who defies a king's order because she cannot do otherwise. In modern times, the conscientious objector, the dissident and the whistleblower are all kin to the characters in this chapter. Writers like Vaclav Havel, with his essays on living in truth under an oppressive state, work in the same spirit, although he did not take his ideas from Epictetus alone.

In philosophy, the idea that some things are unthinkable for a person shows up in twentieth-century discussions of moral character, in the work of Bernard Williams, for instance, who wrote about "one thought too many" and about integrity. Epictetus's notion that a person's character sets limits on deliberation is a remarkable ancient anticipation of those discussions.

Compared with the Enchiridion, the short handbook drawn from the Discourses, this chapter is richer and more concrete. Compared with Seneca's letters, it is rougher and less comfortable. Compared with Marcus Aurelius, it is public, not private: a story told for a class of students who might soon face official power.

Why read it today? Because the situation it describes has not gone away. People still face pressure to go along, to stay quiet, to take part in something they find degrading, and to tell themselves that it is reasonable. The chapter does not tell you what to decide. It tells you that the decision is also a statement about who you are, and that you are setting a price whether you admit it or not. That is an idea worth spending ten minutes with before bed.
