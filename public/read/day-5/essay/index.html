How Should One Read a Book?

Virginia Woolf (1926) • 9 min read

The only advice, indeed, that one person can give another about reading is to take no advice, to follow your own instincts, to use your own reason, to come to your own conclusions.

In the first place, I want to emphasize the note of interrogation at the end of my title. Even if I could answer the question for myself, the answer would apply only to me and not to you. The only advice, indeed, that one person can give another about reading is to take no advice, to follow your own instincts, to use your own reason, to come to your own conclusions. If this is agreed between us, then I feel at liberty to put forward a few ideas and suggestions because you will not allow them to fetter that independence which is the most important quality that a reader can possess. After all, what laws can be laid down about books? The battle of Waterloo was certainly fought on a certain day; but is Hamlet a better play than Lear? Nobody can say. Each must decide that question for himself. To admit authorities, however heavily furred and gowned, into our libraries and let them tell us how to read, what to read, what value to place upon what we read, is to destroy the spirit of freedom which is the breath of those sanctuaries. Everywhere else we may be bound by laws and conventions—there we have none.

But to enjoy freedom, if the platitude is pardonable, we have of course to control ourselves. We must not squander our powers, helplessly and ignorantly, squirting half the house in order to water a single rose-bush; we must train them, exactly and powerfully, here on the very spot. This, it may be, is one of the first difficulties that faces us in a library. What is 'the very spot'? There may be nothing but a conglomeration and huddle of confusion. Poems and novels, histories and memoirs, dictionaries and blue-books; books written in all languages by men and women of all tempers, races, and ages jostle each other on the shelf. And outside the donkey brays, the women gossip at the pump, the colts gallop across the fields. Where are we to begin? How are we to bring order into this multitudinous chaos and so get the deepest and widest pleasure from what we read?

It is simple enough to say that since books have classes—fiction, biography, poetry—we should separate them and take from each what it is right that each should give us. Yet few people ask from books what books can give us. Most commonly we come to books with blurred and divided minds, asking of fiction that it shall be true, of poetry that it shall be false, of biography that it shall be flattering, of history that it shall enforce our own prejudices. If we could banish all such preconceptions when we read, that would be an admirable beginning. Do not dictate to your author; try to become him. Be his fellow-worker and accomplice. If you hang back, and reserve and criticize at first, you are preventing yourself from getting the fullest possible value from what you read. But if you open your mind as widely as possible, then signs and hints of almost imperceptible fineness, from the twist and turn of the first sentences, will bring you into the presence of a human being unlike any other. Steep yourself in this, acquaint yourself with this, and soon you will find that your author is giving you, or attempting to give you, something far more definite.

Wait a little, however, before you judge. Imagine that you yourself have tried to write a novel or a story—for perhaps the quickest way to understand the elements of what a novelist is doing is not to read, but to write; to make your own experiment with the dangers and difficulties of words. Recall, then, some event that has left a distinct impression on you—how at the corner of the street, perhaps, you passed two people talking. A tree shook; an electric light danced; the tone of the talk was comic, but also tragic; a whole vision, an entire conception, seemed contained in that moment. But when you attempt to reconstruct it in words, you will find that it breaks into a thousand conflicting impressions. Some must be subdued; others emphasized; in the process you will lose, probably, all grasp upon the emotion itself. Then turn from your blurred and littered pages to the opening pages of some great novelist—Defoe, Jane Austen, Hardy. Now you will be better able to appreciate their mastery.

Notice how each great writer invites us not merely into a room, but into a complete and distinct world governed by its own perspective. On the flat commons of Robinson Crusoe we march from one plain fact to another; with Jane Austen we are in a drawing-room where people talk and through the many mirrors of their talk reveal their characters; with Thomas Hardy we are taken out onto the dark moor under the stars, where human beings face the impersonal forces of fate and time. To shift from one of these worlds to another requires agility of imagination, yet that very elasticity is what keeps the reader alive.

And what of poetry? The impact of poetry is so hard and direct that for the moment there is no other sensation except that of the poem itself. What profound depths we visit then—how sudden and complete is our immersion! There is nothing here to catch hold of; nothing to stay us in our flight. The illusion of fiction is gradual; its effects are prepared; the writer uses what happens next to lead us on. But the poet is always our contemporary. Our being for the moment is centered and constricted, as in any violent shock of personal emotion. Afterwards, it is true, the sensation begins to spread in wider rings through our minds; remoter sympathies are touched; objects begin to solidify and we recognize our own experience reflected in the crystal of the verse.

Reading, then, is not a single uniform act, but a two-stage art requiring two almost opposite faculties of the mind. In the first stage—the actual process of reading—we must possess the utmost sympathy, openness, and curiosity; we must lower the barriers of our ego and become the author's accomplice, letting the rhythm of his sentences carry us wherever he wills. But once the book is closed, a second and equally vital stage begins. Wait for the dust of reading to settle; for the conflict and the questioning to die down; walk, talk, pull the dead petals from a rose, or fall asleep. Then, suddenly, without our willing it, for it is thus that Nature undertakes these transitions, the book will return, but differently. It will float to the top of the mind as a whole. And the book as a whole is different from the book received currently in separate phrases.

Now, in this second stage, having yielded our sympathy completely while we read, we have earned the right—indeed the duty—to judge with severity. We can compare this new book with the greatest works we already hold in memory—with Lear, with Emma, with La Recherche du Temps Perdu, with Ozymandias or The Waste Land. Like a demon that has slipped into us while we read with open arms, the book must now stand trial before our critical reason. Where did the writer's vision falter? Where did convention or vanity replace truth? Only by pairing generous receptivity during the reading with fearless judgment afterward do we develop true taste.

Who reads to bring about an end, however desirable? Are there not some pursuits that we practice because they are good in themselves, and some pleasures that are final? And is not this among them? I have sometimes dreamt, at least, that when the Day of Judgment dawns and the great conquerors and lawyers and statesmen come to receive their crowns, their laurels, their names carved indelibly upon imperishable marble, the Almighty will turn to Peter and will say, not without a certain envy when He sees us coming with our books under our arms, 'Look, these need no reward. We have nothing to give them here. They have loved reading.'

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

Originally delivered as a lecture to girls at Hayes Court School in Kent in January 1926, published in the Yale Review later that year, and revised as the closing essay of The Second Common Reader (1932), 'How Should One Read a Book?' is the critical credo of Virginia Woolf (1882–1941). Denied a formal university education at Cambridge like her brothers—an exclusion she immortalized in A Room of One's Own (1929)—Woolf educated herself by devouring her father Sir Leslie Stephen's vast Victorian library without a syllabus or professor standing over her. That self-taught freedom shaped her lifelong conviction that the 'common reader' (a term borrowed from Samuel Johnson) possesses an authority equal to any academic critic.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Woolf articulates three transformative principles for any lifelong reader. First is intellectual sovereignty: 'To admit authorities, however heavily furred and gowned, into our libraries... is to destroy the spirit of freedom which is the breath of those sanctuaries.' Second is her advice to try writing a single scene yourself—passing two people talking under a streetlamp while a tree shakes—so that when your own words scatter into confusion, you return to Defoe, Austen, or Hardy with an insider's awe for craft. Third is her two-stage psychology of reading: while reading, open your mind completely ('Do not dictate to your author; try to become him. Be his fellow-worker and accomplice'); then, after sleeping or pulling dead petals from a rose, let the book float back to the top of the mind as a whole and judge it against the greatest masterpieces.

3. Craft, Form & Rhetorical Technique

Notice how Woolf begins by drawing attention to the question mark at the end of her own title—immediately dismantling the top-down lecture format and establishing an intimate alliance between 'I' and 'you.' Her prose moves like water and light: one moment comical ('squirting half the house in order to water a single rose-bush'; donkeys braying and women gossiping at the pump outside the library window), the next luminous and precise ('a tree shook; an electric light danced'). Her closing vision of the Day of Judgment—where conquerors and statesmen line up for marble crowns while readers walk past with books under their arms needing no reward—is one of the most beloved paragraphs in twentieth-century prose.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Alongside Francis Bacon's 'Of Studies' and Marcel Proust's 'On Reading,' Woolf's essay stands at the summit of the literature of reading. Critics from Hermione Lee to Zadie Smith and Alberto Manguel have celebrated how Woolf democratized high Modernism: she demonstrated that deep, rigorous literary criticism does not belong in an ivory tower, but arises naturally from passionate, attentive readers who compare new works against the touchstones stored in their memory. Every night you spend weighing an essay's central metaphor against a short story and a poem trains your own intellect to see beneath the surface of convention and discover original connections across centuries of human thought.

5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 5—halfway through your ten-night starter hearth—Woolf's 'How Should One Read a Book?' collides with F. Scott Fitzgerald's 'Babylon Revisited' and W. B. Yeats's 'The Second Coming.' Notice the profound architecture of this pairing: Yeats warns that in a chaotic world 'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold,' and Fitzgerald's Charlie Wales struggles to prove he has rebuilt his moral center after the reckless twenties. How do we build a center that holds? Woolf gives us the exact daily method: first, read tonight with radical empathy as the author's accomplice; then fall asleep—just as Woolf says ('pull the dead petals from a rose, or fall asleep')—and let Bradbury's three texts float back to the top of your mind tomorrow morning as a unified whole.
