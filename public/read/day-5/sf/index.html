Bradbury 1000 • Night 5 • San Francisco Deep-Dive Story

City Lights, Pocket Poets, and the Trial of 'Howl' in 1950s North Beach

San Francisco — How Lawrence Ferlinghetti's 261 Columbus Avenue paperback bookshop and a 1957 obscenity trial liberated modern American literature (Beat Generation & San Francisco Renaissance (1953–1957)) • 20 min read

“Do I really want to go to jail for this book? Yes, because a poem with any slightest ‘redeeming social importance’ cannot be suppressed.” — Lawrence Ferlinghetti (1957)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Founded in June 1953 by poet Lawrence Ferlinghetti and sociologist Peter D. Martin inside a triangular 1907 building at 261 Columbus Avenue between North Beach and Chinatown, City Lights became the first all-paperback bookstore in the United States and the literary salon of the San Francisco Renaissance. After Allen Ginsberg electrified the Six Gallery at 3119 Fillmore Street on October 7, 1955, with his incantatory reading of 'Howl,' Ferlinghetti published the work as Pocket Poets Series No. 4—prompting U.S. Customs and San Francisco Police Captain William Hanrahan to seize the books in 1957 and triggering a landmark First Amendment trial in which Judge Clayton W. Horn ruled that any work with the slightest redeeming social importance enjoys full constitutional protection.

In June 1953, inside the narrow, wedge-shaped ground floor of the 1907 Artigues Building at 261 Columbus Avenue—where the Italian bakeries of North Beach meet the neon pagoda roofs of Chinatown across a brick lane then called Adler Place—two young military veterans pooled five hundred dollars apiece to open a tiny bookshop that broke every unwritten rule of the American book trade. At the height of the Cold War and the McCarthy hearings, bookstores across the United States were sedate, bourgeois carriage-trade establishments: they sold only expensive three-dollar clothbound hardcovers, employed clerks in neckties who hovered suspiciously over anyone browsing without buying, locked their doors promptly at five o’clock on weekday afternoons, and stayed shuttered all day Sunday. Thirty-four-year-old poet Lawrence Ferlinghetti and sociologist Peter D. Martin had a radically different vision.

A year earlier, Martin—the son of anti-fascist Italian anarchist Carlo Tresca’s circle—had launched a lively cultural journal titled *City Lights* on the second floor of the flatiron building, naming the magazine in tribute to Charlie Chaplin’s 1931 silent film masterpiece about a penniless Little Tramp who refuses to surrender his humanity to a callous metropolis. To help pay the fifty-dollar monthly rent on the building, Martin and Ferlinghetti knocked together wooden bookshelves down on the street corner and opened the *City Lights Pocket Bookshop*—the very first all-paperback bookstore in the United States. Stocking thirty-five-cent Pocket Books, British Penguins, New Directions editions, French existentialist paperbacks, and radical political pamphlets alongside international literary magazines, they installed wooden chairs around a central reading table and kept the front door open until midnight seven days a week.

When Peter Martin sold his half-interest for a thousand dollars in 1955 and returned to New York, Lawrence Ferlinghetti became sole proprietor of the triangular shop at 261 Columbus Avenue, imprinting it with a sensibility forged by war and Parisian bohemianism. Born Lawrence Monsanto Ferling in Yonkers, New York, in March 1919 and raised during his early childhood in Strasbourg, France, Ferlinghetti had commanded a U.S. Navy submarine chaser during the Normandy D-Day invasion of June 1944 before being transferred to the Pacific theater. Six weeks after the atomic bombing of August 1945, young Lieutenant Commander Ferlinghetti walked through the incinerated rubble of Nagasaki—an encounter with total nuclear devastation that transformed him overnight into a lifelong philosophical pacifist. After earning a master’s degree at Columbia University and a doctorate in poetry at the Sorbonne in Paris on the G.I. Bill, he settled in San Francisco in 1951 determined to build a Left Bank literary commons by the Golden Gate.

North Beach in the early 1950s was the ideal crucible for such an experiment. Rebuilt in the months after the 1906 earthquake by the handshake loans of A. P. Giannini’s barrel-top Bank of Italy, the neighborhood remained a tightly knit working-class enclave of Genoese, Sicilian, and Basque fishermen, scavengers, and bakers who viewed artists and poets with warm Mediterranean tolerance. A sunny four-room cold-water walk-up flat on Montgomery, Vallejo, or Filbert street rented for twenty-five to thirty-five dollars a month; fifty cents bought a steaming bowl of minestrone, a plate of spaghetti, a hunk of crusty sourdough bread, and a glass of rough red Zinfandel at Italian family restaurants around Washington Square; and right across Adler Place from City Lights stood Henri Lenoir’s stained-glass *Vesuvio Cafe*, soon joined two blocks up Vallejo Street in 1956 by Giovanni *'Papa Gianni'* Giotta’s *Caffè Trieste*, the first authentic Italian espresso house on the West Coast.

Presiding over this nascent San Francisco Renaissance was its towering intellectual godfather: fifty-year-old poet, translator of Chinese and Japanese verse, mountaineer, and philosophical anarchist Kenneth Rexroth. Every Friday evening inside his book-crammed apartment on Scott Street, Rexroth hosted open literary salons where young conscientious objectors who had spent World War II in the Fine Arts camp at Waldport, Oregon, mingled with visionary Bay Area poets Robert Duncan, Jack Spicer, William Everson (Brother Antoninus), Robin Blaser, and surrealist prodigy Philip Lamantia. Rejecting the prim, ironic, academic formalism that dominated East Coast university English departments during the Eisenhower years, Rexroth and his circle championed poetry as a spoken, communal art—performed aloud to the live syncopation of bebop jazz and addressed directly to human liberation.

Back behind the cash register at 261 Columbus Avenue, meanwhile, the daily heartbeat of City Lights belonged to its beloved Nisei manager, Shigeyoshi *'Shig'* Murao. Born in Seattle in 1926, Murao had been forcibly uprooted from the West Coast with his Japanese-American family during World War II and locked behind barbed wire at the Minidoka War Relocation Center in the high desert of Idaho—yet had volunteered directly from the internment camp to serve as a combat interpreter in the U.S. Army’s Military Intelligence Service during the Allied campaign and occupation of Japan. Drifting down to North Beach in the early 1950s with his trademark dark beard and briar pipe, Shig became the unmistakable gatekeeper of the bookstore: sitting calmly beside the cash box for more than twenty years, ordering obscure mimeographed chapbooks from around the globe, slipping free meals to starving young writers, and later co-editing the iconic street poetry magazine *Beatitude*.

In the summer of 1955, Ferlinghetti took the fateful leap from bookseller to publisher. Convinced that corporate New York publishing houses were ignoring the most vital voices in modern poetry—and that poetry belonged in the hip pocket of a working person’s blue jeans rather than on a parlor coffee table—he founded *City Lights Publishers* and launched the iconic *Pocket Poets Series*. Designed in a distinctive square, saddle-stitched black-and-white paperback format priced at seventy-five cents, the series debuted in August 1955 with Number One, Ferlinghetti’s own lyrical collection *Pictures of the Gone World*, followed quickly by Rexroth’s translations *Thirty Spanish Poems of Love and Exile* (Number Two) and Kenneth Patchen’s *Poems of Humor and Protest* (Number Three). Then came the October night that changed American literature forever.

On the evening of Friday, October 7, 1955, roughly one hundred and fifty poets, painters, students, and neighborhood bohemians squeezed onto wooden folding chairs inside the *Six Gallery*—a drafty former neighborhood auto-repair shop at 3119 Fillmore Street in the Marina District whose grease pits had been covered with planks and whose walls were hung with plasterboard and burlap. Organized by young painter Wally Hedrick and introduced by a beaming Kenneth Rexroth as master of ceremonies, the night featured five poets sharing the bill: Philip Lamantia, Michael McClure, Philip Whalen, Gary Snyder, and a twenty-nine-year-old newcomer from Paterson, New Jersey, and Columbia University named Allen Ginsberg. Down on the floor in front of the low wooden platform sat Ginsberg’s close friend Jack Kerouac, who had just walked around the room collecting dimes and quarters from the audience to buy three gallon jugs of cheap California red Burgundy.

Until a few months before that night, Allen Ginsberg had been working in a coat and tie as a market researcher in downtown San Francisco while undergoing psychotherapy with Dr. Philip Hicks—who had famously asked Ginsberg what he would truly like to do with his life, and upon hearing *'Write poetry and live with a man,'* had replied simply: *'Well, why don’t you?'* Moving into an apartment at 1010 Montgomery Street and falling in love with young Peter Orlovsky, Ginsberg sat down at his secondhand typewriter in the summer of 1955 and poured out an incandescent, three-part prophetic elegy modeled on the long breath-lines of Walt Whitman, the Hebrew Bible, and the tenor saxophone solos of Lester Young. Stepping to the microphone at the Six Gallery while Kerouac banged a wine jug on the floorboards shouting *'Go! Go! Go!'* at the end of every line, Ginsberg began to chant with rising intensity: *'I saw the best minds of my generation destroyed by madness, starving hysterical naked...'*

By the time Ginsberg finished the final cadence of *'Howl,'* his arms outstretched and his voice hoarse with emotion, Kenneth Rexroth was openly weeping and the entire Fillmore Street garage was on its feet roaring. Sitting quietly in the back row of the Six Gallery was Lawrence Ferlinghetti. Recognizing immediately that he had just witnessed a once-in-a-century rupture in American poetic consciousness, Ferlinghetti walked back to his desk at City Lights the next morning and dispatched a Western Union telegram to Ginsberg that deliberately echoed the immortal letter Ralph Waldo Emerson had written to Walt Whitman upon reading the first edition of *Leaves of Grass* exactly one hundred years earlier in July 1855: **'I GREET YOU AT THE BEGINNING OF A GREAT CAREER. WHEN DO I GET THE MANUSCRIPT?'**

Knowing full well that *Howl*’s unflinching vernacular candor about homosexuality, narcotics, ecstatic visions, and the soul-crushing conformist idol of *'Moloch'* would provoke Cold War censors, Ferlinghetti took a prudent precautionary step before going to press: he delivered the typescript to the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) of Northern California and asked whether their attorneys would defend him if he were arrested. Assured of the ACLU’s full backing, and armed with a glowing introduction by Ginsberg’s hometown mentor William Carlos Williams (*'Hold back the edges of your gowns, Ladies, we are going through hell'*), Ferlinghetti commissioned John Sankey’s Villiers Publications in Holloway, London, to handset and print *Howl and Other Poems* as Number Four in the Pocket Poets Series for seventy-five cents a copy.

The first printing of fifteen hundred copies slipped quietly through the U.S. mail into San Francisco in the autumn of 1956, and *San Francisco Chronicle* book editor William Hogan hailed the slim black-and-white volume in his column. But when a second printing of five hundred and twenty copies arrived aboard a British freighter at the Embarcadero docks on March 25, 1957, Collector of U.S. Customs Chester MacPhee impounded the crates under the Tariff Act of 1930, announcing to reporters: *'The words and the sense of the writing is obscene. You wouldn’t want your children to come across it.'* Undaunted, Ferlinghetti outflanked federal customs entirely by hiring a local San Francisco photo-offset printer to produce an American edition wholly within United States borders, while in late May the U.S. Attorney in San Francisco declined to prosecute MacPhee’s customs seizure and ordered the London copies released.

Humiliated by the federal retreat, Captain William Hanrahan of the San Francisco Police Department’s Juvenile Bureau resolved to crush the book under California state criminal law. On the afternoon of June 3, 1957, two plainclothes Juvenile Bureau inspectors, Thomasaina and Russell, strolled into City Lights Bookstore at 261 Columbus Avenue, selected a seventy-five-cent copy of *Howl and Other Poems* from the shelf, and handed Shig Murao a pre-marked one-dollar bill. The instant Murao rang up the sale on the cash register and handed back twenty-five cents change, the officers flashed their badges, placed Murao under arrest for selling obscene literature, and served a warrant for Lawrence Ferlinghetti—who returned from his cabin in Big Sur to turn himself in at the Hall of Justice and post five hundred dollars bail alongside his bookstore manager.

When *People of the State of California v. Lawrence Ferlinghetti and Shigeyoshi Murao* opened that August in San Francisco Municipal Court, civil libertarians braced for the worst: the case had been assigned without a jury to Judge Clayton W. Horn, a devout, conservative Sunday-school teacher famous for ordering female shoplifters to watch Cecil B. DeMille’s *The Ten Commandments* and write moral reflection essays in lieu of jail. Yet facing Assistant District Attorney Ralph McIntosh across the courtroom was a formidable pro-bono defense team: flamboyant San Francisco trial lawyer Jake *'The Master'* Ehrlich—author of *Never Plead Guilty* and the real-life inspiration for Erle Stanley Gardner’s Perry Mason—joined by ACLU Staff Counsel Lawrence Speiser and constitutional scholar Albert Bendich.

After the court swiftly dismissed the charge against Shig Murao because the prosecution could not prove a bookstore clerk had personally read every page of every volume in the shop, the trial of Lawrence Ferlinghetti turned into the most dazzling literary seminar ever conducted under oath. For days, Ehrlich and Speiser called nine of the West Coast’s most respected literary critics, novelists, and scholars to the witness stand—led by UC Berkeley English Department Chairman Mark Schorer, novelist Walter Van Tilburg Clark (*The Ox-Bow Incident*), poet Kenneth Rexroth, and professors Leo Lowenthal and Herbert Blau. Whenever Prosecutor McIntosh pointed indignantly to a four-letter street word in *Howl* and demanded to know how a respectable university professor could defend such language, Schorer and Clark calmly educated the court on the structure of prophetic elegy, explaining that an artist condemning the spiritual wasteland of modern industrial society could no more use polite drawing-room euphemisms than Dante could describe the Inferno without fire.

To the astonishment of the legal establishment, conservative Judge Clayton Horn took the summer recess to study not only every line of *Howl*, but also James Joyce’s *Ulysses*, Judge John M. Woolsey’s famous 1933 federal opinion legalizing Joyce’s novel, and the United States Supreme Court’s brand-new June 1957 decision in *Roth v. United States*. On October 3, 1957, before a packed courtroom, Judge Horn read aloud a landmark thirty-nine-page written opinion acquitting Lawrence Ferlinghetti on all counts. Applying the First Amendment with crystalline clarity, Horn ruled that a book must be judged as a whole rather than by isolated words, that its language must be evaluated by its relevance to the author’s theme, and that no work of literature may be suppressed as obscene unless it is *'utterly without redeeming social importance.'* He closed with a warning that still echoes across every free society: *'The people owe a duty to themselves and to their posterity to preserve inviolate the constitutional liberties of free speech and a free press... The authors of the First Amendment knew that novel and unconventional ideas might disturb the complacent, but they chose to encourage a freedom which they believed essential if vigorous enlightenment was ever to triumph over slothful ignorance.'* Overnight, the acquittal turned *Howl* into a bestseller with over a million copies in print, opened the legal floodgates for the American publication of D. H. Lawrence’s *Lady Chatterley’s Lover*, Henry Miller’s *Tropic of Cancer*, and William S. Burroughs’s *Naked Lunch*, and enshrined the little triangular bookshop at 261 Columbus Avenue—designated San Francisco Historic Landmark No. 229 in 2001—as an immortal sanctuary of the unchained human voice.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. First Amendment Jurisprudence: The 'Redeeming Social Importance' Precedent

Before the 1957 Howl trial in San Francisco, local police departments and customs officials across the United States routinely banned literary works on the basis of isolated four-letter words rather than judging the work as a whole. Judge Clayton Horn's October 3, 1957 opinion established that even if a work uses coarse vernacular or deals with taboo social realities, the First Amendment protects it if it possesses 'the slightest redeeming social importance'—and furthermore ruled that the language of a work must be relevant to its theme and judged by contemporary standards rather than Victorian prudery. 2. Shigeyoshi Murao and the Democratization of the Paperback

Every visitor to City Lights should know the name Shigeyoshi 'Shig' Murao (1926–1999). Interned with his Japanese-American family at the Minidoka concentration camp in Idaho during World War II before serving in the U.S. Army Military Intelligence Service, Murao managed the front counter of City Lights for over twenty years, edited the legendary journal Beatitude, and personally took the arrest collar on June 3, 1957. Together, Ferlinghetti and Murao proved that the small, square-format seventy-five-cent Pocket Poets paperback could bypass corporate New York publishing houses and put living poetry directly into the hands of everyday readers. 3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Three places and artifacts anchor the Beat map of San Francisco: first, the Pocket Poets Series (especially No. 1, Ferlinghetti's Pictures of the Gone World, and No. 4, Ginsberg's Howl and Other Poems), still in print today in its iconic black-and-white cover design; second, Jack Kerouac Alley (formerly Adler Place), the narrow brick lane separating City Lights in North Beach from Chinatown, transformed by Ferlinghetti in 1988 from a garbage alley into a pedestrian literary promenade; and third, Caffè Trieste on Vallejo Street, opened in 1956 by Giovanni Giotta as the West Coast's first espresso house.
