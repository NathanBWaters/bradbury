Bradbury 1000 • Night 5 • San Francisco Deep-Dive Story

City Lights, Pocket Poets, and the Trial of 'Howl' in 1950s North Beach

San Francisco — How Lawrence Ferlinghetti's 261 Columbus Avenue paperback bookshop and a 1957 obscenity trial liberated modern American literature (Beat Generation & San Francisco Renaissance (1953–1957)) • 5 min read

“Do I really want to go to jail for this book? Yes, because a poem with any slightest ‘redeeming social importance’ cannot be suppressed.” — Lawrence Ferlinghetti (1957)

In June 1953, when thirty-four-year-old World War II Navy subchaser commander and Sorbonne PhD Lawrence Ferlinghetti pooled $500 apiece with sociologist Peter D. Martin to open a tiny wedge-shaped bookshop inside the 1907 Artigues Building at 261 Columbus Avenue in North Beach, American bookstores were polite, sedate institutions. Most closed promptly at 5:00 PM on weekdays, stayed shuttered on Sundays, sold only expensive clothbound hardcovers, and discouraged customers from lingering unless they were buying immediately.

Ferlinghetti and Martin named their shop *City Lights Pocket Bookshop*—after Charlie Chaplin’s 1931 silent film masterpiece—and broke every rule of the American book trade. City Lights became the first all-paperback bookstore in the United States. Even more radically, Ferlinghetti installed wooden chairs and benches, stocked foreign-language journals, anarchist pamphlets, and avant-garde poetry magazines that could walk into a student’s or dockworker’s back pocket for thirty-five cents, and kept the doors open until midnight seven days a week. 'I wanted a place,' Ferlinghetti later recalled, 'where you could sit and read for hours without anybody bothering you, like the cafés and bookstalls along the Seine in Paris.'

North Beach in the early 1950s was the ideal crucible for a literary revolution. Rent in the cold-water walk-up flats above Italian bakeries and espresso houses like Caffè Trieste cost thirty dollars a month; poet Kenneth Rexroth hosted Friday-night anarchist-pacifist salons blending jazz, Chinese classical poetry, and radical politics; and across the street at Vesuvio Cafe, bohemian painters, longshoremen, and jazz saxophonists debated until dawn.

Then came the night of October 7, 1955, inside the *Six Gallery*—a converted auto-repair garage at 3119 Fillmore Street in the Marina. Hosted by Kenneth Rexroth while Jack Kerouac passed around jugs of cheap California Burgundy collected with dimes from the crowd of 150 people, five young poets took the stage: Philip Lamantia, Michael McClure, Philip Whalen, Gary Snyder, and a twenty-nine-year-old unknown market researcher from Paterson, New Jersey, named Allen Ginsberg. Swaying to the rhythm of Hebraic prophetic chant and bebop jazz, Ginsberg read a work-in-progress titled *'Howl'* for the very first time in public: *'I saw the best minds of my generation destroyed by madness, starving hysterical naked...'*

Sitting in the audience that night, Lawrence Ferlinghetti knew immediately that he had just witnessed a watershed in American letters. The next morning, returning to his desk at City Lights, he wired Ginsberg a telegram echoing the famous greeting Ralph Waldo Emerson had sent Walt Whitman upon reading *Leaves of Grass* exactly one hundred years earlier in 1855: **'I GREET YOU AT THE BEGINNING OF A GREAT CAREER. WHEN DO I GET THE MANUSCRIPT?'**

Before printing *Howl and Other Poems* as Number Four in his black-and-white paperback *Pocket Poets Series* (commissioned from Villiers Publications in London to keep costs down to seventy-five cents a copy), Ferlinghetti prudently showed the manuscript to the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) of Northern California and asked whether they would defend him if he were arrested for obscenity. Assured of their backing, he imported the first edition in the autumn of 1956. On March 25, 1957, U.S. Customs Collector Chester MacPhee seized 520 copies of the second printing at the San Francisco docks as 'obscene,' declaring: 'The words and the sense of the writing is obscene. You wouldn’t want your children to come across it.'

When federal attorneys declined to prosecute the customs seizure, Ferlinghetti shrewdly printed a new edition entirely inside the United States to bypass Customs completely. On June 3, 1957, two plainclothes officers from the San Francisco Police Department’s Juvenile Bureau walked into City Lights, handed clerk Shigeyoshi Murao—the beloved Nisei manager and literary editor who was the daily soul of the bookstore—a marked one-dollar bill for a copy of *Howl*, and arrested both Murao and Ferlinghetti on criminal obscenity charges carrying up to six months in county jail.

The ensuing summer trial before conservative, Sunday-school-teaching Municipal Judge Clayton W. Horn (*People of the State of California v. Lawrence Ferlinghetti*) became one of the most consequential courtroom battles in twentieth-century cultural history. Represented pro bono by famed trial lawyer Jake Ehrlich, the ACLU’s Lawrence Speiser, and Albert Bendich, the defense called nine eminent literary critics and professors—including Mark Schorer, Walter Van Tilburg Clark, and Kenneth Rexroth—who testified under oath to the structural craft, prophetic tradition, and moral seriousness of Ginsberg’s poem. When the prosecutor mocked the syntax of *Howl* and demanded to know how a poem containing raw anatomical and sexual candor could have literary value, Schorer quietly disassembled the prosecution’s narrow literalism before the court.

Instead of rubber-stamping the police censorship, Judge Clayton Horn did something extraordinary: he took *Howl* home, read James Joyce’s *Ulysses* (which had survived federal censorship in 1933) alongside it, and on October 3, 1957, issued a landmark thirty-nine-page ruling acquitting Ferlinghetti and Murao on all counts. Applying the newly articulated Roth Supreme Court standard, Judge Horn wrote that a book cannot be declared obscene unless it is 'utterly without redeeming social importance'—adding a warning that still rings across free societies: *'The authors of the First Amendment knew that novel and unconventional ideas might disturb the complacent, but they chose to encourage a freedom which they believed essential if vigorous enlightenment was ever to triumph over slothful ignorance.'* Overnight, the ruling opened the floodgates for the legal publication of D. H. Lawrence’s *Lady Chatterley’s Lover*, Henry Miller’s *Tropic of Cancer*, and William S. Burroughs’s *Naked Lunch*—and turned the little triangular bookstore at 261 Columbus Avenue into a global shrine of the free word.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. First Amendment Jurisprudence: The 'Redeeming Social Importance' Precedent

Before the 1957 Howl trial in San Francisco, local police departments and customs officials across the United States routinely banned literary works on the basis of isolated four-letter words rather than judging the work as a whole. Judge Clayton Horn's October 3, 1957 opinion established that even if a work uses coarse vernacular or deals with taboo social realities, the First Amendment protects it if it possesses 'the slightest redeeming social importance'—and furthermore ruled that the language of a work must be relevant to its theme and judged by contemporary standards rather than Victorian prudery. 2. Shigeyoshi Murao and the Democratization of the Paperback

Every visitor to City Lights should know the name Shigeyoshi 'Shig' Murao (1926–1999). Interned with his Japanese-American family at the Minidoka concentration camp in Idaho during World War II before serving in the U.S. Army Military Intelligence Service, Murao managed the front counter of City Lights for over twenty years, edited the legendary journal Beatitude, and personally took the arrest collar on June 3, 1957. Together, Ferlinghetti and Murao proved that the small, square-format seventy-five-cent Pocket Poets paperback could bypass corporate New York publishing houses and put living poetry directly into the hands of everyday readers. 3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Three places and artifacts anchor the Beat map of San Francisco: first, the Pocket Poets Series (especially No. 1, Ferlinghetti's Pictures of the Gone World, and No. 4, Ginsberg's Howl and Other Poems), still in print today in its iconic black-and-white cover design; second, Jack Kerouac Alley (formerly Adler Place), the narrow brick lane separating City Lights in North Beach from Chinatown, transformed by Ferlinghetti in 1988 from a garbage alley into a pedestrian literary promenade; and third, Caffè Trieste on Vallejo Street, opened in 1956 by Giovanni Giotta as the West Coast's first espresso house. 4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Step inside City Lights Bookstore at 261 Columbus Avenue (37.7976° N, 122.4066° E)—designated San Francisco Historic Landmark No. 229 in 2001, the first business in city history landmarked for its cultural rather than purely architectural heritage. Climb the narrow wooden stairs to the second-floor Poetry Room, sit in the rocking chair looking out over Columbus Avenue, then step outside into Jack Kerouac Alley for a glass of wine at Vesuvio Cafe or an espresso two blocks up at Caffè Trieste.
