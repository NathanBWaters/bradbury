Bradbury 1000 • Night 5 • Nightly Poem

The Second Coming

W. B. Yeats (1920) • 5 min read

As the falcon loses contact with the falconer and the center fails to hold, a sphinx-like beast slouches toward Bethlehem.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in January 1919 in the wake of World War I, the Russian Revolution, the Irish War of Independence, and his pregnant wife Georgie Hyde-Lees’s near-fatal battle with the 1918 Spanish Influenza pandemic, W. B. Yeats’s 'The Second Coming' (published in The Dial in 1920) is the preeminent prophetic lyric of High Modernism. Drawing on his esoteric system of interlocking two-thousand-year historical cones ('gyres'), Yeats uses twenty-two lines of muscular blank verse to diagnose civilizational collapse ('Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold') and envision a pitiless sphinx-like 'rough beast' slouching toward Bethlehem to inaugurate a terrifying new epoch. Placed on Night 5 of the Bradbury 1000 curriculum, W. B. Yeats’s 'The Second Coming' (1920) exemplifies how compressed poetic form preserves human perception across centuries.

Turning and turning in the widening gyre

The falcon cannot hear the falconer;

Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;

Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,

The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere

The ceremony of innocence is drowned;

The best lack all conviction, while the worst

Are full of passionate intensity.

Surely some revelation is at hand;

Surely the Second Coming is at hand.

The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out

When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi

Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert

A shape with lion body and the head of a man,

A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,

Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it

Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.

The darkness drops again; but now I know

That twenty centuries of stony sleep

Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,

And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,

Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed in January 1919 and published in The Dial in November 1920, 'The Second Coming' was born out of convergent historical and personal cataclysms. Europe had just emerged from the slaughter of World War I; the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917 had toppled the Russian empire; the Irish War of Independence and the violent Black and Tans campaign were tearing Yeats's homeland apart; and closer to home, Yeats's pregnant wife, Georgie Hyde-Lees, was fighting for her life during the 1918–1919 Spanish Influenza pandemic that killed tens of millions worldwide. To make sense of the chaos, Yeats drew on the esoteric system of interlocking historical cones ('gyres') that he and Georgie were developing through automatic writing, later published in A Vision (1925).

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

In Yeats's philosophy of history, civilizations move in two-thousand-year cycles shaped like interpenetrating conical spirals ('gyres'). As one gyre expands to its widest point of fragmentation—where the 'falcon' (humanity, intellect, or historical action) can no longer hear the 'falconer' (spiritual order, tradition, or moral restraint)—its opposite gyre begins to unwind. The poem's terrifying twist occurs in the second stanza: when the speaker cries out for 'the Second Coming' in hopes of Christian salvation, the collective unconscious ('Spiritus Mundi') sends instead a nightmare inversion. The two-thousand-year Christian era launched by a 'rocking cradle' in Bethlehem is ending, replaced by an antithetical, pitiless sphinx-beast awakening from 'stony sleep' to inaugurate an age of hierarchy and terror.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Written in twenty-two lines of loose, muscular blank verse divided into an eight-line diagnosis and a fourteen-line prophetic vision (a fractured shadow-sonnet), the poem achieves aphoristic permanence in almost every line. The opening participle 'Turning and turning' immediately sets the centrifugal spin in motion, while the sharp caesura in line three—'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold'—splits the pentameter line down the middle. In stanza two, repetition of 'Surely... Surely... The Second Coming!' captures the speaker's desperate self-reassurance before the syntax slows into heavy, spondaic menace ('gaze blank and pitiless,' 'slow thighs,' 'rough beast'). The verb 'Slouches' is a stroke of genius: the beast does not march heroically; it lumbers with predatory, inevitable slouching.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

No twentieth-century poem has proven more eerily prophetic or more widely quoted in political and cultural discourse. Written more than a decade before the rise of European fascism and totalitarianism, its indictment—'The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity'—became the touchstone for diagnosing democratic collapse. Chinua Achebe drew the title of his landmark 1958 novel Things Fall Apart from line three, and Joan Didion titled her iconic 1968 essay collection Slouching Towards Bethlehem after the closing line. Critics from Harold Bloom to Helen Vendler rank it as the supreme apocalyptic lyric of Modernism. Few poems in modern history have provided such an indispensable vocabulary for diagnosing moments when technological power outstrips moral wisdom.

V. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 5, Yeats's widening gyre collides with F. Scott Fitzgerald's 'Babylon Revisited' and Virginia Woolf's 'How Should One Read a Book?' In Fitzgerald's story, Charlie Wales walks through snow-covered Paris in 1930 after the 1929 stock market crash, realizing how the 'snow of Twenty-Nine' froze the reckless, decadent gyre of the Jazz Age in which 'the ceremony of innocence'—his marriage and his daughter Honoria's security—was squandered. Woolf, meanwhile, teaches us how to build an independent critical center inside our own mind through active reading. When the outer world feels fragmented and loud with 'passionate intensity,' your nightly reading practice is how the falcon stays tuned to the falconer. Within the Night 5 reading sequence, W. B. Yeats’s 'The Second Coming' rewards close attention to every cadence, image, and structural turn across its 22 lines.
