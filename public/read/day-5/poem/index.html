Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

The Second Coming

W. B. Yeats (1920) • 5 min read

As the falcon loses contact with the falconer and the center fails to hold, a sphinx-like beast slouches toward Bethlehem.

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Second Coming is one of the most quoted poems of the twentieth century. Phrases from it, like things fall apart, and the best lack all conviction while the worst are full of passionate intensity, turn up in newspapers, speeches and book titles every year. W. B. Yeats, an Irish poet, wrote it in January 1919, just after the First World War had killed millions. The Russian Revolution was only a year old, a deadly flu epidemic was sweeping the world, and Ireland was sliding into its own war of independence. Yeats, who had a lifelong interest in mysticism and had worked out his own private theory of history, saw all this as the end of an age. The poem takes the Christian idea of Christ's return and twists it into something horrifying. Do not worry about the occult details. What matters is the mood: a feeling that the world's old order is breaking up and that something terrible and unknown is about to arrive.

Turning and turning in the widening gyre

The falcon cannot hear the falconer;

Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;

Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,

The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere

The ceremony of innocence is drowned;

The best lack all conviction, while the worst

Are full of passionate intensity.

Surely some revelation is at hand;

Surely the Second Coming is at hand.

The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out

When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi

Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert

A shape with lion body and the head of a man,

A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,

Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it

Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.

The darkness drops again; but now I know

That twenty centuries of stony sleep

Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,

And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,

Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem has two parts. The first stanza is a diagnosis. The second is a vision. Together they describe the end of one world and the birth of another that no one wants.

Begin with the opening image. A falcon is turning and turning in a widening spiral, a gyre, and it can no longer hear the falconer. This is a picture of control slipping away. A trained bird should return to the hand that calls it. Here the circle grows so wide that the call cannot reach. The falconer stands for any central authority, whether God, kings, church, tradition or shared values, and the falcon for a society flying off beyond its reach. Then the famous line, things fall apart; the centre cannot hold. There is no longer anything at the middle to hold the world together.

The rest of the first stanza paints the consequences. Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world, and the blood-dimmed tide is loosed, an image of floodwater and bloodshed, evidently echoing the war that had just ended. The ceremony of innocence is drowned: the rituals and habits that protected people, the gentle manners of ordinary life, are swept away. And then the most-quoted couplet, the best lack all conviction, while the worst are full of passionate intensity. Those who are thoughtful and decent are paralysed by doubt, and those who are cruel and fanatical are certain and energised. That sounds like a description of any period of political crisis, which is why people keep quoting it.

The second stanza begins with a rush of hope. Surely some revelation is at hand; surely the Second Coming is at hand. People in a crisis often want a saviour. But as soon as the words are spoken, a vision interrupts, from the Spiritus Mundi, a Latin phrase meaning spirit of the world, which Yeats used for a kind of shared memory of humanity from which images rise. In the desert is a shape with a lion's body and a man's head, its gaze blank and pitiless as the sun. This resembles the ancient Sphinx, but it is moving. Its slow thighs are stirring, and the desert birds, shadows, reel around it.

The final lines carry the explanation. Twenty centuries of stony sleep, the two thousand years since the birth of Christ, were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle. The Christian era began with a baby in a manger, and Yeats suggests that this event troubled the older, harder world, and that an opposite birth is now due. The rough beast, its hour come round at last, slouches towards Bethlehem to be born. The savage opposite of Christ is coming to the same town.

So the poem's real shock is that the Second Coming is not the return of a saviour, but the arrival of a monster. It reverses the Christian hope and turns the vision of renewal into horror. Yeats ends with a question, and no one answers it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The poem is a masterclass in compression and menace. It is only twenty-two lines, yet it feels vast. Much of the power comes from its pacing. The first stanza moves in short, hammering statements, each one a blow. The second stanza slows and thickens, with long lines and heavy vowels, as though the vision is physically pressing on the speaker's eyes. The line about the slow thighs is a triumph of sound, with words that drag, and the final line, with its slouches towards Bethlehem, uses the sloppy, lurching verb slouches to make the beast seem both lazy and unstoppable.

The word choices are often brilliant. Blood-dimmed is exact and horrifying. Passionate intensity is a wonderfully ironic phrase, since passion is normally a good thing. Slouches is the perfect verb, since a slouch is something you do when you do not care what anyone thinks. The poem also has an unusually effective structure. It moves from a general statement to a specific image, from political abstraction to a nightmare you can see, and the shift makes the abstraction feel real.

But honest assessment must also acknowledge flaws. The poem leans heavily on a private system of belief that Yeats worked out in his book A Vision, with its gyres, cycles and two-thousand-year ages. Without that background, the second half can seem mysterious, even arbitrary. The vision of the Sphinx and the desert birds is evocative, but its logic is hard to explain, and a reader may feel more impressed than informed. Spiritus Mundi is a phrase that sounds profound and gestures at an idea few readers can unpack.

The poem also relies on grand pronouncements that can feel more like oratory than argument. The best lack all conviction is a superb line, but it is also a sweeping claim, and it is not clear how we are supposed to tell who the best and the worst are. It flatters anyone who feels they belong to the best. There is a reasonable suspicion that the line's popularity comes from its flattery as much as its insight.

The poem is also, in some ways, too sure of its own prophetic tone. It speaks in the voice of someone who knows what the century will bring, and while that confidence is thrilling, it can verge on pomposity.

Even so, the poem rises above its flaws. Its best lines are among the most memorable in English, and its imagery has the force of a dream you cannot shake. A poem that you can recite in two minutes and carry for a lifetime has done something right.

III. The Impact It Had

The impact of The Second Coming has been enormous, far greater than its short length would suggest. It first appeared in the American magazine The Dial in November 1920, and then in Yeats's 1921 collection Michael Robartes and the Dancer. It was quickly recognised as a major poem, and it cemented Yeats's reputation as the great poet of his age. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923. The poem's themes of breakdown and fierce rebirth shaped the later phase of his career, in which he wrote with a harder, more modern voice than in his early dreamy verse.

The most visible effect has been on language. The poem has supplied English with a stockpile of titles and catchphrases. Things Fall Apart is the title of Chinua Achebe's 1958 novel about the collapse of an Igbo community under colonialism, one of the most important books of African literature. Joan Didion called her 1968 collection of essays about 1960s California Slouching Towards Bethlehem, using the poem to describe a society coming apart. Many other works have borrowed its lines, from novels to songs to films. The phrase centre cannot hold has become a standard shorthand for institutional collapse.

Politically, the poem is quoted with great regularity. Writers and commentators reach for it whenever democratic societies seem to polarise, and it tends to see a surge in citation in periods of political upheaval. It has been invoked in discussions of the world wars, the Cold War, the rise of extremism, and more recent political crises. Few poems have been used so often as a diagnostic tool for public life.

In literature, it helped define what modernist poetry could do. Eliot's The Waste Land appeared in 1922, and the two works are often placed side by side as the great poems of postwar disillusionment. Yeats gave the movement a voice that was formal, controlled and prophetic, rather than fragmentary. Poets after him, among them W. H. Auden, who wrote an elegy for Yeats in 1939, and later Irish poets like Seamus Heaney, have all had to deal with his shadow.

The poem has also had a wide life in other media. It has been quoted in films and television, in song lyrics, and in numerous science fiction and fantasy works, where the rough beast has become a stock image of an apocalyptic force.

In the classroom, it is one of the most commonly taught poems of the twentieth century, a standard text for introducing modernism, symbolism and the relationship between poetry and history. Its sheer quotability has ensured that even people who have never read it have been shaped by it.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love The Second Coming above all for its prophetic power. Readers in every decade since 1920 have felt that it describes their own moment. The poem seems to anticipate the rise of totalitarian regimes in the 1930s, the horrors of the Second World War, and the political turmoil of later decades. It is easy to think that Yeats had foresight, though what he really had was an exceptional ear for the signs of a society in crisis. Many readers find the poem eerily timeless.

They love the language. The lines have a ring that makes them nearly impossible to forget. Even readers who dislike poetry often know the line about the centre not holding. The poem's phrases have an authority that comes from a mix of plain words and strange images, and from rhythms that feel inevitable. Writers consistently cite it as an example of how poetry can say what ordinary prose cannot.

Readers love its dramatic structure. The poem builds like a play, from a calm, chilling description to a burst of false hope, then the vision, and the final question. People enjoy the moment where the hopeful Second Coming is turned inside out. There is a kind of grim thrill in that reversal. The final question, with its rough beast and its Bethlehem, is among the most haunting endings in poetry.

Fellow poets have been especially admiring. T. S. Eliot, who was in many ways Yeats's rival, called him the greatest poet of their time, and many writers who disagreed with Yeats's politics or his mysticism have admired his craft. Poets point to this poem as a model of how to blend the personal and the public, how to write about history without losing the force of a lyric voice. Generations of young poets have learned from its economy.

Another reason people value the poem is that it expresses a feeling many ordinary readers know but can rarely put into words, the sense that the world has become too loud, too angry and too unstable. A line like the best lack all conviction can be oddly comforting. It tells the reader that the confusion is not personal, and that others have felt it. In that sense, the poem works as a kind of company in dark times.

Finally, readers are drawn to its mystery. Even after hundreds of readings, the beast remains unexplained. What is it? Is it a god, a system, a war, or an idea? The poem refuses to say, and that refusal is part of why it fascinates. It leaves room for everyone's fears, which is perhaps the secret of its long life.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

For all its fame, The Second Coming has attracted real and serious criticism, much of it from readers who admire its craft.

The most common objection is political. Yeats had aristocratic sympathies and a distrust of democracy and the masses, and some readers see this in the poem. The line about the best and the worst has been read as snobbery, with the best standing for a cultivated elite and the worst for the common people. Over time, critics have noted that Yeats flirted in the 1930s with authoritarian ideas, including a brief and awkward involvement with the Irish Blueshirts movement and some admiration for strong-man politics, which makes some readers uneasy about the poem's apparent longing for order. The poem has been quoted by people across the political spectrum, but some critics argue that its worldview is basically conservative and fearful of change.

Another objection concerns the poem's reliance on private mythology. Critics have complained that Yeats's system, with its gyres and cycles, is eccentric and unconvincing, and that the poem is only fully intelligible to someone who has studied A Vision. Yeats himself treated the system partly as scaffolding for his imagination, and some critics say that it adds mystery without adding meaning. The poem's best lines, they argue, work in spite of the system rather than because of it.

There is also a view that the poem has been misused, that people quote the line about the best lacking conviction without thinking about the rest. The most quoted lines are taken out of their context, and commentators across the political spectrum have used them to claim that their opponents are the passionate and ignorant and that they themselves are the thoughtful best. Critics see this as a misuse that flattens a complex vision into a slogan.

Postcolonial readers have raised a different challenge. The poem's picture of a world coming apart is rooted in a particular European sense of crisis, the fall of an order that Yeats cherished, and critics note that for many peoples, the collapse of that order was liberation rather than disaster. Achebe, who borrowed the poem's phrase for his novel, used it to tell a story from the other side of the same history, and this can be read as a quiet rebuke to the poem's viewpoint.

Finally, some critics find the poem's tone too apocalyptic, even melodramatic. A vast statement about civilisation, delivered in oracular tones, can seem to claim too much. A poem that sees the end of the world in every crisis may risk crying wolf. These are criticisms of a poem that nobody doubts is powerful, but they are part of how it should be read, with admiration and with a clear head.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Yeats stands at the hinge of modern poetry. Born in 1865, he began as a late Romantic and a leader of the Irish literary revival, writing misty, musical verse about Irish legend. By the time of The Second Coming he had become something harder and more direct, and he helped create modernism without ever fully joining its experiments. This poem belongs to High Modernism, the movement of the 1910s and 1920s that responded to a world shattered by war, with poems that were compressed, allusive and unsparing. It stands next to Eliot's The Waste Land, Pound's Cantos and Wilfred Owen's war poems as a defining work of the postwar mood.

It descends from several traditions. Its title comes from the New Testament's promise that Christ will return, from the Gospels and the Book of Revelation, and the poem twists that promise on purpose. The beast has echoes of the Book of Revelation's monsters. The gyres and the cycles of history come from older ideas, from Plato and the philosophers of cyclical time, and especially from Blake, whom Yeats had edited and studied closely. The poem's image of a monstrous birth can be read as a dark answer to Blake's The Tyger, which also asks what kind of hand made something terrible. Shelley's Ozymandias, with its desert and its colossal, pitiless face, is another ancestor. The sphinx in the sand has the same lonely emptiness as the ruined king.

What descends from it is a tradition of the poem as political prophecy. Auden, Seamus Heaney and many others, in works about war and political breakdown, followed Yeats's example of how a poem could confront public disaster without becoming a pamphlet. In prose, Achebe and Didion turned its phrases into titles and used them to frame entire books. In popular culture, the image of the rough beast appears whenever a story needs a symbol of coming catastrophe.

It is worth comparing it with Eliot's The Waste Land. Both poems came from the aftermath of the war, and both see civilisation as broken. But Eliot gives us fragments, a collage of voices, and Yeats gives us one clear, commanding picture. Where Eliot is a historian of debris, Yeats is a prophet of what comes next. Compared with the quiet reflection of Frost's woods, Yeats's poem is loud, but both are meditations on a point where an old order is about to end.

Why does it still matter? Because its central fear, that the structures we depend on can fail without warning and that the people who might fix things are the ones least confident, has never gone out of date. Each generation reads the poem and finds that it speaks to it. There is also a deeper reason, which is that it is a model of how to look at something terrible without turning away. It does not offer comfort, and it does not offer a solution. It offers a clear and unforgettable look at the dark, and that is a rare and valuable thing.
