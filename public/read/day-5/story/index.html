The Gift of the Magi

O. Henry (1905) • 10 min read

“Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are wisest.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Gift of the Magi was published in 1905 and collected in 1906 in a book called The Four Million. Its author, who wrote as O. Henry, was really William Sydney Porter, a Southerner who had worked as a pharmacist, a bank teller and a newspaper humorist. He was convicted of embezzlement from a Texas bank and served time in prison, where he began to publish stories under his pen name. After his release he moved to New York and wrote hundreds of stories about ordinary city people, the shopgirls, clerks and wanderers he saw around him. The title of his book is a reply to a society snob who said only four hundred people in New York mattered. O. Henry said the other four million mattered too. This Christmas story is his most beloved. It is about a young couple with almost no money, two gifts, and a twist of fate, and it is told with a wink and a great deal of warmth.

One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. And sixty cents of it was in pennies. Pennies saved one and two at a time by bulldozing the grocer and the vegetable man and the butcher until one’s cheeks burned with the silent imputation of parsimony that such close dealing implied. Three times Della counted it. One dollar and eighty-seven cents. And the next day would be Christmas.

There was clearly nothing to do but flop down on the shabby little couch and howl. So Della did it. Which instigates the moral reflection that life is made up of sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating. While the mistress of the home is gradually subsiding from the first stage to the second, take a look at the home. A furnished flat at $8 per week. It did not exactly beggar description, but it certainly had that word on the lookout for the mendicancy squad. In the vestibule below was a letter-box into which no letter would go, and an electric button from which no mortal finger could coax a ring. Also appertaining thereunto was a card bearing the name “Mr. James Dillingham Young.” The “Dillingham” had been flung to the breeze during a former period of prosperity when its possessor was being paid $30 per week. Now, when the income was shrunk to $20, though, they were thinking seriously of contracting to a modest and unassuming D. But whenever Mr. James Dillingham Young came home and reached his flat above he was called “Jim” and greatly hugged by Mrs. James Dillingham Young, already introduced to you as Della. Which is all very good.

Della finished her cry and attended to her cheeks with the powder rag. She stood by the window and looked out dully at a gray cat walking a gray fence in a gray backyard. Tomorrow would be Christmas Day, and she had only $1.87 with which to buy Jim a present. She had been saving every penny she could for months, with this result. Twenty dollars a week doesn’t go far. Expenses had been greater than she had calculated. They always are. Only $1.87 to buy a present for Jim. Her Jim. Many a happy hour she had spent planning for something nice for him. Something fine and rare and sterling—something just a little bit near to being worthy of the honor of being owned by Jim.

There was a pier-glass between the windows of the room. Suddenly Della whirled from the window and stood before the glass. Her eyes were shining brilliantly, but her face had lost its color within twenty seconds. Rapidly she pulled down her hair and let it fall to its full length. Now, there were two possessions of the James Dillingham Youngs in which they both took a mighty pride. One was Jim’s gold watch that had been his father’s and his grandfather’s. The other was Della’s hair. Had the queen of Sheba lived in the flat across the airshaft, Della would have let her hair hang out the window some day to dry just to depreciate Her Majesty’s jewels and gifts. Had King Solomon been the janitor, with all his treasures piled up in the basement, Jim would have pulled out his watch every time he passed, just to see him pluck at his beard from envy.

So now Della’s beautiful hair fell about her rippling and shining like a cascade of brown waters. It reached below her knee and made itself almost a garment for her. And then she did it up again nervously and quickly. Once she faltered for a minute and stood still while a tear or two splashed on the worn red carpet. On went her old brown jacket; on went her old brown hat. With a whirl of skirts and with the brilliant sparkle still in her eyes, she fluttered out the door and down the stairs to the street.

Where she stopped the sign read: “Mme. Sofronie. Hair Goods of All Kinds.” One flight up Della ran, and collected herself, panting. Madame, large, too white, chilly, hardly looked the “Sofronie.” “Will you buy my hair?” asked Della. “I buy hair,” said Madame. “Take your hat off and let’s have a sight at the looks of it.” Down rippled the brown cascade. “Twenty dollars,” said Madame, lifting the mass with a practised hand. “Give it to me quick,” said Della. Oh, and the next two hours tripped by on rosy wings. Forget the hashed metaphor. She was ransacking the stores for Jim’s present.

She found it at last. It surely had been made for Jim and no one else. There was no other like it in any of the stores, and she had turned all of them inside out. It was a platinum fob chain simple and chaste in design, properly proclaiming its value by substance alone and not by meretricious ornamentation—as all good things should do. It was even worthy of The Watch. As soon as she saw it she knew that it must be Jim’s. It was like him. Quietness and value—the description applied to both. Twenty-one dollars they took from her for it, and she hurried home with the 87 cents. With that chain on his watch Jim might be properly anxious about the time in any company. Grand as the watch was, he sometimes looked at it on the sly on account of the old leather strap that he used in place of a chain.

When Della reached home her intoxication gave way a little to prudence and reason. She got out her curling irons and lighted the gas and went to work repairing the ravages made by generosity added to love. Which is always a tremendous task, dear friends—a mammoth task. Within forty minutes her head was covered with tiny, close-lying curls that made her look wonderfully like a truant schoolboy. She looked at her reflection in the mirror long, carefully, and critically. “If Jim doesn’t kill me,” she said to herself, “before he takes a second look at me, he’ll say I look like a Coney Island chorus girl. But what could I do—oh! what could I do with a dollar and eighty-seven cents?”

At 7 o’clock the coffee was made and the frying-pan was on the back of the stove hot and ready to cook the chops. Jim was never late. Della doubled the fob chain in her hand and sat on the corner of the table near the door that he always entered. Then she heard his step on the stair away down on the first flight, and she turned white for just a moment. She had a habit of saying a little silent prayer about the simplest everyday things, and now she whispered: “Please God, make him think I am still pretty.”

The door opened and Jim stepped in and closed it. He looked thin and very serious. Poor fellow, he was only twenty-two—and to be burdened with a family! He needed a new overcoat and he was without gloves. Jim stopped inside the door, as immovable as a setter at the scent of quail. His eyes were fixed upon Della, and there was an expression in them that she could not read, and it terrified her. It was not anger, nor surprise, nor disapproval, nor horror, nor any of the sentiments that she had been prepared for. He simply stared at her fixedly with that peculiar expression on his face. Della wriggled off the table and went for him. “Jim, darling,” she cried, “don’t look at me that way. I had my hair cut off and sold because I couldn’t have lived through Christmas without giving you a present. It’ll grow out again—you won’t mind, will you? I just had to do it. My hair grows awfully fast. Say ‘Merry Christmas!’ Jim, and let’s be happy. You don’t know what a nice—what a beautiful, nice gift I’ve got for you.”

“You’ve cut off your hair?” asked Jim, laboriously, as if he had not arrived at that patent fact yet even after the hardest mental labor. “Cut it off and sold it,” said Della. “Don’t you like me just as well, anyhow? I’m me without my hair, ain’t I?” Jim looked about the room curiously. “You say your hair is gone?” he said, with an air almost of idiocy. “You needn’t look for it,” said Della. “It’s sold, I tell you—sold and gone, too. It’s Christmas Eve, boy. Be good to me, for it went for you. Maybe the hairs of my head were numbered,” she went on with a sudden serious sweetness, “but nobody could ever count my love for you. Shall I put the chops on, Jim?”

Out of his trance Jim seemed quickly to wake. He enfolded his Della. For ten seconds let us regard with discreet scrutiny some inconsequential object in the other direction. Eight dollars a week or a million a year—what is the difference? A mathematician or a wit would give you the wrong answer. The magi brought valuable gifts, but that was not among them. This dark assertion will be illuminated later on. Jim drew a package from his overcoat pocket and threw it upon the table. “Don’t make any mistake, Dell,” he said, “about me. I don’t think there’s anything in the way of a haircut or a shave or a shampoo that could make me like my girl any less. But if you’ll unwrap that package you may see why you had me going a while at first.”

White fingers and nimble tore at the string and paper. And then an ecstatic scream of joy; and then, alas! a quick feminine change to hysterical tears and wails, necessitating the immediate employment of all the comforting powers of the lord of the flat. For there lay The Combs—the set of combs, side and back, that Della had worshipped long in a Broadway window. Beautiful combs, pure tortoise shell, with jewelled rims—just the shade to wear in the beautiful vanished hair. They were expensive combs, she knew, and her heart had simply craved and yearned over them without the least hope of possession. And now, they were hers, but the tresses that should have adorned the coveted adornments were gone. But she hugged them to her bosom, and at length she was able to look up with dim eyes and a smile and say: “My hair grows so fast, Jim!”

And then Della leaped up like a little singed cat and cried, “Oh, oh!” Jim had not yet seen his beautiful present. She held it out to him eagerly upon her open palm. The dull precious metal seemed to flash with a reflection of her bright and ardent spirit. “Isn’t it a dandy, Jim? I hunted all over town to find it. You’ll have to look at the time a hundred times a day now. Give me your watch. I want to see how it looks on it.” Instead of obeying, Jim tumbled down on the couch and put his hands under the back of his head and smiled. “Dell,” said he, “let’s put our Christmas presents away and keep ’em a while. They’re too nice to use just at present. I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs. And now suppose you put the chops on.” The magi, as you know, were wise men—wonderfully wise men—who brought gifts to the Babe in the manger. They invented the art of giving Christmas presents. Being wise, their gifts were no doubt wise ones, possibly bearing the privilege of exchange in case of duplication. And here I have lamely related to you the uneventful chronicle of two foolish children in a flat who most unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house. But in a last word to the wise of these days let it be said that of all who give gifts these two were the wisest. Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are wisest. Everywhere they are wisest. They are the magi.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story is simple to summarize. It is the day before Christmas, and Della Young has one dollar and eighty-seven cents with which to buy a present for her husband, Jim. She has saved it penny by penny, bargaining with grocers and butchers until her cheeks burned. They live in a shabby furnished flat. Jim's wages have been cut from thirty dollars a week to twenty, and the name Dillingham on their mailbox seems to belong to a more prosperous past.

The couple each have one treasure. Jim owns a gold watch that was his father's and grandfather's. Della has her hair, a cascade of brown waves that falls below her knees, more splendid, the narrator says, than the jewels of the Queen of Sheba. Della cries, then rises, puts on her hat and goes to a shop where Madame Sofronie buys hair. She sells her hair for twenty dollars. Then she searches the stores for the perfect gift and finds a simple platinum chain for Jim's watch, costing twenty-one dollars. It is worthy of the watch, quiet and valuable. She buys it and goes home with eighty-seven cents.

Jim comes home and stops in the doorway, staring at her short hair. She is frightened, but he is not angry. He simply cannot seem to understand. Then he gives her his own gift: a set of beautiful combs, the tortoise-shell kind with jeweled rims, that she had long admired in a shop window and which she can now no longer use. Della consoles herself and holds out the chain. Jim says that he sold his watch to buy the combs.

The narrator then steps back to deliver the moral. The magi, he reminds us, were the wise men who brought gifts to the baby in the manger, and they invented the custom of giving Christmas presents. Della and Jim, he says, were two foolish children who unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house. But in a last word to the wise of these days, let it be said that of all who give gifts, these two were the wisest.

The deeper meaning is a paradox. Their gifts are now useless, and by ordinary measures the exchange is a failure. Yet the sacrifice is the real gift, since each valued the other more than the possession they gave up. The story argues that love is measured not by what is received but by what is surrendered. A first-time reader may miss the quiet humor and the sly use of the word wise, which makes the story's foolishness its wisdom.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Gift of the Magi is a model of charm and economy, but it is also a story whose faults are as visible as its virtues. Start with what works. The first line, one dollar and eighty-seven cents, followed by that was all, is one of the best openings in American short fiction. It is concrete and funny and heartbreaking at once, and it sets the tone: a poor household described with a smile. O. Henry's gift is for the voice. The narrator chats with us, jokes, interrupts himself with asides, and talks about sniffles, sobs and smiles in the manner of a good-natured friend telling a tale across a table. The comic exaggeration, such as the comparison of Della's hair with the treasures of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, keeps the sentiment from becoming syrup.

The structure is tight. Each gift is set up with its own small preparation, the watch and the hair, so that when the reversal comes we feel it as an inevitability and a surprise at once. The twist is not hidden but built into the symmetry of the plot, and the narrator trusts it so much that he spends only a few lines on the reveal before moving to the moral.

Where it falters is in its sentimentality and its self-satisfaction. The narrator tells us what to feel, and does so at length, particularly in the final paragraph about the magi. Some readers find that closing lecture the weakest part, since the story had already done its work and the explanation weakens it. The prose also leans on a kind of knowing, ornate humor, with Latinate flourishes and mock-grand diction, which can feel mannered, even wearisome, to modern ears.

The characters are also thin. Della is a lovely girl who weeps and bustles, and Jim is a quiet young man who stares. They are lovable, but they are types, and the story gives them little inner life. The plot also depends on a coincidence that is slightly too perfect: that both would choose precisely the gift that makes the other's useless. It is an artifice, and a very pleasing one, but it is artifice all the same.

Still, the story's charm survives these objections. Its sentiment is earned by humor and by honest details, such as the pennies saved from the butcher and the way Della powders her nose and tries to look like a truant schoolboy. What could be mawkish becomes warm, and the best moments are the small ones, when a very ordinary couple is shown to be quietly heroic.

It is also worth noticing how fast the story moves. Almost nothing is wasted: the money, the shop, the chain, the combs and the reveal follow one another in a few brisk pages, and the pace carries the reader past the coincidences before they have time to object. That momentum is a real skill, and it is one reason the story feels lighter than its sentiment might suggest.

III. The Impact It Had

The Gift of the Magi first appeared in a New York newspaper's Sunday magazine in December 1905 and then in The Four Million the following year. O. Henry was by then a literary celebrity, writing a story a week for the newspapers, and this one became his signature piece. He died in 1910, at forty-seven, worn out by drink and overwork, but the story had already begun its long afterlife.

Its first and greatest impact has been on Christmas itself. Along with Dickens's A Christmas Carol, it has become one of the standard stories of the season in the English-speaking world, read aloud in homes and schools, reprinted every December in newspapers, and performed on stage. The image of the young couple who sacrifice their treasures has become a shorthand for selfless love at the holiday. The very phrase gift of the magi is used to describe any act of ironic self-sacrifice, and it is common for people to refer to it when a present goes awry in a touching way.

It has been adapted for the stage, as a musical and as a one-act play, for radio, television, ballet and opera, and it was one of the stories collected in the 1952 film anthology O. Henry's Full House. It has been retold in modern settings, parodied in cartoons and comedies, and borrowed by countless sitcoms that feature a Christmas episode in which two characters unwittingly sacrifice for one another.

The story's influence on the short story is also real. O. Henry made the surprise ending a signature of American magazine fiction, and his name is attached to a famous annual collection of short stories, the O. Henry Awards, established in 1919. A generation of popular writers learned the craft of the twist from him. His affection for ordinary people, shopgirls, clerks and strivers, and his belief that they were as worthy of literature as the rich, helped widen the cast of American fiction.

It is also a staple of the classroom, often used to teach irony, symbolism and point of view. Students are asked to consider the meaning of the title, the contrast between material value and emotional value, and the way the narrator's voice shapes our response. For many readers, it is the first story that showed them how an ending can change the meaning of everything before it.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Gift of the Magi, above all, for its heart. It offers a vision of love that is generous and unselfish, and it does so without preaching. Two people, each with almost nothing, give up the one thing they cherish, and the story lets us feel the warmth of that act and the sting of its irony in the same moment. For many, it is a story to which they return each December, like a carol.

They have loved the humor. O. Henry's narrator is an entertainer, quick with a joke and a grand mock-solemn phrase, and he never lets the sweetness go flat. When he tells us that Jim was only twenty-two and already burdened with a family, we smile. The mixture of laughter and tears is one of the story's trademarks, and readers value it because life, too, is usually a mixture of the two.

There is a deep affection for the setting. The shabby flat, the mailbox that will not take a letter, the doorbell that will not ring, the frying-pan on the stove ready for chops, are all drawn with a sure, loving touch. Readers see themselves and their own early years in those details, the years of tight budgets and small celebrations. In an age when Christmas can seem a festival of buying, the story offers a pointed reminder of what giving really means.

The story's neatness has pleased readers who enjoy a satisfying shape. The symmetry, the double sacrifice, the two useless gifts, is the kind of elegance that makes people want to retell a story. It is easily summed up in a sentence and remembered for a lifetime.

Finally, the closing paragraph, though debated, has moved many. The claim that of all who give and receive gifts, those who sacrifice for love are the wisest, has been quoted in sermons, cards and speeches. It turns what looks like a mistake into a triumph, and invites the reader to feel that tenderness is wisdom. That is a comforting thought, and the story presents it with just enough irony to be believable.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most frequent criticism of The Gift of the Magi is sentimentality. Some readers find the story too sweet, too eager to pull at the heart, and too confident that love conquers poverty. The narrator's final moralizing, in which he explains the point as if we had missed it, has seemed to many critics an over-insistence that undercuts the delicacy of the tale. O. Henry's reputation in general has suffered from this charge, and he has often been placed below the more austere masters of the short story.

A second criticism concerns the gimmick. The twist ending, which made O. Henry famous, has been described by some as a trick rather than a revelation. Critics of the surprise ending argue that it makes fiction feel like a puzzle, with every detail serving the punchline, so that characters become pieces in a mechanism. Later writers such as Joyce, and Chekhov before him, favored quieter, more open endings, and in comparison O. Henry can look artificial.

There is a social objection as well. Some modern readers note that the story romanticizes poverty. It presents a couple with next to nothing as being rich in love, and that picture can seem a little too comfortable, as though hardship were a charming condition. The story does not dwell on the real difficulties of a cut in wages, such as hunger or fear of eviction, and it passes quickly over the cruelty of a world that can leave a young couple with a dollar and eighty-seven cents.

Others have raised questions about gender. Della is the one who makes the more visible sacrifice, selling something that is an important part of her appearance, in a story that treats a woman's hair as her chief glory. Some readers find the portrayal of Della, emotional, tearful and delighted by small things, a little patronizing, and they note that Jim's sacrifice is described with far less detail.

Finally, there is simple overexposure. The story is so often reprinted and parodied that it can feel like a greeting card. Critics have sometimes described O. Henry as the writer of an earlier time whose style, with its chatty asides and elaborate diction, has dated. None of this has diminished the story's popularity, but it is part of the honest picture. It is loved, and it is also a story that sophisticated readers sometimes feel they have outgrown.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Gift of the Magi belongs to the American short story tradition of the turn of the twentieth century, a period when magazines and newspapers created a mass audience for fiction. Within that world, O. Henry is the great figure of the twist and the sentimental urban tale, and he stands at the popular end of a spectrum whose other end includes Henry James and Stephen Crane. He drew from the humorists of the American South and West, such as Mark Twain, with their tall tales and comic exaggeration, and from the French master Maupassant, who had perfected the ironic ending a generation earlier. It is no accident that The Necklace and this story are often paired, since both turn on a piece of jewelry and a reversal of fortune, though the spirit is entirely different: Maupassant's story is bleak, and O. Henry's is tender.

The story also draws on a much older source, the biblical account of the magi who brought gold, frankincense and myrrh to the infant Jesus. O. Henry's joke is to claim that his foolish young couple are the true heirs of the wise men. It is a playful use of scripture that also expresses a genuine moral belief: that giving is the heart of the holiday.

In terms of Christmas literature, it stands beside Dickens's A Christmas Carol and, later, stories like Dylan Thomas's A Child's Christmas in Wales, as part of the canon of seasonal tales that people read every year. Where Dickens preaches generosity to the rich, O. Henry celebrates generosity among the poor. The two are complementary.

Its descendants include the many stories, films and television episodes that build a plot around an ironic exchange. The idea of two people independently sacrificing for each other has become a common comic and dramatic device. The twist ending itself, as developed by O. Henry, flowed into the pulp magazines, the radio plays and eventually the television anthologies of the twentieth century.

Why should it still be read? Partly because it is a perfect small machine, ten minutes long, which does exactly what it promises. But also because its central proposition remains challenging. In a culture that measures love in purchases, the story proposes that the most valuable gift is the willingness to give up something dear. We may find it sentimental, and we may notice its flaws, but it is hard to remain unmoved by the last image of two young people with useless gifts and full hearts. That endurance is the best evidence that O. Henry, for all his tricks, understood something true.
