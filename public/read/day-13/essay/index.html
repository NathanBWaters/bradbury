Nightly Essay • Romantic Personal & Travel Essay

On Going a Journey

William Hazlitt (1822) • 9 min read

One of the pleasantest things in the world is going a journey; but I like to go by myself: Hazlitt's exuberant celebration of solitary walking, country inns, and throwing off our city identity.

Key Context & Why It Matters

On Going a Journey, by William Hazlitt, was published in January 1822 and collected that same year in his book Table-Talk. Hazlitt was one of the finest English essayists, a former painter turned critic and political radical, quick to argue and slow to compromise. In this piece he sets aside argument and simply tells you what he loves about taking a trip: going alone. Walking, he says, is for thinking, and conversation spoils it. The essay is full of vivid, almost greedy pleasure, in open roads, evening inns, supper plans and cheap books read by candlelight, and it ends with a sly remark about travel abroad. It is often described as the classic piece of writing about solitude on the road, and it helped create the modern idea of the walking trip as a kind of freedom. Listen for the energy of his sentences, and notice how a man who claims to want no company writes as though he is talking directly to you.

One of the pleasantest things in the world is going a journey; but I like to go by myself. I can enjoy society in a room; but out of doors, nature is company enough for me. I am then never less alone than when alone. “The fields his study, nature was his book.” I cannot see the wit of walking and talking at the same time. When I am in the country I wish to vegetate like the country. I am not for criticizing hedge-rows and black cattle. I go out of town in order to forget the town and all that is in it. There are those who for this purpose go to watering-places, and carry the metropolis with them. I like more elbow-room and fewer incumbrances. I like solitude, when I give myself up to it, for the sake of solitude; nor do I ask for “a friend in my retreat, Whom I may whisper solitude is sweet.”

The soul of a journey is liberty, perfect liberty, to think, feel, do, just as one pleases. We go a journey chiefly to be free of all impediments and of all inconveniences; to leave ourselves behind, much more to get rid of others. It is because I want a little breathing-space to muse on indifferent matters, where Contemplation “May plume her feathers and let grow her wings, That in the various bustle of resort Were all too ruffled, and sometimes impair'd,” that I absent myself from the town for a while, without feeling at a loss the moment I am left by myself. Instead of a friend in a post-chaise or in a Tilbury, to exchange good things with, and vary the same stale topics over again, for once let me have a truce with impertinence. Give me the clear blue sky over my head, and the green turf beneath my feet, a winding road before me, and a three hours' march to dinner—and then to thinking! It is hard if I cannot start some game on these lone heaths. I laugh, I run, I leap, I sing for joy. From the point of yonder rolling cloud I plunge into my past being, and revel there, as the sun-burnt Indian plunges headlong into the wave that wafts him to his native shore. Then long-forgotten things, like “sunken wrack and sumless treasuries,” burst upon my eager sight, and I begin to feel, think, and be myself again. Instead of an awkward silence, broken by attempts at wit or dull common-places, mine is that undisturbed silence of the heart which alone is perfect eloquence.

No one likes puns, alliterations, antitheses, argument, and analysis better than I do; but I sometimes had rather be without them. “Leave, oh, leave me to my repose!” I have just now other business in hand, which would seem idle to you, but is with me “very stuff of the conscience.” Is not this wild rose sweet without a comment? Does not this daisy leap to my heart set in its coat of emerald? Yet if I were to explain to you the circumstance that has so endeared it to me, you would only smile. Had I not better then keep it to myself, and let it serve me to brood over, from here to yonder craggy point, and from thence onward to the far-distant horizon? I should be but bad company all that way, and therefore prefer being alone. I have heard it said that you may, when the moody fit comes on, walk or ride on by yourself, and indulge your reveries. But this looks like a breach of manners, a neglect of others, and you are thinking all the time that you ought to rejoin your party. “Out upon such half-faced fellowship,” say I. I like to be either entirely to myself, or entirely at the disposal of others; to talk or be silent, to walk or sit still, to be sociable or solitary. I was pleased with an observation of Mr. Cobbett's, that “he thought it a bad French custom to drink our wine with our meals, and that an Englishman ought to do only one thing at a time.” So I cannot talk and think, or indulge in melancholy musing and lively conversation by fits and starts.

“Let me have a companion of my way,” says Sterne, “were it but to remark how the shadows lengthen as the sun declines.” It is beautifully said; but, in my opinion, this continual comparing of notes interferes with the involuntary impression of things upon the mind, and hurts the sentiment. If you only hint what you feel in a kind of dumb show, it is insipid: if you have to explain it, it is making a toil of a pleasure. You cannot read the book of nature without being perpetually put to the trouble of translating it for the benefit of others. I am for this synthetical method on a journey in preference to the analytical. I am content to lay in a stock of ideas then, and to examine and anatomise them afterwards. I want to see my vague notions float like the down of the thistle before the breeze, and not to have them entangled in the briars and thorns of controversy. For once, I like to have it all my own way; and this is impossible unless you are alone, or in such company as I do not covet. I have no objection to argue a point with any one for twenty miles of measured road, but not for pleasure. If you remark the scent of a bean-field crossing the road, perhaps your fellow-traveller has no smell. If you point to a distant object, perhaps he is short-sighted, and has to take out his glass to look at it. There is a feeling in the air, a tone in the colour of a cloud, which hits your fancy, but the effect of which you are unable to account for. There is then no sympathy, but an uneasy craving after it, and a dissatisfaction which pursues you on the way, and in the end probably produces ill-humour. Now I never quarrel with myself, and take all my own conclusions for granted till I find it necessary to defend them against objections.

In general, a good thing spoils out-of-door prospects: it should be reserved for Table-talk. Lamb is for this reason, I take it, the worst company in the world out of doors; because he is the best within. I grant, there is one subject on which it is pleasant to talk on a journey; and that is, what one shall have for supper when we get to our inn at night. The open air improves this sort of conversation or friendly altercation, by setting a keener edge on appetite. Every mile of the road heightens the flavour of the viands we expect at the end of it. How fine it is to enter some old town, walled and turreted, just at approach of nightfall, or to come to some straggling village, with the lights streaming through the surrounding gloom; and then, after inquiring for the best entertainment that the place affords, to “take one's ease at one's inn”! These eventful moments in our lives' history are too precious, too full of solid, heart-felt happiness to be frittered and dribbled away in imperfect sympathy. I would have them all to myself, and drain them to the last drop: they will do to talk of or to write about afterwards. What a delicate speculation it is, after drinking whole goblets of tea—“The cups that cheer, but not inebriate,”—and letting the fumes ascend into the brain, to sit considering what we shall have for supper—eggs and a rasher, a rabbit smothered in onions, or an excellent veal-cutlet! Sancho in such a situation once fixed upon cow-heel; and his choice, though he could not help it, is not to be disparaged. Then, in the intervals of pictured scenery and Shandean contemplation, to catch the preparation and the stir in the kitchen. Procul, O procul este profani! These hours are sacred to silence and to musing, to be treasured up in the memory, and to feed the source of smiling thoughts hereafter.

I would not waste them in idle talk; or if I must have the integrity of fancy broken in upon, I would rather it were by a stranger than a friend. A stranger takes his hue and character from the time and place; he is a part of the furniture and costume of an inn. If he is a Quaker, or from the West Riding of Yorkshire, so much the better. I do not even try to sympathise with him, and he breaks no squares. I associate nothing with my travelling companion but present objects and passing events. In his ignorance of me and my affairs, I in a manner forget myself. But a friend reminds one of other things, rips up old grievances, and destroys the abstraction of the scene. He comes in ungraciously between us and our imaginary character. Something is dropped in the course of conversation that gives a hint of your profession and pursuits; or from having some one with you that knows the less sublime portions of your history, it seems that other people do. You are no longer a citizen of the world; but your “unhoused free condition is put into circumscription and confine.” The incognito of an inn is one of its striking privileges—“lord of one's self, uncumber'd with a name.” Oh! it is great to shake off the trammels of the world and of public opinion—to lose our importunate, tormenting, everlasting personal identity in the elements of nature, and become the creature of the moment, clear of all ties—to hold to the universe only by a dish of sweet-breads, and to owe nothing but the score of the evening—and no longer seeking for applause and meeting with contempt, to be known by no other title than the Gentleman in the parlour! One may take one's choice of all characters in this romantic state of uncertainty as to one's real pretensions, and become indefinitely respectable and negatively right-worshipful. We baffle prejudice and disappoint conjecture; and from being so to others, begin to be objects of curiosity and wonder even to ourselves. We are no more those hackneyed common-places that we appear in the world; an inn restores us to the level of nature, and quits scores with society!

I have certainly spent some enviable hours at inns—sometimes when I have been left entirely to myself, and have tried to solve some metaphysical problem, as once at Witham-common, where I found out the proof that likeness is not a case of the association of ideas—at other times, when there have been pictures in the room, as at St. Neot's (I think it was), where I first met with Gribelin's engravings of the Cartoons, into which I entered at once, and at a little inn on the borders of Wales, where there happened to be hanging some of Westall's drawings, which I compared triumphantly (for a theory that I had, not for the admired artist) with the figure of a girl who had ferried me over the Severn, standing up in the boat between me and the twilight—at other times I might mention luxuriating in books, with a peculiar interest in this way, as I remember sitting up half the night to read Paul and Virginia, which I picked up at an inn at Bridgewater, after being drenched in the rain all day; and at the same place I got through two volumes of Madame D'Arblay's Camilla. It was on the tenth of April, 1798, that I sat down to a volume of the New Eloise, at the inn at Llangollen, over a bottle of sherry and a cold chicken. The letter I chose was that in which St. Preux describes his feelings as he first caught a glimpse from the heights of the Jura of the Pays de Vaud, which I had brought with me as a bon bouche to crown the evening with. It was my birth-day, and I had for the first time come from a place in the neighbourhood to visit this delightful spot.

There is undoubtedly a sensation in travelling into foreign parts that is to be had nowhere else; but it is more pleasing at the time than lasting. It is too remote from our habitual associations to be a common topic of discourse or reference, and, like a dream or another state of existence, does not piece into our daily modes of life. It is an animated but a momentary hallucination. It demands an effort to exchange our actual for our ideal identity; and to feel the pulse of our old transports revive very keenly, we must “jump” all our present comforts and connexions. Our romantic and itinerant character is not to be domesticated. Dr. Johnson remarked how little foreign travel added to the facilities of conversation in those who had been abroad. In fact, the time we have spent there is both delightful, and in one sense instructive; but it appears to be cut out of our substantial, downright existence, and never to join kindly on to it. We are not the same, but another, and perhaps more enviable individual, all the time we are out of our own country. We are lost to ourselves, as well as our friends. So the poet somewhat quaintly sings, “Out of my country and myself I go.” Those who wish to forget painful thoughts, do well to absent themselves for a while from the ties and objects that recall them; but we can be said only to fulfil our destiny in the place that gave us birth. I should on this account like well enough to spend the whole of my life in travelling abroad, if I could anywhere borrow another life to spend afterwards at home!

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Hazlitt begins with a blunt declaration: "One of the pleasantest things in the world is going a journey; but I like to go by myself." Everything else in the essay is a defense of that taste, delivered with the confidence of a man who knows he may be called antisocial and does not much care.

His central claim is that the soul of a journey is liberty, "perfect liberty, to think, feel, do, just as one pleases." We do not travel to see sights or to meet people. We travel to leave behind the town, our habits, and even ourselves. A walking companion, however pleasant, interferes. If you notice the scent of a bean-field, your friend may have no sense of smell. If you point at a distant object, he may be short-sighted. Hazlitt does not just dislike chatter; he thinks that explaining a feeling turns pleasure into labor. You cannot read the book of nature, he says, without being constantly put to the trouble of translating it for someone else.

That is what he means by preferring the "synthetical" method to the "analytical." On the road, gather impressions and let them float like thistledown. Afterward, at leisure, take them apart and think them through. It is a theory of how experience works: first absorb, then analyze. The essay is a gentle argument that modern life has this order backwards.

The details are warm and funny. He is willing to talk on the road on exactly one subject, what to have for supper, because the open air sharpens the appetite and every mile improves the meal in imagination. He loves the moment of coming into a walled old town at nightfall and "taking one's ease at one's inn." And he loves the anonymity of that inn. A stranger beside you in the parlor is part of the furniture, but a friend brings your whole history with him. Hazlitt delights in being known as nothing but "the Gentleman in the parlour," free of reputation and obligation, owing nothing but the evening's bill.

The less obvious layer is about identity. Hazlitt was a man whose life was full of public quarrels, political enemies and private disappointment. To be a nameless walker is to be released from being William Hazlitt. The essay's most vivid moments are really about escape from the burdens of self, the "importunate, tormenting, everlasting personal identity."

The final paragraphs turn the idea around. Travel abroad is intoxicating, he admits, but it is a momentary hallucination that does not join onto ordinary life. One becomes another, perhaps more enviable person, then comes home and cannot piece the two lives together. His closing joke is that he would happily spend his whole life travelling abroad, if he could borrow another life to spend afterwards at home. Behind the joke is a serious thought: that we belong to the place we come from, and travel is a vacation from the self, not a replacement.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of prose, the essay is a treat. Hazlitt writes in a style that mixes the casual and the grand: a sentence about a wild rose will be followed by a quotation from Milton and a joke about veal-cutlets. The sentences are long, rolling and propulsive, matching the rhythm of walking, and then suddenly a short one snaps shut, like "I laugh, I run, I leap, I sing for joy." His enthusiasm is infectious, and it is rooted in particular things. We get the three hours' march to dinner, the cups that cheer but not inebriate, the glimpse of lights in a village in the dark, the girl standing up in the ferry boat on the Severn against the twilight. Those images are why the essay feels like a lived experience rather than a thesis.

It is also cleverly constructed. The opening argument, that company ruins a walk, gradually deepens into a statement about self and freedom, and the final turn to foreign travel gives the whole thing an unexpected philosophical edge. The joke at the very end is superb, the sort of finishing touch that makes you reread the piece.

Still, the essay has real flaws. It is extremely self-indulgent. Hazlitt is wonderfully sure of his own taste, and the reader may feel the pleasure of being swept along mixed with a faint irritation at his certainty. Some of his claims are simply too absolute. Is conversation really always a barrier to feeling? Plenty of people find that a shared glance at a view doubles the pleasure. He admits as much indirectly: the most obvious contradiction in the essay is that it is a lively piece of talk addressed to a reader, and he praises the walking-and-silence life in pages crowded with other people's words.

That brings up the quotations. The essay is crowded with them, from Shakespeare, Milton, Sterne and others, often used from memory and not always necessary. In a modern reader this can feel like showing off, or like a barrier. A listener, especially, may lose track of who is speaking in these borrowed lines. The Latin interjection "Procul, O procul este profani," which means roughly keep away, you who are not initiated, is a joke about being left alone, but it does leave the unlearned reader outside.

There is also a narrowness of experience. Hazlitt's journey is a leisurely one for a man with enough money and time to wander and lodge at inns. The poor traveler walking for work is invisible here. And the praise of the inn's anonymity, the thrill of being "indefinitely respectable and negatively right-worshipful," is a joke at the expense of social vanity, but it depends on a man being able to pass as a gentleman.

None of this destroys the essay. It simply means the piece is best taken as a mood and a manifesto, brilliantly expressed, rather than as a balanced account of travel. That, after all, is exactly what its author would say it is.

III. The Impact It Had

On Going a Journey is among the pieces that made Hazlitt's reputation as a writer of the familiar essay, a form in which a lively, opinionated voice talks to the reader about ordinary experience. Table-Talk, the collection where it appeared in 1822, contained several of his best pieces and established him, alongside his friend Charles Lamb, as a master of a style that mixed learning with plain feeling. The essay confirmed that he could write about pleasure as well as he could about politics and painting.

Its longest influence has been on the literature of walking. In the nineteenth century, the cult of the walking tour grew rapidly, and this essay was one of its touchstones. Robert Louis Stevenson, in his own essay Walking Tours in 1876, writes in the same tone of delighted solitude, and he openly admired Hazlitt as a master of English prose. The idea that walking alone is a path to thought, a way of letting the mind loosen, runs from Hazlitt through Stevenson and Leslie Stephen's In Praise of Walking, and on into modern books by writers such as Rebecca Solnit, who has written about the history of walking, and the many contemporary memoirs of long walks and pilgrimages.

It also helped shape the idea of travel as a state of mind. The notion that the real purpose of a journey is not to see monuments but to be free of one's usual role is now so common that we forget it had to be invented. When people today talk about a digital detox, a solo trip or a walk to clear the head, they are speaking a language Hazlitt helped formulate.

Within Hazlitt's own career, the essay marks a high point of the confident, assertive voice that suited the magazine culture of his day. The New Monthly Magazine, where it first appeared, was part of a thriving market for essays, and Hazlitt's pieces for such periodicals made him one of the most widely read prose writers of the 1820s. Even so, he died in relative poverty in 1830, and his reputation went through ups and downs. It took later admirers, including George Orwell and many literary critics, to restore him to a place among the greatest English essayists.

In teaching, it appears in anthologies of English prose and in courses on the essay as a form, usually as an example of how to write about taste and experience with vitality. It is also a favorite among people who simply love walking. Many a hiker has carried a line or two of it in their head, perhaps without knowing where it came from.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love the enthusiasm. It is rare to find a writer so openly delighted by small things: a bowl of tea, the thought of a rabbit smothered in onions, a cheap volume picked up at an inn. Hazlitt lets you in on a secret that most people eventually learn, that happiness is often made of ordinary pleasures attended to with full attention. His sentences carry that attention. When he describes the stir and preparation in the inn's kitchen as a kind of sacred hour, he makes you want to be there.

They also love the honesty about wanting to be alone. Many people feel guilty about needing solitude, especially in a culture that treats sociability as a virtue and solitude as a problem. Hazlitt says boldly that he is never less alone than when alone, and that solitude is not a lack but a fullness. Readers who share that temperament feel suddenly understood. The essay gives them permission, and a vocabulary.

Another source of affection is its portrait of the mind in motion. Hazlitt describes how, on a lonely heath, "long-forgotten things, like sunken wrack and sumless treasuries, burst upon my eager sight," and he begins to "feel, think, and be myself again." Anyone who has found memories rising during a long walk will recognize that. It is one of the best short descriptions in English of how walking loosens thought.

There is also pleasure in the humor, which is sly and self-aware. He teases Lamb, noting that Lamb is the worst company in the world out of doors because he is the best within. He quotes Cobbett's remark that an Englishman should do only one thing at a time. He jokes that Sancho Panza, faced with the choice of supper, picked cow-heel. These touches keep the manifesto from becoming a sermon.

Writers admire the technique of the closing section. The turn to foreign travel, with its subtle suggestion that exotic experience does not truly become part of us, feels both fresh and strangely modern. People who have come home from a long trip and found that it seems like a dream will recognize exactly what he means. And the last sentence, about borrowing another life to spend at home, is a favorite among readers. It is the kind of line that people quote because it compresses a whole attitude into a smile.

Finally, readers love the sense of freedom that the essay gives. It reminds you that you can step out of your usual self for an afternoon, with nothing but a road and a destination of supper.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Hazlitt was a controversial figure in his own time, and much of the hostility toward him was political. He was a lifelong defender of the French Revolution and an admirer of Napoleon at a time when most of the English establishment despised both. Conservative magazines such as Blackwood's and the Quarterly Review attacked him viciously, partly for his radical opinions and partly as a member of what they sneeringly called the Cockney School of writers. Because of that, the reception of his essays was often colored by his politics, and a charming essay on walking could be dismissed because of the author's views.

In more purely literary terms, critics have long noted that Hazlitt is sometimes prolix and careless. He wrote quickly and for money, often quoting from memory, and he repeated himself. On Going a Journey shows some of that. The abundance of quotation, and the sometimes strained flourishes, can be tiresome, and some modern readers feel the essay would be stronger if shorter.

A more philosophical objection concerns its stance. The praise of solitude and the dislike of conversation can seem extreme, even a little selfish. Several readers have pointed out the paradox that Hazlitt, one of the great talkers and arguers of his day, attacks conversation on the road while building a reputation as a brilliant commentator on everything. Some see this as a charming inconsistency, others as a form of posturing. The essay's enthusiasm for the incognito, for being known as no one in particular, sits oddly with a writer who very much wanted to be known and admired.

There is also the objection that the essay is a fantasy of male leisure. The traveler is an educated, unattached man with time to spare, roaming freely and treating others, including the girl at the ferry, as scenery for his reflections. Contemporary readers sometimes remark that women and the working poor appear in these pages only as figures in a landscape. That is not unusual for the period, but it limits how universal the essay's account of freedom can be.

There is a final, more biographical caution. Hazlitt's private life was turbulent. He had a failed marriage and a painful infatuation with a young woman, which he would describe in his book Liber Amoris a year later. Readers who know that story sometimes read the longing for escape in this essay as more desperate than the cheerful surface suggests. Some critics argue that the polished joy conceals loneliness. Whether that makes the essay weaker or deeper is a matter of taste, but it does complicate its advertised serenity.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Hazlitt belongs to the generation of English Romantic writers who valued feeling, nature and the individual voice. He was a younger friend of Coleridge and Wordsworth, whose poetry he admired and whose political drift he came to despise, and he shared with them the conviction that walking in nature was a source of imaginative life. Coleridge and Wordsworth were famous walkers who composed poems on foot, and Hazlitt's own first meeting with Coleridge, which he described in another essay, began a decisive moment in his life. The date he mentions in this essay, the tenth of April, 1798, falls in the very year that Lyrical Ballads, the book that launched English Romantic poetry, was published.

In the essay tradition, he descends from Montaigne, who taught writers that their own taste and moods were worthy subjects, and from the periodical essayists of the eighteenth century such as Addison and Steele. He stands beside Lamb, whose essays are quieter and more disguised, and De Quincey, whose prose is more dreamy. Hazlitt's particular contribution is energy: he writes as though thinking and feeling were athletic.

What descends from him is considerable. The line of walking essays is clear: Stevenson, Leslie Stephen, Thoreau's essay Walking, the travel writing of the twentieth century and today's nature writing. Thoreau, writing in America a generation later, makes a similar claim for walking as a spiritual discipline, though with a more philosophical, even sacred, tone. Hazlitt's version is more convivial and more gourmand: he wants his supper.

It is also worth comparing him with writers who take the opposite side. Dr. Johnson, whom Hazlitt cites, was fond of company and conversation, and James Boswell's biography of him shows an entire philosophy of life built on talk. Sterne, whom Hazlitt quotes saying he wants a companion to remark how the shadows lengthen, represents the more sociable view of travel. Reading Hazlitt alongside them shows how the argument about whether to travel alone or together is really an old argument about what the self needs.

Why does it matter now? In an age of constant connection, when a phone follows you into the wilderness, the essay is oddly urgent. It asks what we are missing when we turn every experience into something to be shared. You do not have to accept all of it. But you may find, after reading it, that the next time you go for a walk you leave your companion, or at least your phone, behind for a mile or two, and notice the scent of a bean-field.
