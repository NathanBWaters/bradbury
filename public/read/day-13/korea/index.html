Suwon Hwaseong Fortress (1794–1796) & Jeong Yak-yong's Geojunggi Pulley Crane

Korea — How King Jeongjo and Silhak polymath Dasan used compound mechanical pulleys, kiln-fired artillery bricks, and fair wage labor to build a modern city in thirty-two months (Late Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Jeongjo (1794–1796)) • 20 min read

“With eight pulleys above and eight pulleys below, a weight of twenty-five thousand geun can be lifted by the strength of just thirty men.” — Jeong Yak-yong (Dasan), Giyeokdoseol (Illustrated Explanation of Cranes, 1792)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Between January 1794 and September 1796, just thirty kilometers south of Seoul in Suwon, the twenty-second Joseon monarch King Jeongjo constructed Hwaseong ('Brilliant Fortress')—a 5.74-kilometer walled reform city built to honor his tragically executed father, Crown Prince Sado, and to establish an autonomous commercial and military capital free from entrenched Seoul court factions. Rejecting traditional unpaid peasant corvée labor, Jeongjo paid every stonecutter and bricklayer daily silver wages, provided summer heat-stroke medicine, and commissioned thirty-year-old Silhak ('Practical Learning') polymath Jeong Yak-yong (Dasan) to engineer the fortress. Using his newly invented Geojunggi compound-pulley crane and hybrid brick-and-granite artillery bastions, Dasan finished a ten-year megaproject in thirty-two months and documented every nail in the UNESCO-inscribed Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe.

Walk along the undulating crest of Mount Paldalsan in central Suwon, and you follow a 5.74-kilometer ribbon of silvery granite blocks and warm gray kiln-fired bricks that hugs the natural ridgeline like a coiled dragon. Forty-eight distinct architectural structures—arched floodgates, multi-tiered corner pavilions, secret sally ports (*ammun*), beacon towers (*bongdon*), and hollow brick artillery bastions (*gongsimdon*)—punctuate the ramparts. This is *Suwon Hwaseong* (수원 화성), built between January 1794 and September 1796 under King Jeongjo, the philosopher-monarch of the eighteenth-century Joseon Renaissance. When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Hwaseong Fortress (Janganmun & Paldalsan), Paldal-gu, Suwon, Gyeonggi Province (37.2882° N, 127.0138° E) to study Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Suwon Hwaseong Fortress shaped every decision made during the Late Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Jeongjo (1794–1796). Nothing in this setting is accidental: the orientation of the ground, the seasonal angle of sunlight, and the acoustic stillness surrounding Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) establish an immediate sensory bridge between twenty-first-century observers and the historical artisans who worked here.

Every stone of Hwaseong was laid in the shadow of a royal trauma that haunted King Jeongjo from childhood. In the scorching summer of 1762, when the future king was only ten years old, violent factional court politics led by the *Noron* party convinced his grandfather, King Yeongjo, to seal Jeongjo’s father—twenty-seven-year-old Crown Prince Sado—inside a wooden rice chest (*뒤주*, *dwiju*) in the courtyard of Changgyeonggung Palace for eight days until he died of thirst and heatstroke. In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Late Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Jeongjo (1794–1796), the rise of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

When Jeongjo ascended the throne in 1776, he declared across the court tablet room: 'I am the son of Crown Prince Sado.' Moving his father’s tomb in 1789 to Mount Hwasan near Suwon—considered by royal geomancers the most auspicious *myeongdang* site in the kingdom—Jeongjo resolved to build a new fortified city at the foot of Paldalsan. Named *Hwaseong* ('Flowering/Brilliant Fortress'), it would serve both as a filial sanctuary where the king could retire beside his father’s grave and as a self-sufficient commercial hub breaking the stranglehold of Seoul’s aristocratic factions. Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Suwon Hwaseong Fortress stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

To design a fortress unlike any built in Korea before, King Jeongjo bypassed elderly conservative mandarins and turned to a thirty-year-old prodigy of the *Silhak* (실학, 'Practical Learning') movement named Jeong Yak-yong, known by his pen name *Dasan* (다산, 1762–1836). Handing Dasan a stack of classical Chinese military treatises alongside the *Qiqi Tushuo* ('Diagrams and Explanations of Wonderful Machines,' a 1627 manual compiled in Beijing by Jesuit polymath Johann Schreck and Wang Zheng that introduced Archimedes screws and Galileo-era European pulley physics), the king asked him to reimagine Korean civil engineering. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) aligned Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

In 1792, Dasan submitted his master plan—the *Seongseol* ('Treatise on the Fortress')—incorporating revolutionary ballistic and structural reforms. Traditional Korean mountain fortresses (*sanseong*) had been built purely of dry-stacked granite to keep enemies out during temporary sieges, leaving urban markets unprotected in the plains below. Conversely, Chinese city walls were built of brick on flat ground. Dasan synthesized both into an integrated *eupseong-sanseong* hybrid: massive dressed granite blocks formed the lower load-bearing foundations, while standardized, kiln-fired gray clay bricks (*벽돌*, *byeokdol*) formed the upper parapets, embrasures, and vaulted observation towers. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Every raw element gathered near Suwon Hwaseong Fortress had to be tested for density, moisture response, and structural resilience before it could be incorporated into Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성), ensuring that the interplay between Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) would withstand centuries of monsoon humidity and freezing winter temperatures.

Why combine granite and brick? When struck by an eighteenth-century cannonball, brittle granite shatters into deadly shrapnel and leaves a gaping structural breach that takes weeks to quarry and re-carve. Kiln-fired clay bricks cemented with lime-oyster-shell mortar (*samhoemul*), by contrast, absorb ballistic impact locally and can be patched overnight by masons laying standard modular bricks. Closer inspection of the primary mechanism reveals a level of quantitative precision that still astonishes contemporary engineers. By calibrating the tolerances of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) and balancing the physical forces acting across Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane), the builders eliminated points of mechanical failure while maximizing the functional efficiency of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) under real-world operating conditions.

Yet moving hundreds of thousands of two-ton granite foundation blocks from the quarries at Seokgyo-ri and Sukji Mountain threatened to consume ten years and exhaust the peasantry. To solve the heavy-lifting bottleneck, Dasan engineered the *Geojunggi* (거중기, 擧重器)—Korea’s first compound mechanical pulley crane. Constructed from stout zelkova and oak timber frames housing four fixed brass pulleys at the top and four movable brass pulleys at the bottom threaded with thick hemp cables wound around twin capstan winches, the *Geojunggi* multiplied human force by a mechanical advantage of eight to ten. Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. When temperature, airflow, or human movement shifted around Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane), the subtle geometry of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) absorbed the stress smoothly, turning potential environmental hazards into sources of long-term stability.

Where lifting a 12,000-*geun* (7.2-ton) granite lintel stone had previously required two hundred men hauling on bamboo poles up earthen ramps—frequently crushing limbs when ropes snapped—Dasan’s *Geojunggi* allowed thirty men turning the twin side windlasses to hoist the stone smoothly into the air. Paired with the *Nokro* (녹로, a tall single-boom rotating crane for lifting stones high onto the gate towers) and the *Yuhyeokcha* (유형거, a broad-wheeled winter sled-cart designed to roll over frozen mud without sinking), these machines saved the royal treasury an estimated 40,000 *nyang* of silver. That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Suwon Hwaseong Fortress. Whenever an early prototype or seasonal cycle threatened to derail Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성), the masters refined their approach to Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and recalibrated Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual), passing hard-won empirical lessons down from mentor to apprentice.

Even more revolutionary than Dasan’s machines was King Jeongjo’s labor law. For two thousand years across East Asia, royal walls and palaces had been built through *buyeok* (부역)—compulsory unpaid peasant corvée labor that pulled farmers away from their fields and sparked rebellions. Under Prime Minister Chae Je-gong, Hwaseong became the first state megaproject in Korean history built entirely with voluntary, performance-paid wage labor (*goyongje*, 고용제). Primary documentary evidence from the Late Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Jeongjo (1794–1796) confirms the extraordinary care devoted to recording these breakthroughs. Scholars examining the surviving texts and inscriptions associated with Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) can trace the exact terminology of Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual), verifying that what once seemed like legend was grounded in rigorous institutional documentation.

Every mason, carpenter, smith, and carter was paid fair silver and rice wages calculated by the exact weight of stone cut and the exact distance hauled. During the blistering July heatwave of 1794, when temperatures soared in Suwon, King Jeongjo ordered construction paused during midday hours and personally dispatched thousands of doses of *Cheongseo-ikgitang* (청서익기탕, an herbal formula of ginseng, astragalus, and schisandra to prevent heat exhaustion) and *Cheokseodan* pills from the Royal Dispensary to every common laborer on the wall. To experience Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. From the tactile grain of Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) beneath the fingers to the rhythmic cadence of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) echoing across Suwon Hwaseong Fortress, every detail reinforced a shared sense of civic and spiritual belonging.

Inspired by dignified wages and humane treatment, the 1,800 master artisans and thousands of paid workers achieved a miracle of productivity. Originally projected to require ten full years of construction (120 months), the entire 5.74-kilometer fortress—including its four monumental gates (*Janganmun*, *Paldalmun*, *Changnyongmun*, and *Hwaseomun*), the seven-arch *Hwahongmun* floodgate spanning the Suwoncheon stream, and the 576-room *Hwaseong Haenggung* temporary palace—was completed in September 1796 after just thirty-two months. Yet history repeatedly tested the survival of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) through the crucible of war, regime change, and rapid modernization. When external upheavals swept across Suwon Hwaseong Fortress, the fragile equilibrium between Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) faced existential threats that could easily have erased centuries of accumulated knowledge in a single generation.

Immediately upon completion, King Jeongjo ordered every detail of the project compiled into a ten-volume, movable-metal-type encyclopedia called the *Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe* (화성성역의궤, 1801). Across 1,300 pages of architectural blueprints, perspective elevations, and accounting ledgers, the *Uigwe* recorded the exact dimensions of the *Geojunggi* pulleys, the names and hometowns of all 1,821 skilled artisans, the recipe for lime mortar, and the cost of every sack of charcoal and iron nail. That Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion. Ordinary guardians, local families, and dedicated researchers risked their livelihoods to shelter Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and preserve the oral and written manuals of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) until a peaceful era could appreciate their full value once more.

That obsessive documentation saved Hwaseong a century and a half later. During the bitter fighting of the Korean War between June 1950 and 1951, aerial bombardment and artillery battles reduced *Janganmun* (the grand northern gate, larger even than Seoul’s Namdaemun), *Changnyongmun*, and over a third of the fortress ramparts to smoking rubble. When modern laboratory scientists and conservation specialists finally examined Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) using non-invasive imaging and chemical analysis, the empirical data validated centuries of traditional wisdom. Measurements of Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and structural modeling of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) revealed optimization strategies that rival twenty-first-century materials science.

When South Korean architects and master craftsmen embarked on the full restoration of Hwaseong between 1975 and 1979, they did not have to guess at a single dimension or brick bond. Opening the 1801 *Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe*, they knew the exact quarry where every granite block had been cut in 1794, the exact firing temperature of the gray bricks, and the exact carpentry joinery of the two-story gate pavilions. Today at Suwon Hwaseong Fortress, a new generation of practitioners and cultural stewards continues to breathe fresh vitality into Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성). By honoring the authentic craft protocols of Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) while introducing contemporary audiences to the philosophy of Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual), they ensure that heritage remains a living workshop rather than a frozen museum relic.

When ICOMOS and UNESCO inspectors evaluated Suwon Hwaseong for the World Heritage List in 1997, several European architectural historians initially doubted whether a fortress largely reconstructed in the 1970s after wartime bombing met the strict international test of 'authenticity.' Yet once Korean scholars presented the 1801 *Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe* alongside the *Oegyujanggak* royal protocols, UNESCO agreed that because every stone and timber had been restored to the millimeter using the original eighteenth-century engineering manual, Hwaseong possessed 100 percent documentary and material authenticity—simultaneously inscribing the fortress on the World Heritage List and the *Uigwe* on the Memory of the World Register. Placed in comparative global perspective, the story of Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) challenges Eurocentric narratives of scientific and artistic history. Long before parallel developments appeared elsewhere, Korean innovators at Suwon Hwaseong Fortress had already synthesized Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane) and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) into a coherent system tailored to human dignity and ecological balance.

Today, if you climb to the top of the spiral brick *Seobuk Gongsimdon* observation tower or stand beneath the rainbow arches of *Hwahongmun* at dusk as willow trees trail into the Suwoncheon stream, you are standing inside the Enlightenment dream of King Jeongjo and Jeong Yak-yong—a city where mechanical science, architectural beauty, and respect for the dignity of working hands were mortared together. 1. Mechanical Physics: Inside Dasan's Geojunggi Compound Pulley System

In his 1792 treatise Giyeokdoseol, Jeong Yak-yong adapted the static pulley theorems of European Renaissance mechanics—transmitted via Jesuit scholar Johann Schreck's 1627 Qiqi Tushuo—to Korean timber and brass metallurgy. By pairing four upper fixed sheaves with four lower movable sheaves and routing the hemp haul lines symmetrically out to two horizontal capstan drums turned by wooden levers, Dasan eliminated lateral tipping torque. Two men turning each capstan could generate 400 kilograms of tension, which the eight-pulley block multiplied into over three metric tons of vertical lift. 2. The 1801 Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe: The Blueprint That Defeated War

Published in November 1801 using 300,000 Jeongnija movable metal type characters, the ten-volume Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe is considered one of the most comprehensive architectural construction reports produced anywhere in the pre-industrial world. Unlike European treatises that recorded idealized architectural theory, the Uigwe documented actual field logistics: exact quarry maps, brick-kiln fuel consumption, daily wage payrolls by trade, medical prescriptions distributed to workers, and exploded isometric drawings of the Geojunggi crane—allowing 1970s restorers to rebuild bombed gates with sub-centimeter fidelity. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Suwon Hwaseong Fortress, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성) to Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual) across the centuries. 3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Three essential terms define this story: first, Silhak (실학, 'Practical Learning'), the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Korean reform movement that championed empirical science, agricultural technology, and social equity over empty metaphysical debate; second, Geojunggi (거중기), Dasan's eight-pulley mechanical crane; and third, Goyongje (고용제), King Jeongjo's historic transition from forced peasant corvée labor to fair, compensated daily wages. Mastering these three Korean terms—Hwaseong Fortress (수원 화성), Geojunggi (거중기 compound-pulley crane), and Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (화성성역의궤 construction manual)—equips any traveler or reader to decode the deeper cultural grammar embedded in the landscape of Suwon Hwaseong Fortress.
