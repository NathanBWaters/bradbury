Suwon Hwaseong Fortress (1794–1796) & Jeong Yak-yong's Geojunggi Pulley Crane

Korea — How King Jeongjo and Silhak polymath Dasan used compound mechanical pulleys, kiln-fired artillery bricks, and fair wage labor to build a modern city in thirty-two months (Late Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Jeongjo (1794–1796)) • 10 min read

“With eight pulleys above and eight pulleys below, a weight of twenty-five thousand geun can be lifted by the strength of just thirty men.” — Jeong Yak-yong (Dasan), Giyeokdoseol (Illustrated Explanation of Cranes, 1792)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Between January 1794 and September 1796, just thirty kilometers south of Seoul in Suwon, the twenty-second Joseon monarch King Jeongjo constructed Hwaseong ('Brilliant Fortress')—a 5.74-kilometer walled reform city built to honor his tragically executed father, Crown Prince Sado, and to establish an autonomous commercial and military capital free from entrenched Seoul court factions. Rejecting traditional unpaid peasant corvée labor, Jeongjo paid every stonecutter and bricklayer daily silver wages, provided summer heat-stroke medicine, and commissioned thirty-year-old Silhak ('Practical Learning') polymath Jeong Yak-yong (Dasan) to engineer the fortress. Using his newly invented Geojunggi compound-pulley crane and hybrid brick-and-granite artillery bastions, Dasan finished a ten-year megaproject in thirty-two months and documented every nail in the UNESCO-inscribed Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe.

Walk along the undulating crest of Mount Paldalsan in central Suwon, and you follow a 5.74-kilometer ribbon of silvery granite blocks and warm gray kiln-fired bricks that hugs the natural ridgeline like a coiled dragon. Forty-eight distinct architectural structures—arched floodgates, multi-tiered corner pavilions, secret sally ports (ammun), beacon towers (bongdon), and hollow brick artillery bastions (gongsimdon)—punctuate the ramparts. This is Suwon Hwaseong, built between January 1794 and September 1796 under King Jeongjo, the philosopher-monarch of the eighteenth-century Joseon Renaissance. Every stone of Hwaseong was laid in the shadow of a royal trauma that haunted King Jeongjo from childhood. In the scorching summer of 1762, when the future king was only ten years old, violent factional court politics led by the Noron party convinced his grandfather, King Yeongjo, to seal Jeongjo’s father—twenty-seven-year-old Crown Prince Sado—inside a wooden rice chest (dwiju) in the courtyard of Changgyeonggung Palace for eight days until he died of thirst and heatstroke.

When Jeongjo ascended the throne in 1776, he declared across the court tablet room: 'I am the son of Crown Prince Sado.' Moving his father’s tomb in 1789 to Mount Hwasan near Suwon—considered by royal geomancers the most auspicious myeongdang site in the kingdom—Jeongjo resolved to build a new fortified city at the foot of Paldalsan. Named Hwaseong ('Flowering/Brilliant Fortress'), it would serve both as a filial sanctuary where the king could retire beside his father’s grave and as a self-sufficient commercial hub breaking the stranglehold of Seoul’s aristocratic factions. To design a fortress unlike any built in Korea before, King Jeongjo bypassed elderly conservative mandarins and turned to a thirty-year-old prodigy of the Silhak (Practical Learning) movement named Jeong Yak-yong, known by his pen name Dasan (1762–1836). Handing Dasan a stack of classical Chinese military treatises alongside the Qiqi Tushuo ('Diagrams and Explanations of Wonderful Machines,' a 1627 manual compiled in Beijing by Jesuit polymath Johann Schreck and Wang Zheng that introduced Archimedes screws and Galileo-era European pulley physics), the king asked him to reimagine Korean civil engineering.

In 1792, Dasan submitted his master plan—the Seongseol ('Treatise on the Fortress')—incorporating revolutionary ballistic and structural reforms. Traditional Korean mountain fortresses (sanseong) had been built purely of dry-stacked granite to keep enemies out during temporary sieges, leaving urban markets unprotected in the plains below. Conversely, Chinese city walls were built of brick on flat ground. Dasan synthesized both into an integrated eupseong-sanseong hybrid: massive dressed granite blocks formed the lower load-bearing foundations, while standardized, kiln-fired gray clay bricks (byeokdol) formed the upper parapets, embrasures, and vaulted observation towers. Why combine granite and brick? When struck by an eighteenth-century cannonball, brittle granite shatters into deadly shrapnel and leaves a gaping structural breach that takes weeks to quarry and re-carve. Kiln-fired clay bricks cemented with lime-oyster-shell mortar (samhoemul), by contrast, absorb ballistic impact locally and can be patched overnight by masons laying standard modular bricks.

Yet moving hundreds of thousands of two-ton granite foundation blocks from the quarries at Seokgyo-ri and Sukji Mountain threatened to consume ten years and exhaust the peasantry. To solve the heavy-lifting bottleneck, Dasan engineered the Geojunggi —Korea’s first compound mechanical pulley crane. Constructed from stout zelkova and oak timber frames housing four fixed brass pulleys at the top and four movable brass pulleys at the bottom threaded with thick hemp cables wound around twin capstan winches, the Geojunggi multiplied human force by a mechanical advantage of eight to ten. Where lifting a 12,000-geun (7.2-ton) granite lintel stone had previously required two hundred men hauling on bamboo poles up earthen ramps—frequently crushing limbs when ropes snapped—Dasan’s Geojunggi allowed thirty men turning the twin side windlasses to hoist the stone smoothly into the air. Paired with the Nokro (a tall single-boom rotating crane for lifting stones high onto the gate towers) and the Yuhyeokcha (a broad-wheeled winter sled-cart designed to roll over frozen mud without sinking), these machines saved the royal treasury an estimated 40,000 nyang of silver.

Even more revolutionary than Dasan’s machines was King Jeongjo’s labor law. For two thousand years across East Asia, royal walls and palaces had been built through buyeok —compulsory unpaid peasant corvée labor that pulled farmers away from their fields and sparked rebellions. Under Prime Minister Chae Je-gong, Hwaseong became the first state megaproject in Korean history built entirely with voluntary, performance-paid wage labor (goyongje). Every mason, carpenter, smith, and carter was paid fair silver and rice wages calculated by the exact weight of stone cut and the exact distance hauled. During the blistering July heatwave of 1794, when temperatures soared in Suwon, King Jeongjo ordered construction paused during midday hours and personally dispatched thousands of doses of Cheongseo-ikgitang (an herbal formula of ginseng, astragalus, and schisandra to prevent heat exhaustion) and Cheokseodan pills from the Royal Dispensary to every common laborer on the wall.

Inspired by dignified wages and humane treatment, the 1,800 master artisans and thousands of paid workers achieved a miracle of productivity. Originally projected to require ten full years of construction (120 months), the entire 5.74-kilometer fortress—including its four monumental gates (Janganmun, Paldalmun, Changnyongmun, and Hwaseomun), the seven-arch Hwahongmun floodgate spanning the Suwoncheon stream, and the 576-room Hwaseong Haenggung temporary palace—was completed in September 1796 after just thirty-two months. Immediately upon completion, King Jeongjo ordered every detail of the project compiled into a ten-volume, movable-metal-type encyclopedia called the Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe (1801). Across 1,300 pages of architectural blueprints, perspective elevations, and accounting ledgers, the Uigwe recorded the exact dimensions of the Geojunggi pulleys, the names and hometowns of all 1,821 skilled artisans, the recipe for lime mortar, and the cost of every sack of charcoal and iron nail.

That obsessive documentation saved Hwaseong a century and a half later. During the bitter fighting of the Korean War between June 1950 and 1951, aerial bombardment and artillery battles reduced Janganmun (the grand northern gate, larger even than Seoul’s Namdaemun), Changnyongmun, and over a third of the fortress ramparts to smoking rubble. When South Korean architects and master craftsmen embarked on the full restoration of Hwaseong between 1975 and 1979, they did not have to guess at a single dimension or brick bond. Opening the 1801 Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe, they knew the exact quarry where every granite block had been cut in 1794, the exact firing temperature of the gray bricks, and the exact carpentry joinery of the two-story gate pavilions.

When ICOMOS and UNESCO inspectors evaluated Suwon Hwaseong for the World Heritage List in 1997, several European architectural historians initially doubted whether a fortress largely reconstructed in the 1970s after wartime bombing met the strict international test of 'authenticity.' Yet once Korean scholars presented the 1801 Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe alongside the Oegyujanggak royal protocols, UNESCO agreed that because every stone and timber had been restored to the millimeter using the original eighteenth-century engineering manual, Hwaseong possessed 100 percent documentary and material authenticity—simultaneously inscribing the fortress on the World Heritage List and the Uigwe on the Memory of the World Register.

Today, if you climb to the top of the spiral brick Seobuk Gongsimdon observation tower or stand beneath the rainbow arches of Hwahongmun at dusk as willow trees trail into the Suwoncheon stream, you are standing inside the Enlightenment dream of King Jeongjo and Jeong Yak-yong—a city where mechanical science, architectural beauty, and respect for the dignity of working hands were mortared together.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Mechanical Physics: Inside Dasan's Geojunggi Compound Pulley System

In his 1792 treatise Giyeokdoseol, Jeong Yak-yong adapted the static pulley theorems of European Renaissance mechanics—transmitted via Jesuit scholar Johann Schreck's 1627 Qiqi Tushuo—to Korean timber and brass metallurgy. By pairing four upper fixed sheaves with four lower movable sheaves and routing the hemp haul lines symmetrically out to two horizontal capstan drums turned by wooden levers, Dasan eliminated lateral tipping torque. Two men turning each capstan could generate 400 kilograms of tension, which the eight-pulley block multiplied into over three metric tons of vertical lift.

2. The 1801 Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe: The Blueprint That Defeated War

Published in November 1801 using 300,000 Jeongnija movable metal type characters, the ten-volume Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe is considered one of the most comprehensive architectural construction reports produced anywhere in the pre-industrial world. Unlike European treatises that recorded idealized architectural theory, the Uigwe documented actual field logistics: exact quarry maps, brick-kiln fuel consumption, daily wage payrolls by trade, medical prescriptions distributed to workers, and exploded isometric drawings of the Geojunggi crane—allowing 1970s restorers to rebuild bombed gates with sub-centimeter fidelity.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Three essential terms define this story: first, Silhak (Practical Learning), the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Korean reform movement that championed empirical science, agricultural technology, and social equity over empty metaphysical debate; second, Geojunggi, Dasan's eight-pulley mechanical crane; and third, Goyongje, King Jeongjo's historic transition from forced peasant corvée labor to fair, compensated daily wages.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Begin at Janganmun Gate, the monumental northern entrance of Hwaseong, and walk east along the parapet past the seven-arch Hwahongmun Watergate and the idyllic Banghwasuryujeong Pavilion perched above Dragon Pond (Yongyeon). Descend into the courtyard of Hwaseong Haenggung Temporary Palace and the adjacent Suwon Hwaseong Museum, where a full-scale working timber-and-brass replica of Jeong Yak-yong's Geojunggi crane stands ready to inspect.
