Nightly Poem • Elizabethan & Metaphysical

The Sun Rising

John Donne (1633) • 4 min read

Scolding the morning sun as a busy old fool peeking through bedroom curtains, John Donne declares that his lovers’ bed already contains all the kingdoms and spices of the earth.

Key Context & Why It Matters

John Donne's The Sun Rising is one of the boldest and funniest love poems in English. A lover lies in bed with his beloved, and the morning sun comes through the curtains to wake them. He is furious, and he tells the sun off like a rude servant. Donne wrote it around the start of the 1600s, a time when poetry was often about worshipping a distant lady in elegant language. Donne did something different. He wrote in the sharp, argumentative voice of a man talking, full of wit, shocks and sudden turns of thought, and he linked love to maps, empires, kings and the new astronomy. His own life gave the poem its edge, since he secretly married Anne More in 1601, which cost him his career and landed him briefly in prison. The poem was printed after his death, in 1633. Its overall meaning is that for lovers, a single room can outweigh the entire world.

Busy old fool, unruly Sun,

Why dost thou thus,

Through windows, and through curtains, call on us?

Must to thy motions lovers’ seasons run?

Saucy pedantic wretch, go chide

Late schoolboys and sour prentices,

Go tell court-huntsmen that the king will ride,

Call country ants to harvest offices;

Love, all alike, no season knows nor clime,

Nor hours, days, months, which are the rags of time.

Thy beams so reverend, and strong

Why shouldst thou think?

I could eclipse and cloud them with a wink,

But that I would not lose her sight so long.

If her eyes have not blinded thine,

Look, and tomorrow late tell me,

Whether both th’ Indias of spice and mine

Be where thou left’st them, or lie here with me.

Ask for those kings whom thou saw’st yesterday,

And thou shalt hear, “All here in one bed lay.”

She’s all states, and all princes I;

Nothing else is;

Princes do but play us; compared to this,

All honour’s mimic, all wealth alchemy.

Thou, Sun, art half as happy as we,

In that the world’s contracted thus;

Thine age asks ease, and since thy duties be

To warm the world, that’s done in warming us.

Shine here to us, and thou art everywhere;

This bed thy centre is, these walls thy sphere.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The Sun Rising is a poem in which a lover picks a fight with the sun, and wins. The setting is a bedroom at dawn. Light is pouring in through the windows and curtains, and the speaker, lying with the woman he loves, is annoyed. The poem is a three-part speech, one part for each stanza, and each part raises the stakes.

In the first stanza he simply scolds. "Busy old fool, unruly Sun, / Why dost thou thus, / Through windows, and through curtains, call on us?" He is treating the sun as if it were a nosy neighbor or an officious servant. Then he demands to know why lovers should have to run on the sun's timetable. He tells it to go and bother people who actually have to get up on time: "Late schoolboys and sour prentices," meaning cranky apprentices, and "court-huntsmen," who must rise when the king goes hunting, and country workers who must get in the harvest. These people live by the clock. Love does not. He sums it up in a famous couplet: "Love, all alike, no season knows nor clime, / Nor hours, days, months, which are the rags of time." Calling hours and days "rags" of time is a delicious insult. Clocks and calendars, he says, are just tattered scraps, not the real thing.

The second stanza turns to boasting. He says he could easily blot out the sun's beams "with a wink," just by closing his eyes, but he does not want to lose sight of his beloved for even that long. Then he suggests the sun go and check whether the riches of the world, the spices of the East Indies and the gold mines of the West, are still where it left them, "Or lie here with me." The kings the sun saw yesterday, he adds, would all say that they lay in one bed. In other words, everything valuable in the world is here.

The third stanza pulls the claim together. "She's all states, and all princes I; / Nothing else is." She is every country, and he is every ruler. All other honor is "mimic," meaning imitation, and all wealth is "alchemy," meaning fake gold. Then comes the final twist. The speaker takes pity on the sun. It is old and tired, and its job is to warm the world, and now it can do that just by warming the two of them. "Shine here to us, and thou art everywhere; / This bed thy centre is, these walls thy sphere."

What a first-time reader may miss is how shrewd the argument is. He seems to be rejecting the sun, but he ends by giving it permission to stay, on his terms. The universe is invited to revolve around the bed.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Sun Rising is a tour de force of voice. The first thing a reader notices is the sound of a living person. The poem begins in mid-argument, with an insult, and the rhythms follow speech instead of a sing-song pattern. The lines vary in length, with short ones like "Why dost thou thus," breaking in as if the speaker were catching his breath or pausing for effect. That makes the poem feel spontaneous, though it is in fact intricately arranged. Each stanza has the same pattern, with lines of different lengths and a rhyming pair at the end, and Donne fills that pattern with arguments that sound improvised.

The humor is a major strength. Donne is willing to be rude to the sun, one of the oldest symbols of power in poetry, and doing so lets him be both playful and grand. The insults, "saucy pedantic wretch," are comic and affectionate. Yet the poem never stops being a love poem. The comedy is the means by which the love is made vivid.

The central conceit, which is the name for an extended, surprising comparison, is also impressive. Love is bigger than the sun because it contains the whole world. The logic is playful but surprisingly precise. By claiming that the room holds the Indies, kings, and wealth, Donne turns the usual hierarchy upside down. He shows how, to someone in love, a bed can seem more important than an empire.

There are real weaknesses as well. The poem is deeply self-absorbed. The speaker does most of the talking, and the woman does not get a word or even a description. She is "she," a prize in a boast. If you read the poem looking for a portrait of an actual person, you will not find one. The sun gets more personality than she does. That gives the poem a slightly lopsided feel, and some readers find the male speaker's swagger tiresome.

Another problem is that the argument is rather thin if you press on it. It is a pose, a series of clever overstatements, not a reasoned view. The claim that all honor is "mimic" and all wealth "alchemy" is gloriously extravagant, but it is not particularly true, and the poem does not seem to expect us to believe it literally. If you want emotional depth, you may find it all a bit glib.

The final stanza is the least tight. After the splendid brag, the sun's "age asks ease" feels like a gentle joke, but it drifts slightly from the main argument. Still, the closing image of the walls as the sun's sphere is so neat that it earns forgiveness. On balance, the poem is exuberant, shrewd and endlessly quotable, and its flaws are those of a young man's high spirits.

III. The Impact It Had

The Sun Rising did not change the world in a single moment, but it belongs to a body of work that did transform English poetry. It first appeared in print in 1633, two years after Donne died, in a volume called Poems, by J. D. Many of the love poems in that book, later grouped under the title Songs and Sonnets, circulated in handwritten copies among friends during his life. They were admired for their daring and sometimes criticized for their strangeness.

After Donne's death, his style shaped a generation of poets now called the metaphysical poets, including George Herbert, Andrew Marvell, Henry Vaughan and Richard Crashaw. They borrowed his habit of surprising comparisons and his conversational tone. Marvell's To His Coy Mistress, which talks of making the sun run and uses cosmic arguments for seduction, is clearly a relative of The Sun Rising.

Then fashion turned. In the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, critics preferred smoother, more polite verse. Samuel Johnson, writing in the 1700s, gave these poets the name metaphysical, and he did not mean it as praise. He thought their comparisons were forced and far-fetched. For a long time, Donne was considered a curiosity.

The great revival came in the early twentieth century. Herbert Grierson's anthology in 1921 helped bring the metaphysical poets back, and T. S. Eliot wrote an influential essay reviewing it. Eliot argued that these poets could feel a thought as immediately as the smell of a rose, and that later poetry had lost that union of thinking and feeling. Eliot's praise made Donne a hero to modernist poets, and poems like The Sun Rising became staples of university courses. Donne's wit and mixing of the intellectual and the physical were seen as a model for modern verse.

Today the poem is a standard in anthologies and classrooms, often used to teach the idea of the conceit and the tone of a speaking voice. It has been adapted, quoted and imitated in various forms, and its image of the bed as the center of the universe has been echoed by other writers. The phrase about the rags of time is often quoted in discussions of how love seems to stand outside the clock.

It has also influenced how readers think about the aubade, a poem about dawn and lovers parting. Donne's twist, in which the lover refuses to part and orders the sun to behave, proved a durable model. The poem remains one of the most vivid examples of how a poet can make argument feel like passion, and that influence runs through nearly every witty love poem since.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Sun Rising for its cheek. Few poems begin by calling the sun a busy old fool, and the sheer nerve of it is a pleasure. People often say that they smile at the first line and keep smiling as the poem continues, because it combines so much joy and so much mischief. It feels like being let in on a private joke.

They love its energy. The poem moves fast, tossing out schoolboys, apprentices, huntsmen, ants, kings and the Indies in a rush. There is a sense of someone thinking out loud, building a case and enjoying how good it sounds. Many readers say the poem makes them feel the thrill of falling in love, when the rest of the world seems small and slightly ridiculous.

The idea at its heart also resonates. Nearly everyone has had a moment, whether in love or deep in a happy hour with friends, when the clock stopped mattering. The poem catches that feeling perfectly. The phrase that hours, days and months are the "rags of time" is one that people remember because it names something they have felt but not said.

Readers who study poetry admire how modern the voice feels. Compared with many poems of the age, which are formal and distant, Donne's speaker sounds like someone you could meet. He is argumentative, bold and a little vain, and he is also tender in his way. The poem also contains real learning, with references to trade, politics, astronomy and alchemy, but it wears it lightly, which impresses readers who expect old poems to be stuffy.

Fellow writers have praised the cleverness of the close. The final lines, with the sun invited to treat the bed as its center, are admired as an elegant reversal. It shows how Donne can take the grandest thing available, a star, and fit it inside a room. Many poets have said that this ability to make the huge small and the small huge is exactly what they would like to learn from him.

Finally, there is the biographical charm. Knowing that Donne risked his job and freedom to marry for love gives the poem a personal weight. Whether or not the poem was written about Anne, readers like to imagine the young man who gave up a promising career for the woman he loved, and then wrote a poem declaring that nothing in the world could compare. That story is probably romanticized, yet it adds to the poem's appeal, and it keeps readers returning.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Sun Rising has drawn criticism, and some of it is as old as the poet's reputation.

The earliest and most famous complaint is aimed at the whole metaphysical style. In the later seventeenth century, John Dryden remarked that Donne affected the metaphysics and perplexed the minds of ladies with philosophy when he should have been courting them. A century later, Samuel Johnson argued that these poets yoked together very different ideas by force, producing comparisons that surprised but did not satisfy. By that view, a poem like The Sun Rising is more of an intellectual stunt than a heartfelt song. Johnson's charge, though sometimes unfair, captures a real feeling that some readers still have: that the cleverness gets in the way of the feeling.

A second line of criticism is about the poem's treatment of the woman. In recent decades, many readers have pointed out that the speaker uses the beloved as proof of his own importance. She is described as a kingdom, owned by a prince, and she never speaks. The poem imagines love as a private empire in which she is territory. Some critics see in this a typical male boast of the period. Others defend the poem as a playful performance, noting that the language of possession was a convention. Still, the unease persists, and it is the version of the poem's weakness that modern classrooms discuss most.

A third criticism is that the poem is less than fully serious. Because it is so witty, some readers feel it cannot be taken as an expression of genuine love, and that it is more about the speaker's cleverness than his feelings. Compare it with other poems by Donne, such as the quieter and more tender ones, and The Sun Rising can feel showy. The argument, that two lovers are worth more than the world, is delightful, but it is also a pose, and the poem knows it.

There is also the question of dating and biography. Many accounts link the poem to Donne's secret marriage and to the early reign of James I, but the evidence is thin. Scholars do not agree on when the poem was written, and the lines about the king hunting may be a general joke, not a record of an event. Readers should be cautious about tying the poem too closely to Donne's life.

Finally, some find the poem simply dated in its science. The picture of the sun moving around the earth was already under challenge from the new astronomy, and the poem's jokes depend on the old model. Yet most readers feel this does not hurt it, since the poem's idea does not depend on the astronomy being right.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Sun Rising belongs to the seventeenth-century movement called metaphysical poetry, a loose group of English poets who wrote with intellectual daring. The name is a bit misleading. These poets were not writing philosophy. They were writing love poems, religious poems and elegies that used the language of science, travel, law and trade to make surprising comparisons. Donne is the founder of the group, and The Sun Rising is a classic example of what the group did best.

To understand what it descends from, it helps to look at two traditions. The first is the dawn poem, the aubade, which goes back to the ancient Roman poet Ovid. In one of his love poems, Ovid scolds the dawn for arriving too soon, and Donne clearly knows that model. The second is the Petrarchan love lyric, with its sighing lovers and idealized ladies. Donne rejects the sighing and the idealizing. His speaker is confident, physical and witty, and he is already in the bed. The poem is in effect a reply to the tradition that came before.

It also reflects the intellectual shifts of its time. Around 1600 the old picture of the universe, with the earth at the center and the sun circling it, was being questioned by Copernicus, Galileo and others. The poem plays with the older view while hinting at the newer. When the speaker declares that the bed is the sun's center and the walls its sphere, he is borrowing the vocabulary of astronomy to make an emotional claim.

Its descendants are many. Marvell and the other metaphysical poets are the obvious heirs. Later on, the Romantic poets reacted against this witty style, preferring simple feeling, but the twentieth century brought it back. Poets like T. S. Eliot and many after him drew on Donne's mixture of passion and argument. Modern love poems that combine humor, intelligence and sensuality owe something to him.

It makes a good comparison with Shakespeare's sonnets. Where Shakespeare often moves slowly and sadly, Donne moves quickly and cheekily. Where Shakespeare's Sonnet 116 compares love to a star that guides sailors, Donne's speaker compares love to a world that swallows the sun. The two poets are almost opposites, and reading them together shows how many different voices the Renaissance could produce.

Why read it today? Because it is a reminder that poetry can be funny, argumentative and alive, and that love can be bold without being sentimental. It also demonstrates how an old poem can still feel fresh. If you have ever resented the alarm clock on a happy morning, you already understand the poem. Donne simply says it better, and with a grander cast of kings and Indies.
