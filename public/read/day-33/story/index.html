Beyond the Door

Philip K. Dick (1954) • 12 min read

“It’s made in Germany,” Larry said. After a moment he added, “Carl got it for me wholesale. He knows some guy in the clock business. Otherwise I wouldn’t have⁠—” He stopped.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Philip K. Dick is remembered for science fiction, but this story, published in the magazine Fantastic Universe in January 1954, has no spaceships and no aliens. It has a house, a marriage gone sour, and a cuckoo clock. Dick was in his mid-twenties, living in Berkeley, California, married for the first time, and writing at astonishing speed to pay the bills. The magazines of the time wanted a touch of the weird, and he often gave them the weird in the form of everyday objects behaving strangely. Here the object is a little carved bird that will come out for one person and not for another. Underneath, it is a story about a husband who treats his wife as property, and about what happens when affection, which he cannot buy or command, goes somewhere else. Listen for how the clock seems to take sides, and for how the last line leaves one question deliberately unanswered.

That night at the dinner table he brought it out and set it down beside her plate. Doris stared at it, her hand to her mouth. “My God, what is it?” She looked up at him, bright-eyed.

“Well, open it.”

Doris tore the ribbon and paper from the square package with her sharp nails, her bosom rising and falling. Larry stood watching her as she lifted the lid. He lit a cigarette and leaned against the wall.

“A cuckoo clock!” Doris cried. “A real old cuckoo clock like my mother had.” She turned the clock over and over. “Just like my mother had, when Pete was still alive.” Her eyes sparkled with tears.

“It’s made in Germany,” Larry said. After a moment he added, “Carl got it for me wholesale. He knows some guy in the clock business. Otherwise I wouldn’t have⁠—” He stopped.

Doris made a funny little sound.

“I mean, otherwise I wouldn’t have been able to afford it.” He scowled. “What’s the matter with you? You’ve got your clock, haven’t you? Isn’t that what you want?”

Doris sat holding onto the clock, her fingers pressed against the brown wood.

“Well,” Larry said, “what’s the matter?”

He watched in amazement as she leaped up and ran from the room, still clutching the clock. He shook his head. “Never satisfied. They’re all that way. Never get enough.”

He sat down at the table and finished his meal.

The cuckoo clock was not very large. It was handmade, however, and there were countless frets on it, little indentations and ornaments scored in the soft wood. Doris sat on the bed drying her eyes and winding the clock. She set the hands by her wristwatch. Presently she carefully moved the hands to two minutes of ten. She carried the clock over to the dresser and propped it up.

Then she sat waiting, her hands twisted together in her lap⁠—waiting for the cuckoo to come out, for the hour to strike.

As she sat she thought about Larry and what he had said. And what she had said, too, for that matter⁠—not that she could be blamed for any of it. After all, she couldn’t keep listening to him forever without defending herself; you had to blow your own trumpet in the world.

She touched her handkerchief to her eyes suddenly. Why did he have to say that, about getting it wholesale? Why did he have to spoil it all? If he felt that way he needn’t have got it in the first place. She clenched her fists. He was so mean, so damn mean.

But she was glad of the little clock sitting there ticking to itself, with its funny grilled edges and the door. Inside the door was the cuckoo, waiting to come out. Was he listening, his head cocked on one side, listening to hear the clock strike so that he would know to come out?

Did he sleep between hours? Well, she would soon see him: she could ask him. And she would show the clock to Bob. He would love it; Bob loved old things, even old stamps and buttons. He liked to go with her to the stores. Of course, it was a little awkward, but Larry had been staying at the office so much, and that helped. If only Larry didn’t call up sometimes to⁠—

There was a whirr. The clock shuddered and all at once the door opened. The cuckoo came out, sliding swiftly. He paused and looked around solemnly, scrutinizing her, the room, the furniture.

It was the first time he had seen her, she realized, smiling to herself in pleasure. She stood up, coming toward him shyly. “Go on,” she said. “I’m waiting.”

The cuckoo opened his bill. He whirred and chirped, quickly, rhythmically. Then, after a moment of contemplation, he retired. And the door snapped shut.

She was delighted. She clapped her hands and spun in a little circle. He was marvelous, perfect! And the way he had looked around, studying her, sizing her up. He liked her; she was certain of it. And she, of course, loved him at once, completely. He was just what she had hoped would come out of the little door.

Doris went to the clock. She bent over the little door, her lips close to the wood. “Do you hear me?” she whispered. “I think you’re the most wonderful cuckoo in the world.” She paused, embarrassed. “I hope you’ll like it here.”

Then she went downstairs again, slowly, her head high.

Larry and the cuckoo clock really never got along well from the start. Doris said it was because he didn’t wind it right, and it didn’t like being only half-wound all the time. Larry turned the job of winding over to her; the cuckoo came out every quarter hour and ran the spring down without remorse, and someone had to be ever after it, winding it up again.

Doris did her best, but she forgot a good deal of the time. Then Larry would throw his newspaper down with an elaborate weary motion and stand up. He would go into the dining-room where the clock was mounted on the wall over the fireplace. He would take the clock down and making sure that he had his thumb over the little door, he would wind it up.

“Why do you put your thumb over the door?” Doris asked once.

“You’re supposed to.”

She raised an eyebrow. “Are you sure? I wonder if it isn’t that you don’t want him to come out while you’re standing so close.”

“Why not?”

“Maybe you’re afraid of him.”

Larry laughed. He put the clock back on the wall and gingerly removed his thumb. When Doris wasn’t looking he examined his thumb.

There was still a trace of the nick cut out of the soft part of it. Who⁠—or what⁠—had pecked at him?

One Saturday morning, when Larry was down at the office working over some important special accounts, Bob Chambers came to the front porch and rang the bell.

Doris was taking a quick shower. She dried herself and slipped into her robe. When she opened the door Bob stepped inside, grinning.

“Hi,” he said, looking around.

“It’s all right. Larry’s at the office.”

“Fine.” Bob gazed at her slim legs below the hem of the robe. “How nice you look today.”

She laughed. “Be careful! Maybe I shouldn’t let you in after all.”

They looked at one another, half amused half frightened. Presently Bob said, “If you want, I’ll⁠—”

“No, for God’s sake.” She caught hold of his sleeve. “Just get out of the doorway so I can close it. Mrs. Peters across the street, you know.”

She closed the door. “And I want to show you something,” she said. “You haven’t seen it.”

He was interested. “An antique? Or what?”

She took his arm, leading him toward the dining-room. “You’ll love it, Bobby.” She stopped, wide-eyed. “I hope you will. You must; you must love it. It means so much to me⁠—he means so much.”

“He?” Bob frowned. “Who is he?”

Doris laughed. “You’re jealous! Come on.” A moment later they stood before the clock, looking up at it. “He’ll come out in a few minutes. Wait until you see him. I know you two will get along just fine.”

“What does Larry think of him?”

“They don’t like each other. Sometimes when Larry’s here he won’t come out. Larry gets mad if he doesn’t come out on time. He says⁠—”

“Says what?”

Doris looked down. “He always says he’s been robbed, even if he did get it wholesale.” She brightened. “But I know he won’t come out because he doesn’t like Larry. When I’m here alone he comes right out for me, every fifteen minutes, even though he really only has to come out on the hour.”

She gazed up at the clock. “He comes out for me because he wants to. We talk; I tell him things. Of course, I’d like to have him upstairs in my room, but it wouldn’t be right.”

There was the sound of footsteps on the front porch. They looked at each other, horrified.

Larry pushed the front door open, grunting. He set his briefcase down and took off his hat. Then he saw Bob for the first time.

“Chambers. I’ll be damned.” His eyes narrowed. “What are you doing here?” He came into the dining-room. Doris drew her robe about her helplessly, backing away.

“I⁠—” Bob began. “That is, we⁠—” He broke off, glancing at Doris. Suddenly the clock began to whirr. The cuckoo came rushing out, bursting into sound. Larry moved toward him.

“Shut that din off,” he said. He raised his fist toward the clock. The cuckoo snapped into silence and retreated. The door closed. “That’s better.” Larry studied Doris and Bob, standing mutely together.

“I came over to look at the clock,” Bob said. “Doris told me that it’s a rare antique and that⁠—”

“Nuts. I bought it myself.” Larry walked up to him. “Get out of here.” He turned to Doris. “You too. And take that damn clock with you.”

He paused, rubbing his chin. “No. Leave the clock here. It’s mine; I bought it and paid for it.”

In the weeks that followed after Doris left, Larry and the cuckoo clock got along even worse than before. For one thing, the cuckoo stayed inside most of the time, sometimes even at twelve o’clock when he should have been busiest. And if he did come out at all he usually spoke only once or twice, never the correct number of times. And there was a sullen, uncooperative note in his voice, a jarring sound that made Larry uneasy and a little angry.

But he kept the clock wound, because the house was very still and quiet and it got on his nerves not to hear someone running around, talking and dropping things. And even the whirring of a clock sounded good to him.

But he didn’t like the cuckoo at all. And sometimes he spoke to him.

“Listen,” he said late one night to the closed little door. “I know you can hear me. I ought to give you back to the Germans⁠—back to the Black Forest.” He paced back and forth. “I wonder what they’re doing now, the two of them. That young punk with his books and his antiques. A man shouldn’t be interested in antiques; that’s for women.”

He set his jaw. “Isn’t that right?”

The clock said nothing. Larry walked up in front of it. “Isn’t that right?” he demanded. “Don’t you have anything to say?”

He looked at the face of the clock. It was almost eleven, just a few seconds before the hour. “All right. I’ll wait until eleven. Then I want to hear what you have to say. You’ve been pretty quiet the last few weeks since she left.”

He grinned wryly. “Maybe you don’t like it here since she’s gone.” He scowled. “Well, I paid for you, and you’re coming out whether you like it or not. You hear me?”

Eleven o’clock came. Far off, at the end of town, the great tower clock boomed sleepily to itself. But the little door remained shut. Nothing moved. The minute hand passed on and the cuckoo did not stir. He was someplace inside the clock, beyond the door, silent and remote.

“All right, if that’s the way you feel,” Larry murmured, his lips twisting. “But it isn’t fair. It’s your job to come out. We all have to do things we don’t like.”

He went unhappily into the kitchen and opened the great gleaming refrigerator. As he poured himself a drink he thought about the clock.

There was no doubt about it⁠—the cuckoo should come out, Doris or no Doris. He had always liked her, from the very start. They had got along well, the two of them. Probably he liked Bob too⁠—probably he had seen enough of Bob to get to know him. They would be quite happy together, Bob and Doris and the cuckoo.

Larry finished his drink. He opened the drawer at the sink and took out the hammer. He carried it carefully into the dining-room. The clock was ticking gently to itself on the wall.

“Look,” he said, waving the hammer. “You know what I have here? You know what I’m going to do with it? I’m going to start on you⁠—first.” He smiled. “Birds of a feather, that’s what you are⁠—the three of you.”

The room was silent.

“Are you coming out? Or do I have to come in and get you?”

The clock whirred a little.

“I hear you in there. You’ve got a lot of talking to do, enough for the last three weeks. As I figure it, you owe me⁠—”

The door opened. The cuckoo came out fast, straight at him. Larry was looking down, his brow wrinkled in thought. He glanced up, and the cuckoo caught him squarely in the eye.

Down he went, hammer and chair and everything, hitting the floor with a tremendous crash. For a moment the cuckoo paused, its small body poised rigidly. Then it went back inside its house. The door snapped tight-shut after it.

The man lay on the floor, stretched out grotesquely, his head bent over to one side. Nothing moved or stirred. The room was completely silent, except, of course, for the ticking of the clock.

“I see,” Doris said, her face tight. Bob put his arm around her, steadying her.

“Doctor,” Bob said, “can I ask you something?”

“Of course,” the doctor said.

“Is it very easy to break your neck, falling from so low a chair? It wasn’t very far to fall. I wonder if it might not have been an accident. Is there any chance it might have been⁠—”

“Suicide?” the doctor rubbed his jaw. “I never heard of anyone committing suicide that way. It was an accident; I’m positive.”

“I don’t mean suicide,” Bob murmured under his breath, looking up at the clock on the wall. “I meant something else.”

But no one heard him.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, the story is a small domestic horror tale. A man named Larry Thomas gives his wife Doris a cuckoo clock, which she adores because it reminds her of one her mother had. But Larry cannot even give her a gift without spoiling it. He tells her he got it wholesale, through a friend named Carl, and that otherwise he could not have afforded it. That one careless remark hurts her badly, and she runs from the room still clutching the clock. "Never satisfied," he says to himself. "They're all that way. Never get enough."

From here the clock becomes a character. Doris talks to the cuckoo and treats him as a companion. She whispers that he is "the most wonderful cuckoo in the world." The bird comes out for her every fifteen minutes, though he only has to appear on the hour. But for Larry he sulks. He comes out at the wrong times, or not at all, and when Larry winds the clock he keeps his thumb over the little door, as though he is afraid. Doris says this teasingly: "Maybe you're afraid of him." Afterward Larry finds a nick on his thumb and wonders what pecked him.

Meanwhile, Doris has a lover, or at least an admirer, named Bob Chambers, a gentle young man who loves old things. Larry catches them together in the dining room. At that moment the cuckoo bursts out whirring, and Larry shouts at it to stop, and it does. Larry then throws them out, but keeps the clock, because "I bought it and paid for it." That sentence is the key to his character. He thinks of the world in terms of ownership.

In the last section, Larry is alone in the silent house. Doris is gone, and the clock is all he has left. But the cuckoo will not come out for him. He begins to talk to it, and his talk turns ugly. He decides the bird likes Doris and Bob, and that the three of them are "birds of a feather." He gets a hammer and threatens to destroy the clock. As he stands there looking down in thought, the door opens, and the cuckoo shoots out and strikes him "squarely in the eye." He falls from the chair he is standing on and breaks his neck.

The final scene is brief. A doctor says it was an accident. Bob asks whether it could have been an accident at all, and when the doctor suggests suicide, Bob murmurs, "I don't mean suicide. I meant something else." No one hears him. The story never tells us whether the clock really killed Larry. It might have been a coincidence, a fall from a chair that happened at the wrong moment. Or the bird might be, in some way, an extension of Doris's love, acting where she could not. The reader is invited to choose, and the best reading is probably to hold both at once: a man who cannot be loved destroys himself, and the world seems to agree.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

There is much to admire in how the story is built. Dick makes the clock work on three levels at once. It is a literal object, a symbol of Doris's longing for warmth and old-fashioned tenderness, and a possibly magical creature. He never lets one level swallow the others. The reader can always read it as an ordinary mechanical toy with a loose spring and a bad temper, which makes the supernatural reading all the more effective when it arrives.

The characterization of Larry is the story's strongest achievement. He is petty, self-pitying, and unaware of it, and Dick lets him condemn himself through his own words. "What's the matter with you? You've got your clock, haven't you?" he says to his weeping wife. He is the sort of person who sees every gift as a transaction and every complaint as ingratitude. Yet Dick allows a flicker of pathos too. When he is alone, he keeps winding the clock because the silence of the house gets on his nerves, and the sound of a clock is better than nothing. That detail makes him human, and it makes his end more disturbing.

The pacing is also good. The story moves quickly through its early scenes, and then slows for the last night, when Larry stalks around the house with his drink and his hammer, talking to a wall. That slow dread is well done. The business with the thumb, the nick, and the question of who, or what, had pecked at him plant the idea of the bird's menace long before the climax.

Now for the weaknesses. Doris and Bob are thin. Doris is sympathetic mainly because her husband is awful, and Bob is a mild shadow, a young man defined by his love of stamps and buttons. The story is not especially interested in whether their relationship is good, only in how it looks to Larry. That narrows the emotional range. There is also a faint hint that the story punishes Larry for his bad character in a way that is a little too neat, as if the universe were running a moral tidy-up. The climax, with the bird striking the eye, is slightly comic in its precision, and a reader may feel the story wants to be both a tragedy and a macabre joke without fully committing.

Some of the dialogue is also dated. The portrait of the stereotypical nagging wife and bullying husband draws on stock figures from magazine fiction, and a few lines of the narration about Doris, with her "bosom rising and falling," belong to a pulpier style than the best of the story. These are small blemishes in a piece that otherwise holds together very well.

III. The Impact It Had

Among Dick's early stories, this is a modest one, and it has not had the sort of afterlife that the famous ones have enjoyed. It was published in Fantastic Universe in January 1954 and later collected in volumes of his short fiction. There is no well-known film version, and it would be wrong to suggest one. Its influence is best understood as part of a pattern, not as a single event.

The pattern is this. In the early 1950s, Dick sold dozens of stories to the magazines. Many of them are about ordinary people in ordinary rooms, and the supernatural or science-fictional element is a small, domestic one. The cuckoo clock fits with several of his other stories in which household objects are quietly alive, and in which the cruelty of everyday relationships has consequences. This sort of story helped him find a voice that would later make his novels so distinctive: a voice that is interested in unhappy, anxious, and rather small people, and in how their private frustration leaks into the world around them.

The story also matters as evidence that Dick was not only a science fiction writer. Readers sometimes assume he was a pure genre author, but he wrote a good deal of realist fiction, including novels about marriage and ordinary life that were not published until after his death. A story like this one shows both strands working together. It is a realistic marriage drama with an uncanny turn, and it prepares the ground for later work in which domestic misery and the strange are thoroughly mixed.

Some readers connect the story's bitterness about marriage to Dick's own life, since he married several times and his marriages were often painful. It is dangerous to read a story as autobiography, and the story itself gives no sign that Larry is a self-portrait. But the interest in a couple in which one partner is controlling and the other seeks escape recurs in his fiction, and it is reasonable to say that the story reflects a concern that stayed with him.

In terms of broader influence, the tale belongs to the long line of short stories in which an object takes revenge, a form that later television anthologies and horror collections used heavily. The idea of an inanimate thing that responds to a person's character is an old one, and this is a neat, compact version. It is reprinted occasionally, discussed by Dick enthusiasts, and sometimes taught as an example of how supernatural fiction can work through suggestion. But it has remained a minor piece, appreciated more by people who already love the writer than by the general public.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who like this story usually begin with the cuckoo. There is something irresistibly charming about a little wooden bird that decides who it will favor. Dick gives the clock a personality without giving it a single line of dialogue. We learn that it sulks, that it shortens its performance, that it speaks "only once or twice, never the correct number of times," and that it has "a sullen, uncooperative note in his voice." These details are funny and sad at once, and they let the reader feel the bird's loyalty to Doris.

Many also praise the story's economy. It manages to sketch a whole marriage in a few scenes: the opening gift, the quarrel, the comedy with the thumb, the discovery, and the final night. There is hardly a wasted line. The reader understands everything about Larry from the first conversation, and yet the story keeps finding new ways to show him. His habit of treating every emotion as a bill that someone owes him is revealed again and again, until we are practically waiting for the clock to get him.

Another thing readers like is the way the story keeps a delicate balance between sympathy and satire. It would have been easy to make Larry a monster, or to make Doris a saint. Instead, there are small touches that complicate both. Doris, for all her tenderness, is a little self-excusing; she tells herself that "you had to blow your own trumpet in the world." Larry, for all his meanness, is lonely. This gives the ending a darker and more interesting flavor than a plain revenge story would have.

Readers also respond to the ambiguity of the final line. People who encounter the story for the first time often say they enjoy the last, quiet moment, when Bob mutters his suspicion and nobody hears. It is the sort of ending that stays with you precisely because it does not explain itself. You can argue about it with a friend, and that is a mark of a good ending.

Finally, fans of Dick's work enjoy the story because it shows a side of him that is easily overlooked: a gentle, observant craftsman of small human moments. There are no cosmic visions here. There is only a household, a clock, and a very particular kind of unhappiness, rendered with a light and sure hand.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because this is a minor and fairly obscure early story, there is little recorded in the way of famous criticism. What can be said is what later readers and critics tend to point out when they discuss Dick's early magazine work, and a few specific objections are fair.

The first is that the story is predictable. The reader sees from the beginning that Larry is a boor, that the clock will favor Doris, and that the bird will eventually do something to him. The pleasure is in the execution, not in surprise, and some readers find the outcome too tidy. A man who is mean to his wife and to a cuckoo clock is struck down by the cuckoo clock, and the moral arithmetic is a little too convenient.

The second concerns the female characters. Doris is presented almost entirely through her sorrow and her affection for the bird. She has little agency, and her affair with Bob is sketched without much interest in her inner life. The story is notably more interested in Larry's psychology than in hers. Modern readers sometimes find this a limitation, and also object to the stereotypes the story relies on, including the sulking husband who complains that women are "never satisfied." It is true that Dick is mocking Larry's view, not endorsing it, but the story still moves within a rather narrow frame of gender roles.

A third objection is about tone. The story is trying to be a poignant domestic tragedy, a ghost story, and a dark comedy at the same time, and it does not always blend them smoothly. The last moments, with Larry on a chair and the bird shooting out like a bullet, tip toward black farce. Some readers feel that this undercuts the real sadness of the marriage.

A fourth is about the suspicious final remark. Bob's whispered comment is a good ending, but a few readers feel it is a cheap twist, an attempt to add menace after the story has already finished its work. Others think the story would be stronger if it committed to a single explanation.

Finally, there is the common complaint made about much of Dick's early output: it was written quickly, for cash, and it shows. Some phrases are stock, some situations are familiar from other magazine stories of the period, and the piece does not reach the depth that Dick would later achieve. That criticism is fair, though it does not take away from what the story does well. It is a competent and affecting tale, not a masterpiece, and it should be read with that in mind.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place this story, it helps to see it as a descendant of the Gothic tradition, adapted to the American suburbs. The Gothic story, from Edgar Allan Poe onward, often takes place in a closed house where a guilty or unhappy person is haunted by something that may be real or may be in the mind. Poe's tales of murderers undone by an imagined heartbeat or a black cat are the great ancestors. Dick moves the house into the 1950s, gives it a refrigerator and a briefcase, and replaces the dungeon with a dining room. The haunting object is not a ghost or a cat but a small machine, which is a very modern twist.

There is also a long literary interest in mechanical creatures that seem alive. E. T. A. Hoffmann, the German Romantic writer, wrote stories in which dolls and automatons unsettle their owners, and the theme runs through the fiction of the nineteenth century. The cuckoo clock is a homely, comic cousin of those uncanny machines. Dick's later novels, with their androids, simulated beings, and living artifacts, would develop the idea on a larger scale. It is interesting to see the seed in a story about a clock.

The story also belongs to a type of mid-century short fiction in which unhappy marriages are examined with sour humor. Magazine writers and literary writers alike produced such stories, and many of them ended in violence or in a twist that punishes a cruel spouse. The tradition runs through weird-tale writers and into the later television anthologies that built entire episodes on a similar plot: an unpleasant person is undone by an object that responds to his or her character. Dick's version has the advantage of being short and graceful.

It can also be compared with other fantasy stories about love and ownership. The key idea, that a person who thinks he owns everything cannot control what matters most, is a durable moral. It echoes fairy tales in which a selfish figure is undone by a magical helper who favors someone gentler. In that sense the cuckoo is like a fairy-tale animal, loyal to the kind and fatal to the cruel.

Why read it today? Because it is short, odd, and quietly sharp about a type of person who is still very much with us: the one who treats generosity as a debt and affection as a possession. It also gives a useful glimpse of Dick before the famous novels, working in a realistic domestic world and finding the uncanny there. It will not change your life, but it is a good ten minutes, and it leaves you with a small, lingering chill.
