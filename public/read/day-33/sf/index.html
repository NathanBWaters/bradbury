The 1934 West Coast Waterfront General Strike and Anton Refregier's Rincon Annex Murals

San Francisco — How Harry Bridges and the longshoremen broke the 'shape-up' after 'Bloody Thursday' on Steuart Street—and how a Russian-born muralist painted their triumph in twenty-seven casein panels (Great Depression Labor Uprising & Postwar Muralism (1934–1948)) • 8 min read

“An injury to one is an injury to all.” — Watchword of the 1934 San Francisco General Strike & the International Longshore and Warehouse Union

Key Context & Why It Matters

Inside the streamlined 1940 Art Deco lobby of the historic Rincon Annex Post Office at 101 Spear Street, one block inland from where San Francisco police fired into striking dockworkers at Steuart and Mission streets on 'Bloody Thursday,' July 5, 1934, stretches Anton Refregier's monumental twenty-seven-panel casein tempera mural cycle, History of San Francisco (1941–1948). Culminating in Panel 25—which depicts the solemn 1934 funeral procession down Market Street for slain strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise—the murals and the eighty-three-day 1934 West Coast Waterfront Strike led by Australian-born longshoreman Harry Bridges abolished the corrupt dockside 'shape-up,' established the union-run hiring hall, and survived a 1953 Congressional effort to chisel the paintings off the walls.

Before May 1934, working as a longshoreman on the piers of the San Francisco Embarcadero meant enduring one of the most exploitative labor systems in industrial America: the 'shape-up' and the mandatory company-run 'Blue Book' union engineered by the Waterfront Employers Association. Every morning before dawn in the cold fog along the Embarcadero, thousands of dockworkers had to stand in a semicircle while walking bosses picked men for the day's cargo gangs—often demanding kickbacks of whiskey, cigars, or cash—and then forced those chosen into 'speed-up' shifts lasting twenty-four to thirty-six consecutive hours hoisting 200-pound coffee and sugar sacks until men collapsed beneath cargo slings.

In 1933, a thirty-two-year-old Australian-born former merchant seaman and winch driver named Alfred Renton 'Harry' Bridges began organizing rank-and-file San Francisco dockworkers around a radical democratic demand: abolish the shape-up entirely and replace it with a union-controlled rotary hiring hall that dispatched jobs fairly by seniority rather than bribery. When shipowners rejected a coastwide contract and control of the hiring halls, 12,000 longshoremen from San Diego to Seattle walked off the docks on May 9, 1934—quickly joined by maritime sailors, firemen, cooks, and stewards until more than 35,000 maritime workers had frozen commercial shipping along the entire Pacific Coast.

After nearly two months of deadlocked negotiations, the Industrial Association of San Francisco decided to break the strike by force on July 3, 1934, hiring armed strikebreakers and police escorts to drive non-union Belt Line trains and trucking convoys from Pier 38 through the picket lines into Rincon Hill warehouses. The confrontation exploded on Thursday, July 5, 1934—forever remembered in labor history as 'Bloody Thursday': all morning in the scorching summer heat, police hurled vomiting gas and tear-gas canisters at thousands of unarmed strikers along the Embarcadero and up the slopes of Rincon Hill. That afternoon, outside the International Longshoremen's Association strike kitchen at the corner of Steuart and Mission streets (one block from the Rincon Annex site), San Francisco police fired riot shotguns and revolvers directly into the crowd, killing longshoreman and World War I veteran Howard Sperry and Greek American Communist Party union cook Nick Bordoise, and wounding over a hundred others.

Stunned by the killings, strikers chalked on the bloody sidewalk at Steuart and Mission streets: '2 ILA Men Killed — Police Murder,' while California Governor Frank Merriam ordered 1,700 California National Guard troops armed with fixed bayonets, machine guns, and light tanks to occupy the Embarcadero behind barbed wire. Four days later, on Monday, July 9, 1934, the city witnessed one of the most extraordinary spectacles in American history: 40,000 longshoremen, sailors, and working families marched eight abreast in complete, self-policed silence up Market Street behind two flatbed trucks bearing the flower-draped coffins of Sperry and Bordoise while a symphony band played Beethoven's Funeral March and not a single policeman stood on the avenue.

Moved by the discipline of that silent funeral march, sixty-five San Francisco labor unions led by the Teamsters voted to launch the four-day San Francisco General Strike from July 16 to July 19, 1934—shutting down 130,000 workers across the city while authorizing union permits for milk deliveries, hospitals, and nineteen public eating houses. When federal arbitration concluded in October 1934, Harry Bridges and the West Coast longshoremen won a historic victory: coastwide recognition, a six-hour standard workday, and joint union-controlled rotary hiring halls—laying the foundation for the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU), which still honors 'Bloody Thursday' by shutting down every West Coast port every July 5.

Seven years later, in 1941, the federal Treasury Section of Fine Arts held a national competition featuring 82 artists to paint a $26,000 mural cycle across the 400-foot frieze of architect Gilbert Stanley Underwood's new Streamline Moderne Rincon Annex Post Office at 101 Spear Street—one block from where Sperry and Bordoise had fallen. The jury awarded the commission to Russian-born New York social-realist painter Anton Refregier, who—after a wartime hiatus—spent 1946 to 1948 atop wooden scaffolding in the Rincon Annex lobby painting twenty-seven monumental panels in luminous casein tempera on gessoed plaster chronicling the complete history of San Francisco from Ohlone basket-weavers to the 1945 founding of the United Nations.

Refregier refused to sanitize California's past: alongside panels celebrating Gold Rush forty-niners, Mount Tamalpais, and building the Golden Gate Bridge, he painted the 1877 anti-Chinese sandlot riots, the frame-up of labor organizer Tom Mooney, and—in Panel 25—the 1934 Waterfront Strike, depicting grieving workers with white-capped ILWU hats gathered beside the coffins of Sperry and Bordoise. During the height of the Red Scare in 1953, freshman California Congressman Hubert B. Scudder and the House Committee on Public Works introduced House Joint Resolution 211 to chisel down and destroy Refregier's Rincon Annex murals as 'subversive propaganda' derogatory to pioneers and shipowners.

An unprecedented defense coalition uniting the San Francisco Museum of Art, the San Francisco Chronicle, museum directors nationwide, and the ILWU defeated the congressional demolition resolution in 1953; placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, Refregier's twenty-seven panels still glow above the lobby of 101 Spear Street as an unyielding monument to working-class history.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From the Corrupt 'Shape-Up' to the Democratic Hiring Hall

Why was control of the hiring hall the non-negotiable core of the 1934 Waterfront Strike? Under the pre-1934 'shape-up,' shipowners kept a permanent surplus of desperate men competing on the pier apron every dawn, allowing bosses to blacklist union organizers and extract bribes. By replacing the shape-up with a union-dispatched rotary hiring hall ('low-man-out' based on hours worked), Harry Bridges and the ILWU transformed casual day laborers into a dignified, racially integrated industrial workforce.

2. Casein Tempera Technique and the 1953 Censorship Battle

Unlike the wet-plaster buon fresco technique used at Coit Tower in 1934, Anton Refregier painted the twenty-seven Rincon Annex panels between 1946 and 1948 using casein tempera (milk-protein binder mixed with dry earth and mineral pigments) applied to dry gessoed plaster walls. This medium gave the panels their distinctive matte, chalky brilliance and crisp graphic contours—and allowed conservators to stabilize and clean the murals when Rincon Annex was adapted into Rincon Center in the late 1980s.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three historical touchstones in mind: first, 'Bloody Thursday' (July 5, 1934), when police killed Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise at Steuart and Mission streets; second, the silent July 9, 1934 funeral march of 40,000 workers up Market Street that catalyzed the San Francisco General Strike; and third, Panel 25 of Anton Refregier's twenty-seven-panel mural cycle inside the Rincon Annex lobby at 101 Spear Street.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Start at the corner of Steuart and Mission streets one block west of the Embarcadero to read the sidewalk plaque marking the site where Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise fell on July 5, 1934. Then walk one block south to 101 Spear Street and step inside the public Art Deco lobby of Rincon Center to walk the full 400-foot perimeter of Anton Refregier's twenty-seven murals.
