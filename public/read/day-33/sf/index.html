The 1934 West Coast Waterfront General Strike and Anton Refregier's Rincon Annex Murals

San Francisco — How Harry Bridges and the longshoremen broke the 'shape-up' after 'Bloody Thursday' on Steuart Street—and how a Russian-born muralist painted their triumph in twenty-seven casein panels (Great Depression Labor Uprising & Postwar Muralism (1934–1948)) • 20 min read

“An injury to one is an injury to all.” — Watchword of the 1934 San Francisco General Strike & the International Longshore and Warehouse Union

Key Context & Why It Matters

Inside the streamlined 1940 Art Deco lobby of the historic Rincon Annex Post Office at 101 Spear Street (37.7918° N, 122.3924° W), one block inland from where San Francisco police fired into striking dockworkers at Steuart and Mission streets on 'Bloody Thursday,' July 5, 1934, stretches Anton Refregier's monumental twenty-seven-panel casein tempera mural cycle, *History of San Francisco* (1941–1948). Culminating in Panel 25—which depicts the solemn 1934 funeral procession down Market Street for slain strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise—the murals and the eighty-three-day 1934 West Coast Waterfront Strike led by Australian-born longshoreman Harry Bridges abolished the corrupt dockside 'shape-up,' established the union-run hiring hall, and survived a 1953 Congressional effort to chisel the paintings off the walls.

Standing today at 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets in the heart of the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero (37.7918° N, 122.3924° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Great Depression Labor Uprising & Postwar Muralism (1934–1948). Before May 1934, working as a longshoreman on the piers of the San Francisco Embarcadero meant enduring one of the most exploitative labor systems in industrial America: the 'shape-up' and the mandatory company-run 'Blue Book' union engineered by the Waterfront Employers Association. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how longshore union leader Harry Bridges (1901–1990), fallen strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise, and muralist Anton Refregier (1905–1979) and a generation of San Franciscans confronted abolishing the corrupt 'Blue Book' company union and dockside shape-up during the 1934 General Strike and defending the Rincon Annex murals against McCarthy-era censorship, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero. Every morning before dawn in the cold fog along the Embarcadero, thousands of dockworkers had to stand in a semicircle while walking bosses picked men for the day's cargo gangs—often demanding kickbacks of whiskey, cigars, or cash—and then forced those chosen into 'speed-up' shifts lasting twenty-four to thirty-six consecutive hours hoisting 200-pound coffee and sugar sacks until men collapsed beneath cargo slings. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during May 9 – July 31, 1934 (Waterfront Strike), July 5, 1934 ('Bloody Thursday'), and 1941–1948 (Refregier murals). In 1933, a thirty-two-year-old Australian-born former merchant seaman and winch driver named Alfred Renton 'Harry' Bridges began organizing rank-and-file San Francisco dockworkers around a radical democratic demand: abolish the shape-up entirely and replace it with a union-controlled rotary hiring hall that dispatched jobs fairly by seniority rather than bribery. Contemporary observers who walked past 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood longshore union leader Harry Bridges (1901–1990), fallen strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise, and muralist Anton Refregier (1905–1979), whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. When shipowners rejected a coastwide contract and control of the hiring halls, 12,000 longshoremen from San Diego to Seattle walked off the docks on May 9, 1934—quickly joined by maritime sailors, firemen, cooks, and stewards until more than 35,000 maritime workers had frozen commercial shipping along the entire Pacific Coast. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets. After nearly two months of deadlocked negotiations, the Industrial Association of San Francisco decided to break the strike by force on July 3, 1934, hiring armed strikebreakers and police escorts to drive non-union Belt Line trains and trucking convoys from Pier 38 through the picket lines into Rincon Hill warehouses. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through May 9 – July 31, 1934 (Waterfront Strike), July 5, 1934 ('Bloody Thursday'), and 1941–1948 (Refregier murals), the surrounding streets of the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. The confrontation exploded on Thursday, July 5, 1934—forever remembered in labor history as 'Bloody Thursday': all morning in the scorching summer heat, police hurled vomiting gas and tear-gas canisters at thousands of unarmed strikers along the Embarcadero and up the slopes of Rincon Hill. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. That afternoon, outside the International Longshoremen's Association strike kitchen at the corner of Steuart and Mission streets (one block from the Rincon Annex site), San Francisco police fired riot shotguns and revolvers directly into the crowd, killing longshoreman and World War I veteran Howard Sperry and Greek American Communist Party union cook Nick Bordoise, and wounding over a hundred others. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets toward private speculation—forcing longshore union leader Harry Bridges (1901–1990), fallen strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise, and muralist Anton Refregier (1905–1979) and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets. Stunned by the killings, strikers chalked on the bloody sidewalk at Steuart and Mission streets: '2 ILA Men Killed — Police Murder,' while California Governor Frank Merriam ordered 1,700 California National Guard troops armed with fixed bayonets, machine guns, and light tanks to occupy the Embarcadero behind barbed wire. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets, the day-to-day craft of union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero. Four days later, on Monday, July 9, 1934, the city witnessed one of the most extraordinary spectacles in American history: 40,000 longshoremen, sailors, and working families marched eight abreast in complete, self-policed silence up Market Street behind two flatbed trucks bearing the flower-draped coffins of Sperry and Bordoise while a symphony band played Beethoven's *Funeral March* and not a single policeman stood on the avenue. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets. Moved by the discipline of that silent funeral march, sixty-five San Francisco labor unions led by the Teamsters voted to launch the four-day San Francisco General Strike from July 16 to July 19, 1934—shutting down 130,000 workers across the city while authorizing union permits for milk deliveries, hospitals, and nineteen public eating houses. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in May 9 – July 31, 1934 (Waterfront Strike), July 5, 1934 ('Bloody Thursday'), and 1941–1948 (Refregier murals), the entire city turned its eyes toward the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets in the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero. When federal arbitration concluded in October 1934, Harry Bridges and the West Coast longshoremen won a historic victory: coastwide recognition, a six-hour standard workday, and joint union-controlled rotary hiring halls—laying the foundation for the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU), which still honors 'Bloody Thursday' by shutting down every West Coast port every July 5. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed May 9 – July 31, 1934 (Waterfront Strike), July 5, 1934 ('Bloody Thursday'), and 1941–1948 (Refregier murals), shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets. Seven years later, in 1941, the federal Treasury Section of Fine Arts held a national competition featuring 82 artists to paint a $26,000 mural cycle across the 400-foot frieze of architect Gilbert Stanley Underwood's new Streamline Moderne Rincon Annex Post Office at 101 Spear Street—one block from where Sperry and Bordoise had fallen. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity. The jury awarded the commission to Russian-born New York social-realist painter Anton Refregier, who—after a wartime hiatus—spent 1946 to 1948 atop wooden scaffolding in the Rincon Annex lobby painting twenty-seven monumental panels in luminous casein tempera on gessoed plaster chronicling the complete history of San Francisco from Ohlone basket-weavers to the 1945 founding of the United Nations. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of longshore union leader Harry Bridges (1901–1990), fallen strikers Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise, and muralist Anton Refregier (1905–1979), discovering anew how deeply its design was tuned to the unique rhythms of the San Francisco peninsula.

By the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, a new generation of preservationists, scientists, and community organizers recognized that the Rincon Annex Post Office Lobby (101 Spear Street) and the Bloody Thursday plaque at Steuart and Mission streets was more than a relic of Great Depression Labor Uprising & Postwar Muralism (1934–1948); it was a living ecological and cultural keystone. Refregier refused to sanitize California's past: alongside panels celebrating Gold Rush forty-niners, Mount Tamalpais, and building the Golden Gate Bridge, he painted the 1877 anti-Chinese sandlot riots, the frame-up of labor organizer Tom Mooney, and—in Panel 25—the 1934 Waterfront Strike, depicting grieving workers with white-capped ILWU hats gathered beside the coffins of Sperry and Bordoise. Through painstaking archival recovery, structural stabilization, and public stewardship, the site at 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets was safeguarded so that future generations could experience its lessons firsthand.

Ultimately, the story of The 1934 West Coast Waterfront General Strike and Anton Refregier's Rincon Annex Murals endures because it captures the quintessential San Francisco paradox: a city perpetually perched on the edge of natural and social upheaval that responds by building works of extraordinary beauty, audacity, and solidarity. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From the Corrupt 'Shape-Up' to the Democratic Hiring Hall

Why was control of the hiring hall the non-negotiable core of the 1934 Waterfront Strike? Under the pre-1934 'shape-up,' shipowners kept a permanent surplus of desperate men competing on the pier apron every dawn, allowing bosses to blacklist union organizers and extract bribes. By replacing the shape-up with a union-dispatched rotary hiring hall ('low-man-out' based on hours worked), Harry Bridges and the ILWU transformed casual day laborers into a dignified, racially integrated industrial workforce. At 101 Spear Street (Rincon Annex) and the intersection of Steuart and Mission streets in the Rincon Hill Waterfront and South Embarcadero, this synthesis of union-dispatched rotary hiring hall logistics, casein-tempera mural painting on gessoed plaster, and general-strike labor solidarity and civic memory remains visible at every turn. Close examination of The 1934 West Coast Waterfront General Strike and Anton Refregier's Rincon Annex Murals in Rincon Center (Historic Rincon Annex Post Office), 101 Spear Street & Steuart/Mission Streets, San Francisco (37.7918° N, 122.3924° W) highlights how primary historical records, physical craftsmanship, and enduring local geography reinforce every detail of this Great Depression Labor Uprising & Postwar Muralism (1934–1948) account.

2. Casein Tempera Technique and the 1953 Censorship Battle

Unlike the wet-plaster *buon fresco* technique used at Coit Tower in 1934, Anton Refregier painted the twenty-seven Rincon Annex panels between 1946 and 1948 using casein tempera (milk-protein binder mixed with dry earth and mineral pigments) applied to dry gessoed plaster walls. This medium gave the panels their distinctive matte, chalky brilliance and crisp graphic contours—and allowed conservators to stabilize and clean the murals when Rincon Annex was adapted into Rincon Center in the late 1980s.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three historical touchstones in mind: first, 'Bloody Thursday' (July 5, 1934), when police killed Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise at Steuart and Mission streets; second, the silent July 9, 1934 funeral march of 40,000 workers up Market Street that catalyzed the San Francisco General Strike; and third, Panel 25 of Anton Refregier's twenty-seven-panel mural cycle inside the Rincon Annex lobby at 101 Spear Street.
