Leda and the Swan

W. B. Yeats (1924) • 3 min read

With a sudden blow of beating wings, Zeus’s assault upon mortal Leda engenders Helen of Troy, the burning roof and tower, and an entire cycle of classical tragedy.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in September 1923 after George Russell (AE) invited Yeats to contribute a political poem to The Irish Statesman, Yeats instead meditated on how catastrophic historical epochs are inaugurated by violent mythic annunciations, publishing the sonnet in his radical Dublin journal To-Morrow in August 1924 and in The Tower (1928). Belonging to the High Modernism tradition and categorized under Modernist & Imagist, 'Leda and the Swan' (1924) unfolds across 15 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. Hailed by Camille Paglia, Helen Vendler, and Harold Bloom as one of the most compressed and formally daring sonnets ever written, 'Leda and the Swan' pairs with 'The Second Coming' to frame Yeats’s vision of how two-thousand-year historical cycles begin.

A sudden blow: the great wings beating still

Above the staggering girl, her thighs caressed

By the dark webs, her nape caught in his bill,

He holds her helpless breast upon his breast.

How can those terrified vague fingers push

The feathered glory from her loosening thighs?

And how can body, laid in that white rush

But feel the strange heart beating where it lies?

A shudder in the loins engenders there

The broken wall, the burning roof and tower

And Agamemnon dead.

Being so caught up,

So mastered by the brute blood of the air,

Did she put on his knowledge with his power

Before the indifferent beak could let her drop?

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in September 1923 after George Russell (AE) invited Yeats to contribute a political poem to The Irish Statesman, Yeats instead meditated on how catastrophic historical epochs are inaugurated by violent mythic annunciations, publishing the sonnet in his radical Dublin journal To-Morrow in August 1924 and in The Tower (1928). Within the broader trajectory of High Modernism, W. B. Yeats composed 'Leda and the Swan' (1924) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"A sudden blow: the great wings beating still"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 15 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Leda and the Swan' lies the terrifying intersection of divine power, mortal vulnerability, and historical determinism: Zeus’s assault on Leda in the form of a swan conceives Helen and Clytemnestra, directly precipitating the Trojan War ('The broken wall, the burning roof and tower / And Agamemnon dead') and raising the final unanswered question of whether mortal flesh also absorbed divine knowledge. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"But feel the strange heart beating where it lies?"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"Before the indifferent beak could let her drop?"—the central metaphor has.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'Leda and the Swan' using a fractured hybrid sonnet (ABAB CDCD EFGEFG) that opens in medias res with a percussive, verbless spondaic shock ('A sudden blow: the great wings beating still'), physically splitting line eleven across a typographic staircase after 'And Agamemnon dead' to mark the rupture between mythic violence and philosophical inquiry. Spanning 15 lines across 3 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "A sudden blow: the great wings beating still" and "Before the indifferent beak could let her drop?" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Hailed by Camille Paglia, Helen Vendler, and Harold Bloom as one of the most compressed and formally daring sonnets ever written, 'Leda and the Swan' pairs with 'The Second Coming' to frame Yeats’s vision of how two-thousand-year historical cycles begin. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Leda and the Swan' has remained a touchstone of the Modernist & Imagist canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
