Sixteen-Year-Old Maya Angelou as San Francisco's First Black Streetcar Conductor (1944)

San Francisco — How teenage Marguerite Johnson refused to take 'no' for an answer at the Market Street Railway offices on Duboce Avenue and broke the wartime color barrier on the 5-McAllister and 7-Haight lines (World War II Home Front & Great Migration (1942–1945)) • 8 min read

“To her, if I wanted to be the conductor on the streetcar, then by all that was right and holy, I would be the conductor on the streetcar. ‘They don’t accept colored people on the streetcars.’ ‘She’ll be the first.’” — Maya Angelou, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings (1969)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In early 1944, during the height of World War II when thousands of African American shipyard workers had migrated to San Francisco's Fillmore District, fifteen-year-old Marguerite Annie Johnson—whom the world would later revere as poet and memoirist Dr. Maya Angelou (1928–2014)—watched the blue-uniformed female conductors ringing the brass bells and punching transfers on the electric streetcars of the Market Street Railway Company and decided she wanted the job. When the dispatcher at the Duboce Avenue carbarn office refused even to hand her an application form because the company did not hire Black conductors, young Marguerite sat inside his waiting room every single day for three weeks until she broke the color line at age sixteen.

Between 1941 and 1945, the wartime shipbuilding boom at Hunters Point Naval Shipyard, Marinship, and the Kaiser yards in Richmond triggered a historic demographic transformation in San Francisco: the city's African American population grew nearly tenfold from roughly 4,800 residents in 1940 to over 43,000 by 1945, centered primarily in the Victorian houses of the Western Addition and Fillmore District. Among the young newcomers living at 1703 Post Street and attending George Washington High School above the Richmond District—while studying dance and drama on scholarship at the leftist California Labor School on Haight Street—was fifteen-year-old Marguerite Annie Johnson, who had moved from Stamps, Arkansas, to live with her fiercely independent, dazzlingly self-assured mother, Vivian 'Lady' Baxter.

Every morning as Marguerite rode the clanging electric streetcars from the Fillmore across town, she watched the wartime women 'conductorettes' who had recently replaced drafted male conductors on the open-platform cars: wearing trim navy-blue wool jackets, billed caps, and gleaming chrome coin changers clipped to their waists, they slid between the seats punching transfers and pulling the leather bell cord. Back in 1944, Market Street still ran four parallel sets of streetcar tracks ('The Roar of the Four'): two inner tracks operated by the city-owned Municipal Railway (Muni) and two outer tracks operated by the private Market Street Railway Company, which maintained its personnel offices near the carbarn at Market and Duboce avenues.

When fifteen-year-old Marguerite announced over breakfast that she was going to drop out of school for a semester and apply to become a streetcar conductor, her mother Vivian Baxter warned her gently of the institutional reality: although wartime labor shortages had forced the private Market Street Railway to hire white women, the company and its union had stubbornly refused to hire Black motormen or conductors. Walking into the streetcar company's wood-paneled personnel office near Duboce Avenue the next morning, Marguerite asked the white receptionist for a job application; the woman glanced up, claimed they were only hiring applicants sent directly from a specific state employment agency, and turned her away without handing over a single sheet of paper.

When Marguerite realized the receptionist had lied—because no such agency referral rule existed—and reported the slight to Vivian Baxter, her mother gave her the moral advice that anchored the rest of Maya Angelou's life: 'Do you want the job? Then go back down there and sit in that office until they give you an application, and here is five dollars for your lunches.' Inside the glass-partitioned office, dispatchers and clerks played a humiliating game of psychological warfare: they walked past her without looking, slammed file cabinets, whispered jokes, and had the receptionist call out to empty air that the personnel manager was 'out of town indefinitely' while Marguerite watched the manager's shadow moving behind the frosted glass door.

Instead of crying or exploding in anger, the teenager practiced what her mother called 'smiling at the absurd': reinforced by evening visits from leaders of the San Francisco Council for Civic Unity and the NAACP who encouraged her stand, she simply turned another page of Dostoevsky, ate her lunch at a nearby automat, and returned to the exact same wooden chair at 1:00 p.m. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

Finally, on a Friday afternoon after nearly three weeks of her silent one-woman sit-in, the frosted glass door swung open and the personnel manager beckoned her inside; exasperated, he asked why she wanted so badly to work on the streetcars, and when she answered honestly, 'I like the uniforms, and I like people,' he handed her the application form—where she wrote that she was nineteen years old. At 4:30 a.m. on her first morning of work as San Francisco's first African American female streetcar conductor—her mother Vivian Baxter driving alongside the electric streetcar down the dark, foggy hills in her Hudson sedan with a pistol in the glove compartment just in case anyone harassed her sixteen-year-old daughter—Marguerite strapped on her heavy nickel-plated four-barrel coin changer and stepped onto the rear platform.

Because senior white motormen initially resented working with a Black teenager, they deliberately assigned her the exhausting split-shift schedules running between 4:30 a.m. and midnight on the hilly 5-McAllister and 7-Haight lines; yet Marguerite stood firm on the swaying wooden platform, calling out every transfer intersection from the Ferry Building to Golden Gate Park, making lightning-fast change for nickels and dimes, and pulling the two-bell 'Go Ahead' cord. Twenty-five years later, when Random House published her landmark 1969 autobiography I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings—written at the urging of her friend James Baldwin and editor Robert Loomis—readers around the world cheered that triumphant chapter in 1944 San Francisco where a teenage girl armed with a library book stared down Jim Crow bureaucracy.

Sixty-four years after she rang the bell on the Market Street Railway, Dr. Maya Angelou was officially honored in San Francisco by the Municipal Transportation Agency (SFMTA) and the Friends of Market Street Railway for breaking the transit color line just months before Muni absorbed the private company on September 29, 1944. Today, outside the San Francisco Main Library at Civic Center Plaza stands Lava Thomas's nine-foot bronze monument Portrait of a Phenomenal Woman (dedicated September 2024), while just fifty yards away down Market Street, vintage 1920s and 1940s electric streetcars still roll past Duboce Avenue—every ding of their brass bells echoing a sixteen-year-old girl's unbreakable resolve.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Wartime Labor Shortages and 'The Roar of the Four' on Market Street

Why did Market Street have four parallel streetcar tracks in 1944, and why was transit integration such a flashpoint? Until the consolidation of September 29, 1944, city-owned Muni cars ran on the two inner tracks while the private Market Street Railway Company ran on the two outer tracks. Even as World War II conscription drained male operators and forced both systems to hire women ('conductorettes'), entrenched racial barriers persisted—until Audley Cole at Muni (1941) and sixteen-year-old Marguerite Johnson (Maya Angelou) at the Market Street Railway (1944) broke the color line.

2. The Mechanics of the Two-Person Open-Platform Streetcar

Operating an early-twentieth-century San Francisco electric streetcar required two distinct crew members: the motorman up front, who controlled the brass K-controller handle and air brake valve, and the conductor on the rear platform. The conductor collected seven-cent fares into a mechanical registering farebox, dispensed coins from a four-barrel belt changer, punched paper time-and-route transfers, and communicated with the motorman via overhead leather bell-cord signals (one bell to stop at the next corner, two bells to proceed).

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three San Francisco touchstones from Maya Angelou's youth: first, the Duboce & Market carbarn personnel office where she staged her three-week sit-in with a Russian novel in 1944; second, George Washington High School and the California Labor School, where she studied drama and dance while working split shifts on the 5-McAllister and 7-Haight lines; and third, Lava Thomas's 2024 bronze monument Portrait of a Phenomenal Woman at the San Francisco Main Library.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Begin at Civic Center Plaza outside the Larkin Street entrance of the San Francisco Main Library to stand before Lava Thomas's bronze monument to Dr. Maya Angelou. Then board a vintage electric streetcar on the F-Market & Wharves line along Market Street and ride west to Market and Duboce avenues, where the historic streetcar yard and Muni Metro portal still anchor the intersection.
