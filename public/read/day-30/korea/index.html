Gwangjang Market (1905): From Anti-Colonial Silk Stock Company to Mung-Bean Bindaetteok Alleys

Korea — How Korean merchants pooled 100,000 Hwan to defend domestic commerce in July 1905, and how stone-ground mung beans and vintage silk shaped a century of Seoul (Korean Empire (July 5, 1905) to Present) • 9 min read

“If we cede our marketplaces and currency to foreign monopoly, our people will become guests in their own capital.” — Founding Prospectus of Gwangjang Corporation (July 5, 1905)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Step beneath the vaulted skylights of Gwangjang Market at Jongno 4-ga in central Seoul, where the sizzle of golden mung-bean pancakes (nokdu bindaetteok) fried on cast-iron griddles rises below a second-floor labyrinth of 1,500 silk, satin, and vintage clothing stalls. While visitors today know Gwangjang as Korea's most famous street-food mecca, few realize it was founded on July 5, 1905, as an act of patriotic economic resistance. As Imperial Japan seized control of Korean monetary policy and opened the Namdaemun market under Japanese management, Korean merchants and officials led by Park Seung-jik and Kim Jong-han pooled 100,000 Hwan in domestic capital to incorporate the Gwangjang Stock Company—creating Korea's first permanent modern daily market between two bridges.

Follow your ears and nose through the West Gate or North Gate 2 of Gwangjang Market at Jongno 4-ga in central Seoul just after sunset. Ahead of you stretches a soaring, cross-shaped arcade echoing with the clink of green makgeolli rice-wine bowls, the heavy granite rumble of motorized millstones (maetdol) grinding soaked yellow mung beans into creamy batter, and the rhythmic chiiik of eighty cast-iron griddles frying golden-brown nokdu bindaetteok in pork fat and vegetable oil. Above the steam of the food stalls, steep stairways climb into two upper stories packed with 5,000 bolts of shimmering Korean silk (bidan), ramie (mosi), wedding hanbok, and vintage tailored coats.

Today, millions of travelers and Seoulites flock to Gwangjang Market for its legendary street food, yet almost nobody pausing over a plate of mayak-gimbap looks up at the market’s name to ask what Gwangjang actually means—or why this market was born in July 1905 as a battleground for Korean national survival. During the Joseon Dynasty, commerce inside the walled capital of Hanyang was strictly regulated around the licensed Sijeon shops on Jongno and three sprawling open-air markets: the I-hyeon Market (Pear Hill Market) near Dongdaemun, the Chilpae Market outside Namdaemun, and the Jongnu Market at Bosingak. In the late nineteenth century, I-hyeon Market was the beating agricultural and textile heart of eastern Seoul, but it remained an unpaved, seasonal five-day market where merchants set up temporary straw mats in the mud.

Then came the existential crisis of 1904–1905. During the Russo-Japanese War, Japanese financial adviser Megata Tanetaro forced the Korean Empire (Daehan Jeguk) to implement the Currency Reform of June 1905 (Hwapyeseongni), invalidating traditional Korean nickel and copper coins overnight and driving Korean merchants into bankruptcy for lack of Japanese Dai-Ichi Bank notes. Simultaneously, Japanese colonial speculators acquired control of Namdaemun Market, threatening to monopolize all wholesale distribution in the capital. Refusing to surrender Seoul’s eastern commercial artery without a fight, a coalition of patriotic Korean merchants and reformist court officials—led by Kim Jong-han, Park Seung-jik (founder of Korea’s oldest surviving corporation, Doosan, who opened Park Seung-jik Store right here in August 1905 selling cotton cloth and cosmetics!), and Jang Du-hyeon—pooled 100,000 Hwan of purely Korean domestic capital (minjok jabon).

On July 5, 1905—just four months before the Eulsa Treaty stripped the Korean Empire of its diplomatic sovereignty—they officially registered the Gwangjang Stock Company (Gwangjang Jusikhoesa), the first privately capitalized Korean market corporation in history, obtaining royal permission to transform the muddy I-hyeon Market into Korea’s first permanent, roofed, seven-day-a-week modern market! Why did the founders name it Gwangjang ? Originally, the market was planned on the reclaimed streambed between two historic stone bridges spanning Cheonggyecheon: Gwanggyo (Broad Bridge) to the west and Jangtonggyo (Long Bridge) to the east—taking the first syllable of Gwang and Jang! When floods proved that narrow site unsuitable and the market was built slightly east on the old military garrison grounds of Hullyeonwon in Yeji-dong, the founders kept the name Gwangjang, writing it with the characters meaning 'To Gather From Afar and Store in Abundance.' Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Gwangjang Sijang (Korea's first permanent covered market, 1905) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment.

Throughout the thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule (1910–1945), while Japanese department stores such as Mitsukoshi, Hwasin, and Minakai dominated Chungmuro and Jongno 2-ga, Gwangjang Market remained the stubborn citadel of Korean domestic commerce, textile distribution, and nationalist solidarity. After the Korean War (1950–1953) reduced the wooden market sheds to ashes, Gwangjang rose again between 1954 and 1959 as a multi-story reinforced-concrete mega-arcade covering 42,000 square meters and housing more than 5,000 individual shops. For the next four decades, a wedding anywhere in South Korea was literally incomplete without a pilgrimage to Gwangjang Market. Brides and mothers traveled by train from every province to the second-floor Ju-dan (Silk and Satin Alley) and Chimgu (Bedding) halls of Gwangjang to commission custom silk Hanbok, embroidered marriage quilts (yedan ibul), mother-of-pearl lacquer boxes (ham), and ritual Pyebaek wedding chests filled with chestnuts, jujubes, and spiced beef jerky.

To feed those thousands of wholesale silk buyers, seamstresses, and porters who worked from four in the morning until late at night inside the unheated market corridors, women set up small charcoal-briquette stoves along the ground-floor intersection—giving birth to Gwangjang’s world-famous culinary alley (Meokja Golmok). Why did Nokdu Bindaetteok (crispy stone-ground mung-bean pancakes) become the signature dish of a silk market? Unlike wheat-flour pancakes (pajeon), which turn soggy and rubbery within twenty minutes of cooling, pancakes made from 100 percent stone-ground hulled yellow mung beans (nokdu), mixed with crunchy mung-bean sprouts (sukju), bracken fern (gosari), kimchi, and pork, fry up with a lacy, aerated crust that stays hot and satisfying for hours—and because mung beans naturally detoxify and cool the body according to Dongui Bogam medicine, they fueled market workers without weighing them down.

Beside the bindaetteok griddles emerged three other Gwangjang icons: first, Mayak Gimbap (Narcotic/Addictive Mini Seaweed Rice Rolls), invented in the market alleys as bite-sized rolls stuffed simply with pickled yellow radish (danmuji), julienned carrots, and spinach brushed with toasted sesame oil so busy textile porters could eat them standing up with a dip of sweet-hot mustard soy sauce (gyeoja-jang) without needing a knife to slice them! Second, in the narrow side lane known as Yukhoe Golmok (Seasoned Raw Beef Alley)—pioneered in the 1970s by stalls such as Changsin Yukhoe and Jamaejip using fresh daily cuts delivered straight from the Majang-dong meat market—plates of ruby-red Korean hanwoo beef julienned with crisp white Korean pear sticks (bae), garlic, sesame oil, and crowned with a golden raw egg yolk are paired with steaming bowls of spicy beef-radish soup.

Third, hidden up a narrow concrete stairwell on the third floor above the traditional hanbok shops sits the Guje Sijang (Vintage Clothing Labyrinth)—originally born after the Korean War when merchants repaired and tailored secondhand US military overcoats and wool suits, and now packed with hundreds of tiny boutiques where young Seoul stylists, musicians, and thrift hunters dig through racks of Burberry trenches, Japanese denim, and 1980s leather jackets. Stand at the central crossroads of Gwangjang Market at eight o’clock on a rainy evening when the skylights drum with rain and a seventy-year-old vendor at Soonhee’s Bindaetteok flips a sizzling golden mung-bean pancake with two iron spatulas. One hundred and twenty years after Park Seung-jik and his fellow merchants pooled 100,000 Hwan to keep Seoul’s marketplace in Korean hands, Gwangjang is still gathering from afar and storing in abundance.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Economic History: How the 1905 Gwangjang Stock Company Resisted Colonial Monopoly

Economic historians view the founding of the Gwangjang Stock Company on July 5, 1905, as a watershed moment in modern Korean capitalism. Confronted with Megata Tanetaro's June 1905 Currency Reform—which starved Korean merchants of liquidity—and Japanese encroachment on Namdaemun Market, Korean entrepreneurs led by Kim Jong-han and Park Seung-jik used the modern Western joint-stock corporation structure to pool 100,000 Hwan, buying land and building Korea's first permanent daily market governed by Korean directors.

2. Food Science: Why Stone-Milled Mung Beans ('Nokdu') Create the Ultimate Crisp Crust

Why do Gwangjang Market vendors insist on grinding soaked hulled mung beans between wet granite millstones (maetdol) on-site rather than using dry commercial mung-bean flour? Wet stone-milling shears the mung-bean cotyledon cells gently at low temperature without denaturing the native globulins or gelatinizing the high-amylose mung-bean starch. When ladled onto a 190°C greased cast-iron griddle, water trapped inside the coarse bean grits flashes into steam micro-pockets while mung-bean proteins crisp into a shatteringly light, golden lattice.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three Gwangjang terms in mind: first, Gwangjang, originating from the Gwanggyo and Jangtonggyo bridges and meaning 'to gather widely and store abundantly'; second, Nokdu Bindaetteok, the stone-ground mung-bean pancake fried with pork and kimchi; and third, Mayak Gimbap, the bite-sized finger-thick seaweed rice rolls dipped in pungent mustard-soy sauce.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Take Seoul Subway Line 1 to Jongno 5-ga Station (Exit 8) and enter Gwangjang Market. Watch the stone millstones turning at the central bindaetteok roundabout, step into Yukhoe Alley for raw beef with Korean pear, climb to the 2nd-floor Silk and Hanbok Arcade to see thousands of bolts of traditional woven bidan, and explore the 3rd-floor Guje vintage clothing maze.
