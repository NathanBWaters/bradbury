Meditations, Book II: Written Among the Quadi on the Granua

Marcus Aurelius (c. 172 AD) • 13 min read

Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Marcus Aurelius was emperor of Rome from 161 to 180, and he wrote this book for an audience of one: himself. Nobody was meant to read it. These private notes, written in Greek and later called the Meditations, are among the most loved books of ancient philosophy, because they show a man with almost unlimited power trying, every day, to be decent. Book Two is traditionally marked as written among the Quadi, a Germanic people living north of the Danube, near the River Hron in what is now Slovakia. Rome was fighting long, grinding wars on that frontier, and a deadly plague was moving through the empire. Marcus was in his fifties, often ill, and far from home. He writes in the cold, short bursts of the Stoics, a school that taught that we cannot control events, only our judgments about them. As you listen, notice how often he scolds himself, and how kind he is to everyone else. His meaning is simple: life is short, so be good now.

Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil. But I who have seen the nature of the good that it is beautiful, and of the bad that it is ugly, and the nature of him who does wrong, that it is akin to me, not only of the same blood or seed, but that it participates in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divinity, I can neither be injured by any of them, for no one can fix on me what is ugly, nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him. For we are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. To act against one another then is contrary to nature; and it is acting against one another to be vexed and to turn away.

Whatever this is that I am, it is a little flesh and breath, and the ruling part. Throw away thy books; no longer distract thyself: it is not allowed; but as if thou wast now dying, despise the flesh; it is blood and bones and a network, a contexture of nerves, veins, and arteries. See the breath also, what kind of a thing it is, air, and not always the same, but every moment sent out and again sucked in. The third then is the ruling part: consider thus: Thou art an old man; no longer let this be a slave, no longer be pulled by the strings like a puppet to unsocial movements, no longer either be dissatisfied with thy present lot, or shrink from the future.

All that is from the gods is full of Providence. That which is from fortune is not separated from nature or without an interweaving and involution with the things which are ordered by Providence. From thence all things flow; and there is besides necessity, and that which is for the advantage of the whole universe, of which thou art a part. But that is good for every part of nature which the nature of the whole brings, and what serves to maintain this nature. Now the universe is preserved, as by the changes of the elements so by the changes of things compounded of the elements. Let these principles be enough for thee, let them always be fixed opinions. But cast away the thirst after books, that thou mayest not die murmuring, but cheerfully, truly, and from thy heart thankful to the gods.

Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things, and how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it. Thou must now at last perceive of what universe thou art a part, and of what administrator of the universe thy existence is an efflux, and that a limit of time is fixed for thee, which if thou dost not use for clearing away the clouds from thy mind, it will go and thou wilt go, and it will never return.

Every moment think steadily as a Roman and a man to do what thou hast in hand with perfect and simple dignity, and feeling of affection, and freedom, and justice; and to give thyself relief from all other thoughts. And thou wilt give thyself relief, if thou doest every act of thy life as if it were the last, laying aside all carelessness and passionate aversion from the commands of reason, and all hypocrisy, and self-love, and discontent with the portion which has been given to thee. Thou seest how few the things are, the which if a man lays hold of, he is able to live a life which flows in quiet, and is like the existence of the gods; for the gods on their part will require nothing more from him who observes these things.

Do wrong to thyself, do wrong to thyself, my soul; but thou wilt no longer have the opportunity of honouring thyself. Every man’s life is sufficient. But thine is nearly finished, though thy soul reverences not itself but places thy felicity in the souls of others.

Do the things external which fall upon thee distract thee? Give thyself time to learn something new and good, and cease to be whirled around. But then thou must also avoid being carried about the other way. For those too are triflers who have wearied themselves in life by their activity, and yet have no object to which to direct every movement, and, in a word, all their thoughts.

Through not observing what is in the mind of another a man has seldom been seen to be unhappy; but those who do not observe the movements of their own minds must of necessity be unhappy.

This thou must always bear in mind, what is the nature of the whole, and what is my nature, and how this is related to that, and what kind of a part it is of what kind of a whole; and that there is no one who hinders thee from always doing and saying the things which are according to the nature of which thou art a part.

Theophrastus, in his comparison of bad acts⁠—such a comparison as one would make in accordance with the common notions of mankind⁠—says, like a true philosopher, that the offences which are committed through desire are more blameable than those which are committed through anger. For he who is excited by anger seems to turn away from reason with a certain pain and unconscious contraction; but he who offends through desire, being overpowered by pleasure, seems to be in a manner more intemperate and more womanish in his offences. Rightly then, and in a way worthy of philosophy, he said that the offence which is committed with pleasure is more blameable than that which is committed with pain; and on the whole the one is more like a person who has been first wronged and through pain is compelled to be angry; but the other is moved by his own impulse to do wrong, being carried towards doing something by desire.

Since it is possible that thou mayest depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly. But to go away from among men, if there are gods, is not a thing to be afraid of, for the gods will not involve thee in evil; but if indeed they do not exist, or if they have no concern about human affairs, what is it to me to live in a universe devoid of gods or devoid of Providence? But in truth they do exist, and they do care for human things, and they have put all the means in man’s power to enable him not to fall into real evils. And as to the rest, if there was anything evil, they would have provided for this also, that it should be altogether in a man’s power not to fall into it. Now that which does not make a man worse, how can it make a man’s life worse? But neither through ignorance, nor having the knowledge, but not the power to guard against or correct these things, is it possible that the nature of the universe has overlooked them; nor is it possible that it has made so great a mistake, either through want of power or want of skill, that good and evil should happen indiscriminately to the good and the bad. But death certainly, and life, honour and dishonour, pain and pleasure, all these things equally happen to good men and bad, being things which make us neither better nor worse. Therefore they are neither good nor evil.

How quickly all things disappear, in the universe the bodies themselves, but in time the remembrance of them; what is the nature of all sensible things, and particularly those which attract with the bait of pleasure or terrify by pain, or are noised abroad by vapoury fame; how worthless, and contemptible, and sordid, and perishable, and dead they are⁠—all this it is the part of the intellectual faculty to observe. To observe too who these are whose opinions and voices give reputation; what death is, and the fact that, if a man looks at it in itself, and by the abstractive power of reflection resolves into their parts all the things which present themselves to the imagination in it, he will then consider it to be nothing else than an operation of nature; and if anyone is afraid of an operation of nature, he is a child. This, however, is not only an operation of nature, but it is also a thing which conduces to the purposes of nature. To observe too how man comes near to the deity, and by what part of him, and when this part of man is so disposed.

Nothing is more wretched than a man who traverses everything in a round, and pries into the things beneath the earth, as the poet says, and seeks by conjecture what is in the minds of his neighbours, without perceiving that it is sufficient to attend to the daemon within him, and to reverence it sincerely. And reverence of the daemon consists in keeping it pure from passion and thoughtlessness, and dissatisfaction with what comes from gods and men. For the things from the gods merit veneration for their excellence; and the things from men should be dear to us by reason of kinship; and sometimes even, in a manner, they move our pity by reason of men’s ignorance of good and bad; this defect being not less than that which deprives us of the power of distinguishing things that are white and black.

Though thou shouldst be going to live three thousand years, and as many times ten thousand years, still remember that no man loses any other life than this which he now lives, nor lives any other than this which he now loses. The longest and shortest are thus brought to the same. For the present is the same to all, though that which perishes is not the same; and so that which is lost appears to be a mere moment. For a man cannot lose either the past or the future: for what a man has not, how can anyone take this from him? These two things then thou must bear in mind; the one, that all things from eternity are of like forms and come round in a circle, and that it makes no difference whether a man shall see the same things during a hundred years or two hundred, or an infinite time; and the second, that the longest liver and he who will die soonest lose just the same. For the present is the only thing of which a man can be deprived, if it is true that this is the only thing which he has, and that a man cannot lose a thing if he has it not.

Remember that all is opinion. For what was said by the Cynic Monimus is manifest: and manifest too is the use of what was said, if a man receives what may be got out of it as far as it is true.

The soul of man does violence to itself, first of all, when it becomes an abscess and, as it were, a tumour on the universe, so far as it can. For to be vexed at anything which happens is a separation of ourselves from nature, in some part of which the natures of all other things are contained. In the next place, the soul does violence to itself when it turns away from any man, or even moves towards him with the intention of injuring, such as are the souls of those who are angry. In the third place, the soul does violence to itself when it is overpowered by pleasure or by pain. Fourthly, when it plays a part, and does or says anything insincerely and untruly. Fifthly, when it allows any act of its own and any movement to be without an aim, and does anything thoughtlessly and without considering what it is, it being right that even the smallest things be done with reference to an end; and the end of rational animals is to follow the reason and the law of the most ancient city and polity.

Of human life the time is a point, and the substance is in a flux, and the perception dull, and the composition of the whole body subject to putrefaction, and the soul a whirl, and fortune hard to divine, and fame a thing devoid of judgement. And, to say all in a word, everything which belongs to the body is a stream, and what belongs to the soul is a dream and vapour, and life is a warfare and a stranger’s sojourn, and after-fame is oblivion. What then is that which is able to conduct a man? One thing and only one, philosophy. But this consists in keeping the daemon within a man free from violence and unharmed, superior to pains and pleasures, doing nothing without purpose, nor yet falsely and with hypocrisy, not feeling the need of another man’s doing or not doing anything; and besides, accepting all that happens, and all that is allotted, as coming from thence, wherever it is, from whence he himself came; and, finally, waiting for death with a cheerful mind, as being nothing else than a dissolution of the elements of which every living being is compounded. But if there is no harm to the elements themselves in each continually changing into another, why should a man have any apprehension about the change and dissolution of all the elements? For it is according to nature, and nothing is evil which is according to nature.

This in Carnuntum.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Book Two opens with one of the most famous morning routines in literature. Marcus tells himself to begin the day by expecting trouble: the busybody, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious. This is not pessimism. He is rehearsing, the way an athlete might picture a hard match. The point comes right after: these people behave badly because they do not understand what is truly good and truly bad. Since wrongdoing is a kind of ignorance, they cannot really hurt him, and he has no reason to hate them. He calls them his kin, sharing the same intelligence, and he reaches for a homely image, that we were made to work together like feet, hands, eyelids and rows of teeth. To turn on one another is to go against our own design.

Next comes a sudden, almost brutal turn inward. Marcus asks what he actually is, and answers that he is a little flesh, a little breath, and the ruling part. The ruling part is his reasoning mind, the piece of him that judges and chooses. He tells himself to throw away his books, since there is no time left for more reading, and to stop letting that ruling part be pulled around like a puppet on strings. Notice the tone. He is talking to an old man, and he is impatient with him. The book is not a lecture to the world. It is a man leaning on his own shoulder.

The middle of the book circles the idea of Providence and nature. Everything that happens, he argues, is woven into the order of the whole universe, and what is good for the whole is good for each part. So complaining about what happens is like a hand complaining about the body. Underneath the abstract language is a practical habit. When something goes wrong, do not ask why this is happening to me. Ask what the situation requires of me.

The ending is the key to the whole book. Marcus gathers it into a list: the body is a stream, the soul a dream and vapour, life is a warfare and a stranger's sojourn, and fame after death is oblivion. Then he asks what can guide a person through all this, and answers with a single word, philosophy. By that he means a way of keeping the guiding spirit within you unharmed, doing nothing without purpose, never lying or putting on a show, and waiting for death with a cheerful mind. His last argument is gentle. If nothing is harmed when elements change into other elements, why fear our own change? Death, he says, is according to nature, and nothing according to nature is evil. A first-time reader might miss that this bleakness is meant to free us, not crush us. If everything fades, then petty grievances and vanity are not worth carrying.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged purely as writing, Book Two is uneven, and that is part of its honesty. It was never edited, polished or arranged for publication. Marcus jumps from the afterlife to the proper behavior of teeth to the shape of a good death. Some passages are brilliant, compressed to a single flinty sentence. Others are flat repetitions of Stoic doctrine, the sort of thing a student writes in a notebook to drill himself. If you came to this as a normal book you might feel it wander, and you would be right. Its shape is that of a habit, not an argument.

What is masterful is the voice. Rarely in literature does someone with that much power talk to himself with such unsparing clarity. The commands come in the second person, thou in the older translation used here, and they sting precisely because there is no audience to impress. When he says that he has been putting these things off, and that the opportunity from the gods keeps being wasted, you feel a real person caught in an old failing. The images, too, are sharp and plain: the puppet on strings, the stream, the stranger passing through a foreign land. They are the kind of images that stay in the mind after the argument has gone.

There are real weaknesses, though. The theology is a strain. Marcus leans on Providence and the intelligence of the universe, and then, in other places, allows that it might all just be atoms. A modern reader may not find the cosmic reassurance convincing, and Marcus himself seems to hedge. The sweeping claim that nothing according to nature is evil can sound glib when you consider the plague that was killing people around him, or the wars he was ordering. He treats pain and loss as matters of opinion, and anyone who has grieved may find that too neat. There is also some self-righteousness in the way he sorts people into the ignorant and the wise, even as he insists he should not feel anger.

The Long translation, from the nineteenth century, adds its own difficulty. The thees and thous give the book a sermon-like dignity that the Greek, which is blunt and everyday, probably does not have. Newer translations read more like notes jotted down by a tired man, which is closer to the truth of the thing.

Still, the weaknesses are tied to the strengths. Because the book is not trying to be impressive, it never performs. The repetition is the point, since Marcus must keep reminding himself, and the reader senses how hard the lesson is. The contradictions feel like a mind at work. A tidier book would be a lesser one, because we would trust it less. The roughness is evidence that someone was actually trying to live this way, and failing, and trying again.

III. The Impact It Had

The astonishing thing about the Meditations is that it was never meant to have an impact at all. Marcus did not title it or publish it. The Greek words usually given for it mean something like to himself, and it seems to have survived by a chain of lucky copying. A Byzantine scholar named Arethas, around the tenth century, owned a copy and quoted it. The first printed edition appeared in Zurich in 1559. From there it traveled slowly through the European educated classes, and it became a book that people kept close, partly for its wisdom and partly because of the strange fact of who wrote it.

Its first great influence was moral. In the English-speaking world, the early translation by Meric Casaubon in 1634 and then George Long's in 1862 made it a staple of Victorian self-improvement. The poet and critic Matthew Arnold wrote a well-known essay on Marcus Aurelius in 1863, presenting him as a saintly pagan who did without the comforts of Christian faith and still managed to be good. That image, of a lonely, honorable man holding the line, shaped how generations read him.

The book also helped keep Stoicism alive as a living practice. Stoics like Epictetus, whom Marcus read closely, had taught that we control only our own judgments and choices. The Meditations showed that idea under pressure, in a tent in the cold, with real consequences. In the twentieth century, the French scholar Pierre Hadot argued in a widely admired book, The Inner Citadel, that Marcus was practicing spiritual exercises, daily drills to reshape his own thinking, rather than building a theory. That reading changed how philosophers understand ancient thought itself, as a way of life rather than only an argument.

In modern times its reach has become enormous and oddly practical. Soldiers, executives, athletes and ordinary people dealing with anxiety return to it. Gregory Hays's 2002 translation, in brisk modern English, brought it to a new audience, and the recent revival of interest in Stoicism, in self-help books and online communities, owes a great deal to it. Book Two's morning rehearsal of bad behavior and its reminder that life is a stranger's sojourn are quoted constantly.

It has also reached popular culture. The film Gladiator, released in 2000, features a thoughtful, dying Marcus, though the film's story of his death at the hands of his son is invented. Historians would say the real Marcus did name his son Commodus as heir, and that this is often counted as a failure of his rule, which only deepens the sadness of reading a book so devoted to duty. The book's impact, then, is mostly the impact of a voice. It has lasted not because it changed laws or armies but because generations of readers have recognized themselves in an emperor who was just as tempted to complain as they are.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing readers have loved is the strange intimacy. Most books by great rulers are justifications, memoirs polished for history. This one is a private self-correction. Readers find it almost shocking that the most powerful man in the Western world spent his scarce free hours telling himself to be patient with people who irritated him. The gap between his power and his humility is what hooks people. We are used to power corrupting, and here is a case where it seems to have been met with relentless self-examination.

Readers also love how practical it is. Book Two gives you tools you can use before breakfast. Expect difficult people, and you will be less surprised. Remember that those who do wrong are mistaken rather than evil, and your anger softens. Remember that you are a small and temporary thing, and ambition becomes lighter. People who have never read any philosophy find that these ideas work like a cold glass of water in the middle of an argument or a bad day. It is rare for a text nearly two thousand years old to address a twenty-first century commute, but this one seems to.

There is also comfort in its honesty about death. Marcus does not pretend to know what happens after. He is willing to say life is a stranger's sojourn and fame is oblivion, and yet the result is peace, not despair. Many readers going through illness, grief or old age have said, in countless personal accounts, that the book steadied them. The thought that death is a change of elements, as natural as the seasons, can feel like permission to stop being afraid.

Writers and leaders have admired it for different reasons. The Victorians saw in Marcus a model of conscience. Soldiers and statesmen have valued the discipline. Some modern readers value its psychology, noting how close it is to techniques used in cognitive therapy, where you examine your judgments about events rather than the events themselves. Marcus's line of thought, that harm depends on opinion, anticipates that approach, though he did not name it so, and later parts of the book put it even more sharply.

Finally, readers love the sheer beauty of certain sentences. The comparison of people to feet, hands, eyelids and teeth is oddly charming. The idea of a soul as a dream and vapour is haunting. Even the nagging address to himself, the way he commands his own mind to stop being a puppet, has a poetry of severity. And there is something moving about the fact that this brave and anxious voice was never meant to be heard. Reading it feels like finding a diary on a hillside, and discovering that it is wiser than you are.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The criticisms of Book Two fall into a few groups, some about philosophy, some about the man, and some about how we read him. It is worth saying first that, since the book was private, there are no contemporary reviews of it in any normal sense. Its critics are later readers, and their objections have grown more pointed as the book has become a symbol.

The oldest philosophical complaint is about detachment. The Stoic program tells us to treat external events as indifferent and to protect only our inner judgment. Critics have long wondered whether this is a retreat from life. If loss, pain and injustice are only matters of opinion, then caring about suffering might seem to be a mistake. Defenders answer that Stoics urged active duty and justice, and Marcus clearly did, since he kept working at his post. But the worry remains that the philosophy can become a way of not feeling, a polished refusal to grieve. Readers who have gone through real loss sometimes find the tone chilly.

A second objection concerns the metaphysics. Marcus's trust in Providence and a rational universe is hard to square with the world he lived in. Plague killed vast numbers across the empire, and he was fighting costly wars. The notion that everything is arranged for the good of the whole can sound like a rationalization of whatever happens. Marcus himself gives himself an exit by suggesting it might be atoms after all, and some modern readers feel that this makes his certainty look thin.

There are also criticisms of the man that color how people read him. Marcus's reign saw persecution of Christians in some places, including at Lyons in 177, and historians debate how much he knew or approved. The contrast between his gentle private voice and the violent business of empire troubles many. His choice to make his son Commodus emperor, who proved cruel and erratic, has been called a grave failure of judgment. Critics ask whether a book about justice can be taken at face value from a ruler who presided over such things. A fair reply is that the book is exactly the record of a man holding himself to a standard he sometimes missed, but the tension is real.

Finally, some scholars criticize the modern popularity itself. The recent Stoic revival is sometimes accused of turning Marcus into a productivity coach, stripping away the strange religious and cosmic elements and leaving only advice to keep calm and carry on. A famous emperor's private anguish becomes a branded self-help slogan. These critics argue that reading him properly means accepting how foreign his world was, rather than treating him as a friend who just happens to wear a toga. Even that complaint, though, is a kind of tribute to the book's staying power.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Book Two belongs to the tradition of Stoic philosophy, which began in Athens around 300 BC with Zeno of Citium, who taught under a painted porch, the stoa, that gave the school its name. Stoicism spread to Rome, where it became a philosophy of public life. Seneca, a statesman and playwright, wrote letters and essays on it. Epictetus, who had been born a slave, taught it in a lecture room, and his student Arrian wrote down what he said. Marcus knew this lineage well, and in Book One of the Meditations he thanks the people who shaped him, including the teacher who introduced him to Epictetus. So Book Two sits at the end of a long line of thinkers who treated philosophy as a discipline for living.

What makes the Meditations unusual within that line is its form. Seneca writes polished letters to a friend. Epictetus talks to students. Marcus talks to no one. The nearest relatives might be the later tradition of spiritual diaries and notebooks, such as the Confessions of Augustine, which is also an inward address, though to God. It also shares something with the short, aphoristic style of later writers like Pascal and Montaigne, who both read the ancient moralists, though they worked in very different forms.

The book also descends from older Greek ideas. His image of everything as a stream owes something to Heraclitus, the thinker who said you cannot step into the same river twice. His picture of the universe as a single living order owes much to earlier Stoic physics. Yet he applies these ideas with a very Roman focus on duty, service and the common good.

What descends from it is wide. Its moral seriousness fed Christian and Renaissance humanist ethics. The Enlightenment admired Marcus as a philosopher-king, and Edward Gibbon, in his history of Rome's decline, praised the age of the Antonine emperors as among the happiest in history, with Marcus as its final figure. In our time its ideas live inside therapy, military resilience training and the popular Stoic movement.

It is useful to compare it with other short, intense works of self-discipline, like the Enchiridion of Epictetus, the Tao Te Ching, or the Bhagavad Gita, which also faces a warrior with the problem of acting without clinging to results. Each addresses the question of how to live well with limited control. Marcus's answer is perhaps the most human, because it is so obviously unfinished.

Why read it today? Because it shows what it looks like to take wisdom seriously when you are tired, irritated and responsible for others. It will not solve your problems. It does offer something rarer, the company of someone who has been there, speaking plainly into the dark. In an age of constant performance and distraction, a book written for no audience has a strange power.
