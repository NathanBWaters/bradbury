House Divided: Yun Posun, Chang Myon, and Korea's Nine-Month Democracy

Korea — How two victorious allies tore apart Seoul's parliamentary experiment before the generals ever rolled across the river (1960–1962 (Second Republic: Yun Posun & Chang Myon)) • 15 min read

“I regret that I made so little contribution to the nation that a military revolution has occurred.” — Yun Posun, statement following the May 1961 coup

Key Context & Why It Matters

In the spring of 1960, university students in Seoul toppled twelve years of autocratic rule and handed the country its purest chance at self-government. Lawmakers spent six heated weeks rewriting the charter to strip presidential decrees down to ceremonial dignity while vesting real authority in a prime minister backed by parliament. Everything looked ready for a stable democratic rebirth. One fatal flaw sat inside the landslide: the victorious ruling party's two wings detested each other almost as fiercely as the dictator they'd chased into exile.

Sweat darkened stiff summer collars inside Seoul's domed Capitol chamber on August 17, 1960, as clerks unlocked a wooden ballot box and unfolded paper slips onto green felt. Newly elected lawmakers leaned forward across mahogany desks waiting for one routine formality. Three weeks earlier, their Democratic Party had swept two-thirds of the lower house in post-dictatorship elections. Now President Yun Posun had sent over his first nominee for prime minister, veteran party financier Kim Do-yeon. Clerks tallied the stack aloud and stopped dead. Out of 227 members present, Kim drew 111 yes votes against 112 no, leaving him three shy of the needed majority. Half the winning party had just kneecapped its own president on the floor.

Nobody watching from the packed press gallery could pretend that shock was an accident. Four months earlier, high schoolers and college marchers had faced police bullets across central Seoul during the April Revolution, forcing aging strongman Syngman Rhee onto an American evacuation plane to Hawaii. Determined never to crown another life president, reform legislators spent June tearing up the old charter. On June 15, 1960, they created Korea's Second Republic under a British-style parliamentary cabinet. Real executive authority shifted to a prime minister chosen by a two-chamber National Assembly, while the president shrank into a ceremonial head of state elected jointly by both chambers.

On paper, that blueprint looked airtight. Voters reinforced the mandate on July 29, handing the Democratic Party 175 of 233 lower-house seats because they trusted the coalition that'd spent years enduring police batons together. Victory snapped that glue instantly. On August 12, both legislative chambers elected sixty-two-year-old Yun Posun as president with 208 out of 259 votes. Every reporter in Seoul knew his first task was naming a prime minister. They also knew the ruling tent held two hostile camps who barely shared a dining room.

North of the Capitol in Anguk-dong stood Yun Posun's sprawling tiled family compound, an aristocratic island of old Korea. Born into the storied Haepyeong Yun clan and raised as nephew to famous independence reformer Yun Chi-ho, Posun had studied archaeology at the University of Edinburgh during colonial rule before working as Seoul's mayor and commerce minister. He carried himself like landed gentry. To his mind, ancient family lineage entitled his conservative Old Faction to hold the cabinet keys. The party's rival wing smelled to him of upstart technocrats and unprincipled compromise. That wasn't private snobbery. It hardened into open refusal whenever allies asked him to share cabinet posts.

Waiting downtown inside the Bando Hotel's Suite 809 sat his chief opponent, sixty-year-old Chang Myon. A devout Roman Catholic educator holding a degree from Manhattan College in New York, Chang had directed a Seoul boys' school under Japanese occupation before traveling to Washington as South Korea's first ambassador. Back in September 1956, stepping onto the stage at Seoul's Municipal Hall as opposition vice president, he'd survived an assassin's pistol shot that tore right through his left palm. Rumor pinned that shooting on ruling-party thugs, though courts never traced the top order. That healed scar won deep affection among urban reformers and younger New Faction lawmakers who prized policy skill over noble bloodlines.

Kim Do-yeon's rejection forced Yun Posun to swallow hard. Two days after that floor humiliation, on August 19, Yun reluctantly nominated Chang Myon instead. Even then, Old Faction loyalists worked behind the scenes to sink the ballot, leaving the new prime minister scraping through by 117 votes to 107. That's how crippled the launch looked. Chang took office clutching a fragile ten-vote margin inside an assembly supposedly owned by his own coalition.

Trouble crashed through the Capitol doors before Chang finished unpacking his desk. Lifting twelve years of police censorship uncorked every pent-up grief in a war-scarred country where average yearly income still hovered below ninety dollars. Between August and the following spring, more than eighteen hundred street demonstrations flooded Seoul and provincial plazas, drawing roughly a million cumulative marchers. Teachers rallied for legal unions while unpaid dockworkers chained factory gates. Grieving families from wartime civilian massacres demanded exhumations, and student leagues pressed for direct talks with North Korea. Radio bulletins carried fresh picket lines almost hourly, convincing rural onlookers that the capital's center had stopped holding.

Seoul's uniformed constables watched most marches with hands buried deep inside trouser pockets. Over the summer, lawmakers had dismissed thousands of senior police officers tainted by Rhee's rigged March ballots, leaving precinct houses understaffed and afraid to swing a single baton. Crowd control collapsed into paralysis. On October 11, 1960, wounded April uprising veterans brushed past Capitol guards, stormed into the lower house chamber, and seized the speaker's microphone demanding harsher punishment for former regime officials. Shaken legislators bowed to the gallery and passed retroactive laws revoking civil rights from hundreds of Rhee-era politicians and commanders—infuriating legal purists and army brass alike.

Parliamentary discipline dissolved during the very same weeks. Smarting from their August defeat, Yun Posun's Old Faction followers bolted in November to register the rival New Democratic Party. That split stripped Chang's cabinet of any dependable majority. Every routine budget bill now demanded exhausting midnight bargains among splinter clubs inside Bando Hotel corridors, forcing Chang through three messy cabinet reshuffles in barely nine months.

Wait before accepting the army's favorite excuse for what followed. Later junta pamphlets claimed Chang Myon's ministers simply froze while Seoul burned. Behind closed doors, his economic team actually attacked structural distortions Syngman Rhee had shielded for a decade.

Currency reform came first. Rhee's treasury had kept an artificially high official exchange rate that priced American aid dollars far below street value, letting favored importers buy cheap currency and resell goods at huge markups while honest domestic mills starved. In January and February 1961, Chang's finance ministry devalued the hwan in two bold leaps—first to one thousand, then toward thirteen hundred hwan per dollar—drying up black-market arbitrage and rewarding real exports. Next, ministers targeted desperate hunger before spring barley harvests. Launching the National Construction Service in early 1961, the cabinet hired jobless university graduates to supervise public-works crews paving gravel roads and reinforcing river dikes for cash and grain rations.

Behind those work crews sat an even bolder blueprint. Working inside the Capitol's planning council, Chang's young staff drafted a Five-Year Development Plan centered on coal mines and electric power dams as well as cement and fertilizer plants. They didn't survive long enough to pour one turbine base. Even so, those typed binders mapped the exact state-steered industrial engine Korea's incoming military junta would pull off government shelves and rebrand as its own miracle.

Washington doubted those blueprints could outrun street anger. In February 1961, senior American aid official Hugh Farley quit his Seoul post in frustration. He mailed an eighteen-page warning to John F. Kennedy's White House calling South Korea a sick society choked by graft and cabinet drift. Farley feared an explosion around the April 19 uprising's first anniversary. That milestone passed peacefully, yet Chang's grip stayed brittle. Whenever the prime minister floated modest protest-control bills in March, torchlight rallies surged back onto Jongno avenues accusing him of reviving Rhee's police state.

What Chang Myon never learned to command was the army parked beyond city limits. Over six hundred thousand soldiers crowded barracks in a republic poorer than many colonial outposts, watching senior generals skim fuel and construction kickbacks while inflation ate away junior pay. Younger colonels from the Military Academy's eighth class—led by a sharp-tongued strategist named Kim Jong-pil—mounted a purification drive in 1960 demanding corrupt commanders step down. Army brass jailed the plotters briefly with light reprimands, then sent word to Suite 809 that restless brigade officers were eyeing Seoul.

Chang waved those rumors aside. A mild-mannered schoolmaster who trusted constitutional ballots and American backing, he kept assuring reporters that Korean soldiers knew their place in a democracy. He hadn't built loyalist cells inside any combat division either. Across town at the Blue House, President Yun Posun listened to very different whispers. Yun nursed fierce contempt for Chang's faction and scarcely hid his conviction that the prime minister couldn't keep order.

Before sunrise on May 16, 1961, urgent telephone calls shattered sleep inside both the Bando Hotel and the presidential palace. Rebel columns had crossed the Han River and severed downtown switchboards. Inside Suite 809, panicked aides pulled Chang Myon down back service stairs and hurried him to the Hyehwa-dong Carmelite Convent, where nuns locked heavy timber gates behind the prime minister. Chang vanished from public contact for two critical days. Without his voice on radio or his signature on dispatch orders, loyal field commanders groped in the dark while coup brigades seized key bridges.

At the Blue House sitting room that morning, American diplomat Marshall Green and United Nations Commander Carter Magruder pleaded with Yun Posun to rescue constitutional rule. Loyal First Army divisions along the eastern front stood ready to march south and crush the small mutiny if Yun signed suppression orders. They'd expected a quick civilian signature. Instead, the aristocratic president looked across the carpet and refused. Ordering Korean soldiers against fellow countrymen, Yun insisted, would spill fraternal blood and tempt northern tanks over the border. Turning toward coup officers entering his parlor, he murmured the words that branded his presidency forever: what was bound to happen has arrived.

That fatal remark froze loyalist armor in its tracks. Deprived of Yun's signature and Chang's radio command, hesitant generals stood down while American officials withheld combat troops. Two days later, on May 18, Chang Myon slipped out of the silent convent and walked into the Capitol for one bleak cabinet session. Seated beside stunned ministers, he signed his formal resignation so civil war wouldn't tear up the capital—and stepped out into military confinement.

Yun Posun lingered inside the emptied Blue House for ten uneasy months as the junta's ceremonial shield, hoping generals might restore civilian conservatives once order settled. That illusion snapped in March 1962. Park Chung-hee's ruling council enacted the Political Activities Purification Law, barring over four thousand former officeholders—including Chang Myon—from public life. On March 22, 1962, Yun resigned in protest, trading palace isolation for bitter opposition campaigns against Park in 1963 and 1967.

Neither founder ever saw his broken experiment rebuilt in his lifetime. Chang Myon lived under plainclothes surveillance in Myeongnyun-dong until dying in June 1966, mourned by Catholic parishioners while state textbooks mocked his nine months as chaos. Over in Anguk-dong's shaded courtyard, Yun Posun spent his remaining years sheltering young dissidents who fought military rule. He died in 1990, nearly three decades after two stubborn halves of one winning party let Korea's first parliamentary republic fall without a single loyal regiment marching to its defense.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The Unresolved Debate

Scholars still divide sharply over whether South Korea's Second Republic died of fatal constitutional flaws or sheer political sabotage. Academic Sungjoo Han argued in The Failure of Democracy in South Korea that acute ideological polarization tore the fragile republic apart. Progressive students demanded swift purges and northern dialogue while conservative landowners feared losing social control. Sociologist Gregory Henderson read the same collapse differently in Korea: The Politics of the Vortex. He blamed a hyper-centralized culture lacking strong civic buffers, which sucked every ambitious faction toward executive spoils instead of coalition bargaining. On that reading, no charter could survive winners who treated parliamentary rivals like mortal enemies. Historians remain split on whether steadier statecraft could've bought crucial time.

2. The Road Not Taken

Consider the narrow window between August and November 1960, before Yun Posun's Old Faction bolted to form the New Democratic Party. Had Yun and Kim Do-yeon negotiated a real power-sharing cabinet with Chang Myon—dividing major ministries fairly rather than ambushing nominees on the floor—their alliance would've commanded nearly two-thirds of parliament. A united governing bench could've backed measured crowd policing, trimmed bloated army rosters gradually, and won early backing from John F. Kennedy's incoming administration. Parliamentary unity wouldn't have erased poverty immediately. Even so, it might've denied restless colonels their cleanest excuse to march across the river and seize downtown Seoul.

3. How It Shapes Daily Life Today

Whenever South Korean lawmakers debate revising the constitution today, the ghost of 1960 walks right into the hearing room. Voters and drafters remember how fast a pure parliamentary cabinet fractured into floor ambushes while daily marches shook downtown avenues. That deep institutional scar keeps Korea fiercely wedded to directly elected presidents with fixed single terms, even when critics warn that imperial palaces breed patronage. Step inside central economic ministries too and you trace Chang Myon's quieter administrative imprint. Young civil servants recruited through his 1961 National Construction Service staffed infrastructure and planning desks for decades, proving technocratic statecraft didn't begin with army boots.

4. Essential Books, Films & Places to Go Deeper

For a wide-angle portrait of this turbulent transition, start with Carter J. Eckert and his co-authors in Korea Old and New: A History, paired with Bruce Cumings' sharp narrative in Korea's Place in the Sun. Primary-source readers can explore declassified embassy cables in Foreign Relations of the United States alongside Marshall Green's candid oral history, which captures Seoul's panic hour by hour as generals closed the bridges. Korean Film Archive newsreels also capture the electric street energy of 1960 raw and unfiltered. In Seoul, walk the preserved timber courtyard of Yun Posun's ancestral house in Anguk-dong or visit Chang Myon's modest home in Myeongnyun-dong, then climb the hillside paths of the April 19th National Cemetery in Suyu-dong.
