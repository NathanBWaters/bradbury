The Last Crowns of Old Korea: Emperor Gojong, Emperor Sunjong, and the Twilight of the Korean Empire

Korea — From a predawn palanquin escape in Seoul to electric streetcars, secret envoys at The Hague, and the 1910 annexation (1897–1910 (Korean Empire: Gojong & Sunjong)) • 20 min read

“Without needing to officially declare it to the world, the world already knows the designation of Daehan.” — Emperor Gojong, Annals of King Gojong, October 11, 1897

Key Context & Why It Matters

At the close of the nineteenth century, Seoul stood caught between a collapsing Qing dynasty in Beijing and ambitious military empires in Tokyo and St. Petersburg. Behind high palace stone walls, a haunted monarch watched foreign envoys bargain over northern timber belts and iron railroad franchises right around his throne. Could an ancient royal court remake itself into a modern sovereign empire fast enough to survive predatory neighbors—or would autocratic palace habits undo the crown before outside armies marched?

Two curtained sedan chairs slipped out the rear gate of Gyeongbokgung Palace before sunrise on February 11, 1896. Sentries backed by Tokyo watched the main stone arches, expecting routine errands by palace women moving through winter darkness. Instead, forty-three-year-old King Gojong sat cramped behind heavy silk blinds in one wooden litter. Crown Prince Cheok, only twenty-one, rode silent in the second. Bearers hurried south down icy lanes toward the diplomatic quarter in Jeongdong. When iron gates parted at the Russian Legation, Minister Karl Ivanovich Weber ushered both royals into his brick compound. Bolts slid shut behind them. For the first time in five centuries of Joseon rule, Korea's monarch governed from beneath a foreign flag.

Why would an anointed king crawl out of his own capital before breakfast? Four months earlier, Japanese mercenaries and local conspirators had stormed Gyeongbokgung at dawn to murder Queen Min in her sleeping quarters. Gojong became a prisoner in that gilded courtyard, ringed by a pro-Japanese cabinet whose rifles pointed inward. He'd grown terrified of poison at dinner, tasting almost nothing sent from royal kitchens unless trusted Western missionaries delivered boiled eggs sealed in unbroken shells. Safe inside Russian guest rooms, Gojong outlawed the cabinet tied to his wife's killing. Premier Kim Hong-jip was dragged from street shelter and beaten to death near Gwanghwamun by furious crowds.

From Weber's reception parlor, Gojong spent twelve months dismantling Tokyo's grip. He rescinded forced topknot-cutting decrees that had sparked rural unrest across provinces, while inviting Russian instructors to drill a loyal guard battalion. That refuge carried a steep price tag. St. Petersburg didn't house a fugitive monarch out of charity. Czarist agents secured timber rights along northern rivers and lucrative mining claims, gaining sharp leverage over Seoul's treasury. Gojong had escaped Japanese bayonets only to discover how quickly one imperial patron turns into an unwanted landlord.

Living inside another nation's diplomatic wing couldn't last. In February 1897, Gojong marched back across Jeongdong into Gyeongungung, known today as Deoksugung, wedged directly beside British and American legations so Western witnesses could watch every gate. Eight months later, on October 12, 1897, the king climbed newly laid stone tiers at Hwangudan Altar while court musicians struck bronze bells through morning frost. Shedding tributary crimson garments, he donned imperial yellow silk robes reserved previously for Chinese emperors alone. He proclaimed the Great Han Empire, or Daehan Jeguk, choosing Gwangmu as his reign title. That ceremony sent one blunt message to rival capitals: Korea bowed to no neighbor.

To make that sovereignty visible, Gojong rebuilt Deoksugung into an international diplomatic stage where ceremony worked like armor. Russian architect Afanasy Seredin-Sabatin drew plans for Jeonggwanheon, an airy veranda pavilion where the emperor planned to drink roasted coffee and host foreign guests beneath carved wooden eaves. Brass bands played European marches on palace lawns. Court tailors stitched gold-braided uniforms modeled on German officers. Gojong believed pageantry and stone reception halls proved Korea belonged among modern powers. He'd bet royal survival on imperial pomp, even while downtown alleys murmured over court extravagance.

Poison slipped past those guarded gates anyway. On September 11, 1898, royal servants carried steaming coffee trays toward the evening table. Disgraced Russian-legation interpreter Kim Hong-ryuk, recently fired for graft, had bribed a kitchen aide to dump heavy doses of opium into the pot. Gojong lifted his porcelain cup and caught a bitter reek beneath the roast; he spat the dark mouthful onto the floorboards. Twenty-four-year-old Crown Prince Cheok swallowed deeply before warning cries rang out. The young heir collapsed vomiting blood, surviving only through frantic doctoring that cost him his teeth and left him permanently frail. Whenever Gojong looked at his son afterward, he saw a diminished heir waiting behind the throne.

Six weeks later, citizens testing the bounds of imperial rule jammed downtown crossroads. Founded by American-educated physician Philip Jaisohn, the Independence Club packed autumn street rallies with merchants and students demanding open budgets and an elected advisory council. Gojong initially flattered speakers with polite edicts. When court conservatives whispered that reformers meant to scrap the monarchy and declare a republic, the emperor slammed the door shut. He didn't rely on soldiers alone. Loyalists activated the conservative Imperial Association, sending itinerant peddler guilds swinging heavy oak cudgels to smash rally tents before a November decree banned the club altogether.

Crushing dissent never cooled Gojong's appetite for modern machinery. Joining forces with American contractors Henry Collbran and Harry Bostwick in the Hanseong Electric Company, he funded a brick coal generator near Dongdaemun and spiked steel rails down Seoul's broad central spine. On May 17, 1899, bright yellow streetcars clanged from Seodaemun Gate past Jongno toward Cheongnyangni while amazed spectators lined wooden eaves. Nine days later, that triumph snapped when an inbound trolley fatally struck a five-year-old boy near Pagoda Park. Rumors swept tea stalls that iron tracks offended earth spirits and choked off spring rain. Angry onlookers and out-of-work palanquin bearers dragged the motorman from his cab and tipped one car sideways, torching it to twisted iron.

Electric light reshaped Seoul's nights despite the trolley riot. Within months, arc lamps hung by Collbran and Bostwick cast pale circles over market stalls and palace verandas, turning darkness into visible proof of imperial ambition. Financial strain soured the partnership fast. When cash-strapped officials fell behind on installment bills for power plants and municipal waterworks, the American contractors clamped heavy mortgage liens directly onto the tramway franchise. After dark, Seoul glowed under borrowed light while the ownership deeds rested in foreign vaults.

Royal bureaus simultaneously tried locking Korean sovereignty onto army rosters and tax maps. In June 1899, Gojong formed the Board of Marshals—known as Wonsubu—to bring expanding infantry battalions under personal imperial command, buying Mauser rifles and field cannons from Europe. Downtown, a British customs chief shielded harbor duties from reckless spending while widening mud lanes and fencing Pagoda Park as Seoul's first public garden. American diplomats steered northern gold leases at Unsan toward Western syndicates to tether outside investors to Korean survival. Royal surveyors also launched the Gwangmu land survey, dragging brass chains across paddies to issue modern property deeds.

That modernization budget depended on a darker engine hidden behind palace curtains. Gojong routed lush revenue streams—ginseng harvests and coastal fishing dues—away from public ministry books into his private household purse, the Naejangwon, steered by relentless treasurer Yi Yong-ik. Provincial offices went up for discreet auction to palace favorites, who squeezed tenant farmers twice as hard to recoup their bribes. Worse, private brokers holding royal mint licenses flooded alleys with debased copper-nickel coins called baekdonghwa. Counterfeits and thin blanks wrecked monetary trust, doubling grain prices in Seoul markets while small shopkeepers stared at sacks of worthless metal. Reform and rot ran through the exact same hallways.

Russian troops and lumber crews crowded Korea's northern frontier in 1903 without seeking Gojong's consent. Under earlier logging leases around Yongampo on the Yalu River, they threw up fortified camps disguised as timber depots. Tokyo's generals watched every floating log raft as an intolerable threat across the strait. Gojong proclaimed strict neutrality as war approached over Manchuria, hoping European diplomats would shield his peninsula like an Asian Belgium. Nobody answered. Tokyo loaded horses and field batteries onto troop transports at Sasebo.

War tore past Korean neutrality in February 1904, when Japanese warships attacked Russian cruisers off Incheon and marched infantry onto Korean soil. Pushing Czarist troops back across Manchuria, Tokyo quickly coerced protocols seating Japanese advisers inside Seoul ministries. Army engineers seized prime rail lines running north from Busan toward the Yalu. Financial director Megata Tanetaro then stepped ashore with a cold ledger fix for Seoul's monetary chaos. Under his July 1905 overhaul, Japanese tellers called in Koreans' debased baekdonghwa at steep discounts—paying half their nominal value for pristine coins, a fifth for worn blanks, and zero for cruder strikes. Tokyo simultaneously declared notes from Japan's Dai-Ichi Bank sole legal tender, wiping out local cloth guilds' savings in weeks.

Gojong wagered his crown on parchment signed twenty-three years earlier. Article One of the 1882 treaty between Washington and Seoul pledged American good offices if another power treated Korea unjustly. As Japanese barracks ringed his capital that autumn, the emperor slipped urgent pleas into the coat pocket of trusted adviser Homer Hulbert, dispatching him across the Pacific to appeal directly to President Theodore Roosevelt. What Gojong hadn't learned sat buried in confidential Washington cables. Back in July 1905, Secretary of War William Howard Taft had met Japanese Prime Minister Katsura Taro in Tokyo, trading American silence over Korea for imperial acceptance of U.S. rule in the Philippines. September's Portsmouth Peace Conference then handed Japan effective dominance across the peninsula. The referee Gojong trusted had already left the ring.

Warm gas lamps lit the red-brick chamber where the trap closed indoors. On November 17, 1905, Japanese special envoy Ito Hirobumi rode to Deoksugung backed by infantry battalions and artillery rolling past stone walls. Cabinet ministers huddled inside Jungmyeongjeon, the two-story library where Gojong often entertained overseas guests. Ito demanded immediate signatures placing Korea's entire diplomatic service under Tokyo's authority. Prime Minister Han Gyu-seol refused flatly, weeping aloud until Japanese gendarmes dragged him kicking into an adjacent parlor and shut the door. Facing bayonets under heavy guard, five cabinet signers led by Education Minister Yi Wan-yong endorsed the Eulsa Protectorate Treaty. Gojong refused to press his personal seal onto the document. Tokyo didn't care, wiring foreign capitals by dawn that Korea's independent voice was finished.

Three mornings later, outrage broke past military checkpoints onto Seoul streets. Editor Jang Ji-yeon defied army censors at the Hwangseong Sinmun newspaper by printing a scorching front-page editorial titled Wailing Loudly on This Day. Extra news bundles flew through downtown alleys before police shut his presses and hauled Jang to jail. Days later, senior courtier Min Young-hwan dressed in formal silk robes and penned parting appeals begging foreign legations to expose Japan's coercion before ending his life with a pocket blade. Across Western capitals, Korean embassies packed their trunks and lowered their flags.

Stripped of diplomats and watched in his courtyard, Gojong gambled once more in spring 1907. Word reached Seoul that world powers would convene that June in the Netherlands for the Second Hague Peace Conference. Working past Japanese sentries around Deoksugung, the trapped sovereign pressed red wax onto secret credentials naming three trusted envoys: former prosecutor Yi Jun, scholar Yi Sang-seol, and linguist Yi Wi-jong, barely twenty-three. Riding the Trans-Siberian railway west while Hulbert raced separately from America, the delegates brought one blunt brief to Europe. They'd come to prove the 1905 protectorate document carried zero legal force.

Conference halls stayed bolted when the Koreans reached Holland that June. Dutch hosts and Western delegates denied them official seats, ruling that Tokyo alone spoke for Seoul under diplomatic rules. Refusing to vanish silently, Yi Wi-jong stepped before an international journalists' forum on July 9 and delivered a stinging French address titled A Plea for Korea. Reporters applauded his eloquence and printed sympathetic columns across European dailies, changing zero government votes. Struck down by a sudden facial infection and fever at the De Jong Hotel, prosecutor Yi Jun died far from home on July 14 while powers debated naval rules down the avenue.

News of the Hague defiance snapped Ito Hirobumi's patience. When leaks confirmed Gojong had dispatched the mission, furious Seoul crowds torched a pro-Japanese newspaper press and besieged Yi Wan-yong's residence before armed patrols sealed city gates. Ito marched back into Deoksugung on July 19, 1907, compelling Gojong to step down after more than four decades on the throne. Crown Prince Cheok—taking the title Emperor Sunjong at thirty-three—ascended beneath bayonets and shifted two miles east into Changdeokgung Palace so father and son couldn't coordinate from one courtyard.

Five days later, Ito hammered the cowed cabinet with the July 24 Jeongmi Treaty. Under seven stark articles, Yi Wan-yong handed Tokyo's appointees veto power over Korean laws and senior posts while seating Japanese vice-ministers behind every department desk. Outside judges entered local courts, and protectorate officers took command of the police, turning Korea's civilian administration into a hollow shell. That same night, a secret companion clause ordered the dissolution of Korea's remaining regular army. By late July, nobody in Seoul doubted who held the whip.

Imperial garrisons marched unarmed onto the Hunryeonwon drill field near Dongdaemun on the morning of August 1, 1907, for formal disbandment. Inside barracks beside the square, battalion commander Park Seung-hwan read the dissolution decree aloud before stepping behind closed doors. Dipping his brush, the major wrote stark parting words: a soldier unable to shield his country and a subject who'd failed loyalty held no regret in dying ten thousand times. One pistol shot cracked under the wooden rafters. Hearing their commander's death, enraged guards smashed open ammunition crates and seized rifles, battling Japanese machine-gun crews near Namdaemun Gate for four bloody hours until bullets gave out.

Escaping survivors carried stolen rifles past city walls into steep mountain counties. Disbanded army sergeants joined Confucian village scholars and rural tiger hunters to wage guerrilla war as the Righteous Armies. By January 1908, commander Yi In-yeong had united regional fighters into a Thirteen-Province coalition and pushed an advance column within miles of Dongdaemun before modern Japanese firepower threw his assault back. Scattered bands kept tearing up train rails and severing telegraph wires through rugged inland ridges. When Tokyo's divisions launched the brutal autumn 1909 Great Southern Punitive Operation across Jeolla, troops cordoned steep valleys and burned sympathetic villages before snow hit. By year's end, imperial tallies logged nearly seventeen thousand Korean fighters killed, while cooperative Seoul aristocrats collected Japanese peerage titles.

Rural defiance couldn't stop Tokyo's timetable after October 26, 1909. When Korean independence fighter An Jung-geun shot former Resident-General Ito Hirobumi dead on the Harbin railway platform in Manchuria, army hawks swept aside cautious protectorate paperwork. That gunshot ended patience in Tokyo. Generals dispatched War Minister Terauchi Masatake to Seoul with orders for outright annexation.

Behind Changdeokgung's pine walls, Sunjong inhabited a velvet trap as his empire dissolved outside. Japanese architects remodeled Huijeongdang and Daejojeon Halls with French carpets and glass chandeliers, staging a serene royal showcase even while stripping away palace guards and independent treasury funds. Weakened since the 1898 coffee plot and fenced in by Yi Wan-yong's cabinet, he stamped Tokyo's routine decrees. Silence closed in. Only muffled clock chimes answered across empty courtyards, two miles east of his deposed father's guarded quarters.

By August 1910, even the pretense of a separate throne lost purpose in Tokyo. Terauchi ringed Changdeokgung with armed gendarmes on August 22 as Yi Wan-yong steered the Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty across the council table. Sunjong refused to brush his personal signature onto the sheet, yet Japanese officials pressed the state seal into the parchment anyway. Posted across Seoul on August 29, 1910, the decree erased the thirteen-year Korean Empire and reduced Sunjong to the colonial rank of King Yi of Changdeokgung. That morning, fresh bilingual annexation notices slapped wet onto timber city gates while two deposed emperors sat guarded behind separate palace walls.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The Unresolved Debate

Historians argue fiercely over why the Korean Empire fell so quickly in 1910. Institutional critics fault Gojong's palace culture first. They note how the throne crushed the 1898 Independence Club, auctioned provincial posts, and let private mints wreck urban credit years before Japanese annexation. Without parliamentary checks or transparent budgets, those scholars argue, an autocratic court couldn't rally ordinary taxpayers to sacrifice for royal survival. Revisionist historian Yi Tae-jin pushes hard the other way. Studying streetcar lines, modern property deeds, and telegraph grids laid down under the Gwangmu banner, Yi contends Gojong led genuine state modernization that alarmed Tokyo precisely because it was starting to pay off. Between internal corruption and foreign coercion, both forces fed directly on each other. No neat verdict clears either side.

2. The Road Not Taken

One critical hinge arrived during November 1898, when Gojong chose street-peddler guilds over partnership with the Independence Club. Suppose the emperor had shared real budget oversight with that proposed advisory assembly instead of shutting down street rallies. Public bidding on mines and tariffs might have curbed Naejangwon favoritism and stopped debased nickel coin printing before 1904. Could a constitutional monarchy have turned away Japanese warships off Incheon in 1905? Not by rifles alone. Yet a unified cabinet backed by organized citizens wouldn't have folded inside Jungmyeongjeon without raising far louder international alarm abroad. History turned on a bolted palace gate.

3. How It Shapes Daily Life Today

Central Seoul displays Gojong's vanished empire in plain view on any workday. South Korea's official constitutional name, Daehan Minguk, preserves the Great Han designation proclaimed at Hwangudan in 1897, deliberately setting older Joseon dynastic labels aside. Commuters stepping off subway exits near City Hall walk past Hwangudan's surviving octagonal stone pavilion wedged behind tall glass hotel towers. Even main surface transit avenues linking Seodaemun to Dongdaemun along Jongno follow the 1899 streetcar rails spiked into packed dirt under royal charters. Modern Seoul commutes directly across that short-lived blueprint. Every daily ride crosses imperial ground.

4. Essential Books, Films & Places to Go Deeper

For eyewitness urgency written while Seoul shook, start with American adviser Homer B. Hulbert's vivid chronicle, The Passing of Korea. British reporter Fred A. McKenzie brings equal heat from the countryside; his fierce dispatch, The Tragedy of Korea, follows rural fighters into burned mountain valleys after 1907. On print culture and shifting sovereignty, Andre Schmid's Korea Between Empires dissects how Seoul editors debated nationhood. Michael E. Robinson covers the wider colonial arc in his brisk survey, Korea's Twentieth-Century Odyssey. On screen, director Hur Jin-ho's historical feature The Last Princess follows the displaced royal family after annexation. In person, tour red-brick Jungmyeongjeon behind Deoksugung's stone wall, or visit the Yi Jun Peace Museum in The Hague. That's where paper treaties feel painfully real.
