Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

Sailing to Byzantium

W. B. Yeats (1927) • 4 min read

An aging poet leaves behind the sensual country of youth and biological decay to voyage toward the golden mosaics and timeless artifice of ancient Byzantium.

Key Context & Why It Matters

W. B. Yeats wrote this poem in 1926, when he was sixty-one. He was an Irish Nobel Prize winner and a senator of the new Irish Free State, and he was thinking hard about growing old. It became the opening poem of his 1928 book The Tower, and it is often named among the greatest poems of the twentieth century. Yeats had a particular fascination with Byzantium, the old Christian empire centered on what is now Istanbul. He had seen its glittering gold mosaics in Ravenna, in Italy, and in Sicily. To him it was a place where art, religion and daily life were once joined, and where artists worked for something beyond themselves. In the poem, an aging man turns away from a country of youth and desire and sails to Byzantium, hoping to be remade into a work of art that cannot die. The meaning lies in that trade, and in the poem's honest doubt about whether it can really be made.

That is no country for old men. The young

In one another’s arms, birds in the trees,

—Those dying generations—at their song,

The salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas,

Fish flesh or fowl, commend all summer long

Whatever is begotten born and dies.

Caught in that sensual music all neglect

Monuments of unaging intellect.

An aged man is but a paltry thing,

A tattered coat upon a stick, unless

Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing

For every tatter in its mortal dress,

Nor is there singing school but studying

Monuments of its own magnificence;

And therefore I have sailed the seas and come

To the holy city of Byzantium.

O sages standing in God’s holy fire

As in the gold mosaic of a wall,

Come from the holy fire, perne in a gyre,

And be the singing masters of my soul.

Consume my heart away; sick with desire

And fastened to a dying animal

It knows not what it is; and gather me

Into the artifice of eternity.

Once out of nature I shall never take

My bodily form from any natural thing,

But such a form as Grecian goldsmiths make

Of hammered gold and gold enamelling

To keep a drowsy emperor awake;

Or set upon a golden bough to sing

To lords and ladies of Byzantium

Of what is past, or passing, or to come.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a voyage in four stanzas, and each stanza is a stage in a decision. The first says what the speaker is leaving, the second says why, the third makes a prayer, and the fourth imagines what he will become.

It begins with a famous sentence: "That is no country for old men." The country is not named, but we understand it is Ireland, or really any place ruled by youth and nature. It is a world of "the young in one another's arms," of birds in the trees, of "the salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas." Everything is making more life. Yeats calls the birds "those dying generations," because every singer in this paradise is on its way to death. That is the trap. The speaker says that, "caught in that sensual music," everyone forgets the "monuments of unaging intellect," the great works of thought and art that do not wither. A world drunk on the body has no time for such things, and an old man, whose body is failing, has no place in it.

The second stanza makes the speaker's predicament plain. "An aged man is but a paltry thing, / A tattered coat upon a stick." The image is of a scarecrow, a body reduced to rags. But there is a way out, he says: "unless / Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing / For every tatter in its mortal dress." The more the body falls apart, the louder the soul must sing. The trouble is that there is no school for that kind of singing except "studying monuments of its own magnificence." The soul learns to sing by looking at the great works the soul itself has produced. So he has "sailed the seas and come / To the holy city of Byzantium."

In the third stanza he addresses the sages he sees in a gold mosaic, "standing in God's holy fire / As in the gold mosaic of a wall." He asks them to leave the wall, to "perne in a gyre," which means to spin down in a spiral, like thread unwinding from a spool, and become the teachers of his soul. And then comes the most painful and honest part of the poem. He asks them to "consume my heart away; sick with desire / And fastened to a dying animal / It knows not what it is." The heart is sick with desire, and the body it is stuck to is a dying animal. He wants to be freed of both, and "gathered into the artifice of eternity." Artifice means something made by craft, and he sees no shame in that.

In the last stanza, he imagines the result. Out of nature, he will take no bodily form from any natural thing, but will become a golden bird, hammered by goldsmiths "to keep a drowsy emperor awake," singing to lords and ladies "of what is past, or passing, or to come."

A first reader may miss how oddly this wish ends. The ideal self is a mechanical bird serving an emperor. Is that eternity, or a gilded cage? The bird still sings of "what is passing," the very mortal world he has fled.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

It is hard to find a short poem in English more tightly built than this one, and it is worth saying exactly how. It is written in four stanzas of eight lines, in a rhyme scheme called ottava rima, borrowed from Italian poetry, which runs ababab and ends with a couplet. The couplet at the end of each stanza lands like a closing gate. Yeats takes this formal, stately pattern, associated with grand themes, and gives it a speaking voice. The result is a poem that sounds both ceremonial and urgent.

The first line is one of the great openings in modern poetry. It begins in the middle of a thought, with a flat, final pronouncement, and that confidence draws the reader in at once. The sound of the first stanza is also brilliant. The crowded, tumbling phrases, "Fish flesh or fowl," "begotten born and dies," enact the thick, busy, sensual life the speaker is rejecting. Then the poem slows into the austere gold of Byzantium. The shift in texture, from a loud, jumbled natural world to a hard, bright, ordered one, is the poem's main trick, and it is done with real mastery.

The central images, the scarecrow, the mosaic sages, the golden bird, are simple enough to hold in the mind and rich enough to bear a lifetime of reading. The phrase "artifice of eternity" is a fine example of something Yeats does very well, which is to yoke two words that seem to fight each other. Artifice suggests fakery. Eternity suggests truth. Together they capture the poem's whole argument.

As for weaknesses, they are real but small. Some readers find the second stanza a little abstract, with its talk of soul and singing schools and monuments, compared with the vivid first and fourth. The phrase "perne in a gyre" is deliberately odd, drawn from Yeats's private system of symbols about history moving in spirals, and a reader with no background can find it obscure. The poem asks for a certain willingness to take on trust that spirals are meaningful.

There is also a question about sincerity. A reader may ask whether the speaker truly wants to be a golden bird, or whether the poem is a kind of aesthetic fantasy, a rich man's longing for a tidy immortality. The final image is so beautiful that it almost conceals how strange it is. A less charitable reading holds that the poem escapes the pain of aging through style alone, rather than facing it. Others reply that this is the point, and that the poem's tension is deliberate.

Finally, the final stanza is a little cooler than the others. After the heat of the third stanza's cry, "consume my heart away," the closing image is polished and distant. That might be the correct effect, since the speaker is becoming metal, but it also means the poem ends in admiration rather than emotion. On balance, though, these are quibbles. As a piece of controlled, musical, intellectually serious verse, it is close to flawless.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's influence has been wide and, in one famous way, almost accidental. Its first line has become one of the most borrowed in English. The novelist Cormac McCarthy took it for the title of his 2005 novel No Country for Old Men, and the Coen brothers' 2007 film version won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Millions of people who have never read a line of Yeats now know those words, though the film and novel are about a violent, changing world rather than about aging as such. The phrase, in all its uses, now stands for a world that has grown too hard for the old ways.

Within Yeats's career, the poem was a turning point. By 1926 he had changed from the dreamy writer of the 1890s into a harder, more public, more modern poet. The Tower, the 1928 collection it opens, is generally counted among his finest, and many critics consider the late Yeats of The Tower and The Winding Stair the peak of his work. Sailing to Byzantium announces the book's chief theme, the struggle of an old man to keep his imagination alive as his body fails. Yeats followed it with a companion piece, Byzantium, written in 1930, which treats the same city in a darker, more tormented way.

The poem's influence on other poets has been significant. It helped establish a model for the great modernist poem that handles personal feeling through symbol, rather than through plain confession. Later poets have written their own answers, poems that turn on aging and art, and the vocabulary of the poem, golden birds, gyres, and the artifice of eternity, has become a shared language for talking about art versus mortality.

It has had a long afterlife in education, too. It is a standard text in school and university courses across the English-speaking world, and it is often one of the first poems students meet when they are introduced to the difficulty of modernist verse. Examination boards and anthologies have kept it in print for generations, and it features in countless essays on art and time.

Beyond the classroom, it has become one of those poems quoted at retirements, at funerals, and in books on growing old. Writers on aging often turn to it, and some are drawn to its stern advice that an old person should sing louder rather than fade. In popular culture, apart from McCarthy's title, there are references in music, film, and fiction.

Finally, the poem helped fix Byzantium in the Western literary imagination as a symbol of art that is eternal, holy, and a little cold. Visitors to Ravenna and Istanbul sometimes come with the poem in mind, which is a rare thing for a poem to do to a city.

IV. What Readers Loved

The deepest thing readers love about this poem is that it gives dignity to growing old without sentimentality. Many writers treat age as a topic for pity or for cheap comfort. Yeats treats it as a crisis with a task attached. The old man is not asked to be wise and serene. He is asked to sing louder, with more passion, precisely because his body is falling away. People who face illness, aging, or the decline of their powers often find that bracing. It is an unusually brave statement of what an old person might still do.

Readers also love the poem for its sound. Even someone who knows nothing of Byzantium or gyres can hear that the poem is beautiful. The measured, stately lines carry real music, and the golden images, the mosaic wall, the hammered gold, the golden bough, have a gleaming, tactile quality. Many people say the poem is one of the few they have memorized voluntarily, and some can recite the first stanza on demand.

Another source of love is the poem's central, shining idea: that art can be a kind of immortality. Writers, painters, musicians, and anyone who makes things feel the force of that idea. The hope that something we make might outlast us is a deep one, and this poem has given it its most gorgeous expression. For makers of every kind, it is a kind of patron poem.

Critics and fellow poets have been generous. The late critic Harold Bloom, for instance, was devoted to Yeats and ranked him among the very greatest poets of the century, and many others have called this poem one of the supreme achievements of modern English verse. The poem is regularly placed on lists of the best poems of the twentieth century, and it is often chosen by poets themselves when asked to name a favorite.

There is also affection for the poem's honesty about desire. The line "sick with desire / And fastened to a dying animal" is one of the plainest, most startling admissions in the poet's work. It confesses that the speaker's yearning has not faded with age, that he is still tormented by wanting, and that he wants relief from his own heart. Readers feel the rawness of that. It makes the grandeur of the poem human.

Finally, readers love that it rewards patience. It can be enjoyed in a first reading as a beautiful voyage, and then returned to for years, each time revealing a new layer in the symbols and the doubt. Few poems offer both an immediate pleasure and a lifelong study.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even this much-praised poem has attracted serious objections, and they fall into a few groups.

The first is about the poem's attitude to life. Some critics have argued that it expresses a kind of disdain for the body and for ordinary experience, preferring a cold, aristocratic perfection to the warmth of living. The poem calls the natural world's creatures "those dying generations," and wants to be taken out of nature altogether. A reader who values the messy, mortal, physical side of life might feel that Yeats is rejecting the very thing that makes poetry worth writing. Critics who read the poem as a flight from life say that it trades a living heart for a golden toy.

A more subtle form of this objection points out that the poem seems to undercut itself. The golden bird sings of "what is past, or passing, or to come," which means it is still singing about time, the mortal thing. Some readers think this is Yeats's great irony: that he cannot imagine art without the life it comes from. Others think it is a flaw, a sign that the poem has not quite resolved what it wants. Whether this is a weakness or a strength is the major debate about the poem, and honest readers disagree.

Another line of criticism concerns the politics and the personality behind it. Yeats in the 1920s was a conservative, aristocratic figure who admired order, hierarchy, and old families, and who later flirted for a time with authoritarian ideas. Critics such as George Orwell, in an essay on Yeats, and later the Irish writer Conor Cruise O'Brien, were wary of the aristocratic and anti-democratic tendencies in his thought. The poem's dream of a golden court, with an emperor and lords and ladies, can sound to such readers like a fantasy of rank. This criticism is usually aimed at Yeats's work as a whole, but the poem's final stanza gives it a convenient target.

A different kind of complaint concerns difficulty. The poem draws on Yeats's personal mythology, his book A Vision, with its theories of historical cycles, and a reader who tries to decode every symbol can feel lost. Some critics think the poem leans too heavily on private system-building, though most agree that the poem works well enough without it.

Finally, a few readers find the poem too polished. Its beauty is so controlled that it can feel distant, and the speaker's pain, though stated, is expressed in a form so refined that it never quite hurts. That is a matter of taste, and a great many readers find just that refinement moving.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This poem belongs to high modernism, the early twentieth-century movement that sought new forms to deal with a fractured modern world. Yeats is a slightly unusual modernist. Unlike his friend and sometime collaborator Ezra Pound, or the younger T. S. Eliot, he did not abandon rhyme and regular meter. He kept traditional forms and charged them with new, harder meaning. In this respect, Sailing to Byzantium sits between the old lyric tradition and the modern age.

It descends from several ancestors. The first is the Romantic poets, especially Keats, whose Ode to a Nightingale also sets the fleeting life of the body against the permanence of song. Yeats's golden bird is a deliberate answer to Keats's nightingale, which is a living bird, whereas his is a made one. The second is the long tradition of poems about the soul's journey, from Dante's voyage through the afterlife to the quests of medieval romance. The third is the ancient idea, found in Greek and Roman writers, that poetry can outlast bronze and stone.

Looking forward, the poem influenced generations of poets who wrote about aging and art. Writers as various as Seamus Heaney, who admired Yeats deeply, and many American poets turned to its example. Wallace Stevens's late poems about aging and imagination share its concern, though Stevens works in a cooler, more abstract mode.

It also pairs beautifully with Yeats's own later poem Byzantium, which revisits the city and treats it far more darkly, with flames and spirits rather than calm gold. Reading the two together shows a mind arguing with itself. Another useful companion is Eliot's Journey of the Magi or Four Quartets, which explore time and spiritual search in a more fragmented style. Set against Eliot, Yeats seems more ceremonial and more confident.

Why does it still matter? First, because the problem it poses is permanent. All of us will grow old, and all of us wonder what we can leave behind or become. Second, because it models a way of facing mortality that neither denies it nor surrenders, one that says, in effect, make something of yourself while you can. Third, because it is a rare poem that is both profound and beautiful, a thing you can read once for the music and many times for the meaning.

And it carries the last, slightly uncanny honesty of the whole poem: that even in the golden city, the singer cannot stop singing about time. The great irony, and the great comfort, is that the bird of eternity is still preoccupied with what passes.
