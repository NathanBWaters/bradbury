Sailing to Byzantium

W. B. Yeats (1927) • 4 min read

An aging poet leaves behind the sensual country of youth and biological decay to voyage toward the golden mosaics and timeless artifice of ancient Byzantium.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in the autumn of 1926 when Yeats was sixty-one—now a Nobel Laureate and Senator of the Irish Free State, inspired by the sixth-century Byzantine mosaics he had admired in Ravenna and Sicily—and published as the opening poem of his landmark 1928 collection The Tower. Belonging to the High Modernism tradition and categorized under Modernist & Imagist, 'Sailing to Byzantium' (1927) unfolds across 32 verbatim lines in 4 stanzas. Universally acclaimed as one of the supreme masterpieces of twentieth-century poetry, 'Sailing to Byzantium' gave Cormac McCarthy the title of No Country for Old Men and established Byzantium as Modernism’s ultimate emblem of transcendent aesthetic form. Through four magisterial eight-line ottava rima stanzas (ABABABCC) in iambic pentameter, W. B. Yeats fuses formal precision with emotional immediacy, making the poem a cornerstone of the Bradbury 1000 nightly curriculum.

That is no country for old men. The young

In one another’s arms, birds in the trees,

—Those dying generations—at their song,

The salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas,

Fish flesh or fowl, commend all summer long

Whatever is begotten born and dies.

Caught in that sensual music all neglect

Monuments of unaging intellect.

An aged man is but a paltry thing,

A tattered coat upon a stick, unless

Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing

For every tatter in its mortal dress,

Nor is there singing school but studying

Monuments of its own magnificence;

And therefore I have sailed the seas and come

To the holy city of Byzantium.

O sages standing in God’s holy fire

As in the gold mosaic of a wall,

Come from the holy fire, perne in a gyre,

And be the singing masters of my soul.

Consume my heart away; sick with desire

And fastened to a dying animal

It knows not what it is; and gather me

Into the artifice of eternity.

Once out of nature I shall never take

My bodily form from any natural thing,

But such a form as Grecian goldsmiths make

Of hammered gold and gold enamelling

To keep a drowsy emperor awake;

Or set upon a golden bough to sing

To lords and ladies of Byzantium

Of what is past, or passing, or to come.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in the autumn of 1926 when Yeats was sixty-one—now a Nobel Laureate and Senator of the Irish Free State, inspired by the sixth-century Byzantine mosaics he had admired in Ravenna and Sicily—and published as the opening poem of his landmark 1928 collection The Tower. Within the broader trajectory of High Modernism, W. B. Yeats composed 'Sailing to Byzantium' (1927) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"That is no country for old men. The young"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 32 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Sailing to Byzantium' lies the conflict between biological transience ('The salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas,' where every dying generation is 'caught in that sensual music') and the quest for spiritual and artistic permanence, as the aging speaker—'a tattered coat upon a stick' fastened to a dying animal—invokes the sages in God’s holy fire to gather him into 'the artifice of eternity'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"O sages standing in God’s holy fire"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"Of what is past, or passing, or to come."—the central metaphor has transformed the.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'Sailing to Byzantium' using four magisterial eight-line ottava rima stanzas (ABABABCC) in iambic pentameter, where Yeats infuses Renaissance aristocratic stanza form with bold slant rhymes ('wall / soul,' 'sing / studying'), colloquial bluntness ('That is no country for old men'), and the glittering symbol of the hammered gold bird singing of 'what is past, or passing, or to come'. Spanning 32 lines across 4 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "That is no country for old men. The young" and "Of what is past, or passing, or to come." to reverberate in the ear.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Universally acclaimed as one of the supreme masterpieces of twentieth-century poetry, 'Sailing to Byzantium' gave Cormac McCarthy the title of No Country for Old Men and established Byzantium as Modernism’s ultimate emblem of transcendent aesthetic form. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Sailing to Byzantium' has remained a touchstone of the Modernist & Imagist canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
