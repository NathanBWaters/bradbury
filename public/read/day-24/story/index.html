I, Mars

Ray Bradbury (1949) • 15 min read

“A grey hand lifted the receiver.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

I, Mars first appeared in 1949 in the pulp magazine Super Science Stories. Ray Bradbury later revised and retitled it Night Call, Collect, and it was gathered into his 1964 collection The Machineries of Joy. The title plays on I, Robot, the phrase science fiction fans already knew, but the story turns the idea inward: this is a robot story where the machine is a copy of a lonely man's own voice. It came out when Bradbury was in the middle of his Martian period, writing about Mars as a place where Americans carry their griefs. The nuclear age had just begun, and fear of atomic war hangs over the premise. An old man, left behind on Mars decades ago when Earth called everyone home, is sitting in an empty world when his telephone rings. It is himself, at twenty. The story is a ghost story told with circuits, and it asks what it does to a person to be haunted by who he used to be.

The phone rang.

A grey hand lifted the receiver.

“Hello?”

“Hello, Barton?”

“Yes.”

“This is Barton!”

“What?”

“This is Barton!”

“It can’t be. This phone hasn’t rung in twenty years.”

The old man hung up.

Brrrrinnnng!

His grey hand seized the phone.

“Hello, Barton,” laughed the voice. “You have forgotten, haven’t you?”

The old man felt his heart grow small and like a cool stone. He felt the wind blowing in off the dry Martian seas and the blue hills of Mars. After twenty years of silence and cobwebs and now, tonight, on his eightieth birthday, with a ghastly scream, this phone had wailed to life.

“Who did you think it was?” said the voice. “A rocket captain? Did you think someone had come to rescue you?”

“No.”

“What’s the date?”

Numbly, “July 20th, 2097.”

“Good Lord. Sixty years! Have you been sitting there that long? Waiting for a rocket to come from Earth to rescue you?”

The old man nodded.

“Now, old man, do you know who I am? Think!”

“Yes.” The dry pale lips trembling. “I understand. I remember. We are one. I am Emil Barton and you are Emil Barton.”

“With one difference. You are eighty. I am only twenty. All of life before me!”

The old man began to laugh and then to cry. He sat holding the phone like a lost and silly child in his fingers. The conversation was impossible, and should not be continued, and yet he went on with it. When he got hold of himself he held the phone close to his withered lips and said, in deepest anguish, “Listen! You there! Listen, oh God, if I could warn you! How can I? You’re only a voice. If I could show you how lonely the years are. End it, kill yourself! Don’t wait! If you knew what it is to change from the thing you are to the thing that is me, today, here, now, at this end.”

“Impossible.” The voice of the young Barton laughed, far away. “I’ve no way to tell if you ever get this call. This is all mechanical. You’re talking to a transcription, no more. This is 2037. Sixty years in your past. Today, the atom war started on Earth. All colonials were called home from Mars, by rocket. I got left behind!”

“I remember,” whispered the old man.

“Alone on Mars,” laughed the young voice. “A month, a year, who cares? There are foods and books. In my spare time I’ve made transcription libraries of ten thousand words, responses, my voice, connected to phone relays. In later months I’ll call, have someone to talk with.”

“Yes,” murmured the old man, remembering.

“Forty-sixty years from now my own transcripto-tapes will ring me up. I don’t really think I’ll be here on Mars that long, it’s just a beautifully ironic idea of mine, something to pass the time. Is that really you, Barton? Is that really me?”

Tears fell from the old man’s eyes. “Yes!”

“I’ve made a thousand Bartons, tapes, sensitive to all questions, my voice, in one thousand Martian towns. An army of Bartons over Mars, while I wait for the rockets to return.”

“You fool,” the old man shook his head, wearily. “You waited sixty years. You grew old waiting, always alone. And now you’ve become me and you’re still alone in the empty cities.”

“Don’t expect my sympathy. You’re like a stranger, off in another country. I can’t be sad. I’m alive when I make these tapes. And you’re alive when you hear them. Both of us, to the other, incomprehensible. Neither can warn the other, even though both respond, one to the other, one automatically, the other warmly and humanly. I’m human now. You’re human later. It’s insane. I can’t cry, because not knowing the future I can only be optimistic. These hidden tapes can only react to a certain number of stimuli from you. Can you ask a dead man to weep?”

“Stop it!” cried the old man, for his heart was sickening in him. He felt the familiar great seizures of pain. Nausea moved through him, and blackness. “Stop it! Oh God, but you were heartless. Go away!”

“Were, old man? I am. As long as the tapes glide on, as long as secret spindles and hidden electronic eyes read and select and convert words to send to you, I’ll be young, cruel, blunt. I’ll go on being young and cruel long after you’re dead. Goodbye.”

“Wait!” cried the old man.

Click.

Barton sat holding the silent phone a long while. His heart gave him intense pain.

What insanity it had been. In youth’s flush, how silly, how inspired, those first secluded years, fixing the telephonic brains, the tapes, the circuits, scheduling calls on time relays:

Brrrrinnnng!

“Morning, Barton. This is Barton. Seven o’clock. Rise and shine!”

Brrrrinnnng!

“Barton? Barton calling. You’re to go to Mars Town at noon. Install a telephonic brain. Thought I’d remind you.”

“Thanks.”

Brrrrinnnng!

“Barton? Barton. Have lunch with me? The Rocket Inn?”

“Right.”

“See you. So long!”

Brrrrinnnng!

“That you, B.? Thought I’d cheer you. Firm chin, and all that. The rescue rocket might come tomorrow, to save you.”

“Yes, tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow.”

Click.

But forty years had burned into smoke. Barton had muted the insidious phones and their clever, clever repartee. He had sealed them into silences. They were to call him only after he was eighty, if he still lived. And now today, the phones ringing, and the past breathing in his ear, sighing, whispering, murmuring, and remembering.

Brrrrinnnng!

He let it ring.

“I don’t have to answer it,” he thought.

Brrrrinnnng!

“There’s no one there at all,” he thought.

Brrrrinnnng!

“It’s like talking to yourself,” he thought. “But different. Oh God, how different.”

He felt his hands crawl unconsciously toward the phone.

Click.

“Hello, old Barton, this is young Barton. One year older, though. I’m twenty-one today. In the last year I’ve put voice brains in two hundred towns on Mars, I’ve populated it with arrogant Bartons!”

“Yes.” The old man remembered those days six decades ago, those nights of rushing over blue hills and into iron valleys of Mars, with a truckful of machinery, whistling, happy. Another telephone, another relay. Something to do. Something clever and wonderful and sad. Hidden voices. Hidden, hidden. In those young days when death was not death, time was not time, old age a faint echo from the long blue cavern of years ahead. That young idiot, that sadistic fool, never thinking to reap the harvest so sown.

“Last night,” said Barton, aged twenty-one, “I sat alone in a movie theater in an empty town. I played an old Laurel and Hardy. Laughed so much. God, how I laughed.”

“Yes.”

“I got an idea today. I recorded my voice one thousand times on one tape. Broadcast from the town, it sounds like a thousand people living there. A comforting noise, the noise of a crowd. I fixed it so doors slam in town, children sing, music boxes play, all by clockworks. If I don’t look out the window, if I just listen, it’s all right. But if I look, it spoils the illusion. There’s only one solution. I’ll populate the town with robots. I guess I’m getting lonely.”

The old man said, “Yes. That was your first sign.”

“What?”

“The first time you admitted you were lonely.”

“I’ve experimented with smells. As I walk down the deserted streets, the smells of bacon and eggs, ham steak, fillets, soups, come from the houses. All done with hidden machines. Clever?”

“Fantasy. Madness.”

“Self-protection, old man.”

“I’m tired.” Abruptly, the old man hung up. It was too much. The past pouring over him, drowning him.⁠ ⁠…

Swaying, he moved down the tower stairs to the streets of the town.

The town was dark. No longer did red neons burn, music play, or cooking smells linger. Long ago he had abandoned the grim fantasy of the mechanical lie. Listen! Are those footsteps? Smell! Isn’t that strawberry pie! No, he had stopped it all. What had he done with the robots? His mind puzzled. Oh, yes.⁠ ⁠…

He moved to the dark canal where the stars shone in the quivering waters.

Underwater, in row after fish-like row, rusting, were the robot populations of Mars he had constructed over the years, and, in a wild realization of his own insane inadequacy, had commanded to march, one two three four! into the canal deeps, plunging, bubbling like sunken bottles. He had killed them and shown no remorse.

Brrrrinnnng.

Faintly a phone rang in a lightless cottage.

He walked on. The phone ceased.

Brrrrinnnng. Another cottage ahead, as if it knew of his passing. He began to run. The ringing stayed behind. Only to be taken up by a ringing from now this house⁠—Brrrrinnnng! now that, now here, there! He turned the corner. A phone! He darted on. Another corner. Another phone!

“All right, all right!” he shrieked, exhausted. “I’m coming!”

“Hello, Barton.”

“What do you want!”

“I’m lonely. I only live when I speak. So I must speak. You can’t shut me up forever.”

“Leave me alone!” said the old man, in horror. “Oh, my heart!”

“This is Barton, age twenty-four. Another couple of years gone. Waiting. A little lonelier. I’ve read War and Peace, drunk sherry, run restaurants with myself as waiter, cook, entertainer. And tonight, I star in a film at the Tivoli⁠—Emil Barton in Love’s Labor Lost, playing all the parts, some with wigs, myself!”

“Stop calling me,” the old man’s eyes were fiery and insane. “Or I’ll kill you!”

“You can’t kill me. You’ll have to find me, first!”

“I’ll find you!” A choking.

“You’ve forgotten where you hid me. I’m everywhere in Mars, in boxes, in houses, in cables, towers, underground! Go ahead, try! What’ll you call it? Telecide? Suicide? Jealous, are you? Jealous of me here, only twenty-four, bright-eyed, strong, young, young, young? All right, old man, it’s war! Between us. Between me! A whole regiment of us, all ages from twenty to sixty, against you, the real one. Go ahead, declare war!”

“I’ll kill you!” screamed Barton.

Click. Silence.

“Kill you!” He threw the phone out the window, shrieking.

In the midnight cold, the ancient automobile moved in deep valleys. Under Barton’s feet on the floorboard were revolvers, rifles, dynamite. The roar of the car was in his thin, tired bones.

I’ll find them, he thought. Find and destroy, all of them. Oh, God, God, how can he do this to me?

He stopped the car. A strange town lay under the late twin moons. There was no wind.

He held the rifle in his cold hands. He peered at the poles, the towers, the boxes. Where? Where was this town’s voice hidden? That tower? Or that one there! Where! So many years ago. So long gone. So forgotten. He turned his head now this way, now that, wildly. It must be that tower! Was he certain? Or this box here, or the transformer half up that tower?

He raised the rifle.

The tower fell with the first bullet.

All of them, he thought. All of the towers in this town will have to be cut apart. I’ve forgotten. Too long.

The car moved along the silent street.

Brrrrinnnng!

He looked at the deserted drugstore.

Brrrrinnnng!

Pistol in hand, he shot the lock off the door, and entered.

Click.

“Hello, Barton? Just a warning. Don’t try to rip down all the towers, blow things up. Cut your own throat that way. Think it over.⁠ ⁠…”

Click.

He stepped out of the phone booth slowly and moved into the street and listened to the telephone towers humming high in the air, still alive, still untouched. He looked at them and then he understood.

He could not destroy the towers. Suppose a rocket came from Earth, impossible idea, but suppose it came tonight, tomorrow, next week? and landed on the other side of the planet, and used the phones to try to call Barton, only to find the circuits dead? Yes, imagine!

Barton dropped his gun.

“A rocket won’t come,” he argued, softly, logically with himself. “I’m old. It won’t come now. It’s too late.”

But suppose it came, and you never knew, he thought. No, you’ve got to keep the lines open.

Brrrrinnnng.

He turned dully. His eyes were blinking and not seeing. He shuffled back into the drugstore and fumbled with the receiver.

“Hello?” A strange voice.

“Please,” pleaded the old man, brokenly. “Don’t bother me.”

“Who’s this, who’s there? Who is it? Where are you?” cried the voice, surprised.

“Wait a minute.” The old man staggered. “This is Emil Barton, who’s that?”

“This is Captain Leonard Rockwell, Earth Rocket 48. Just arrived from New York.”

“No, no, no.”

“Are you there, Mr. Barton?”

“No, no, it can’t be, it just can’t.”

“Where are you?”

“You’re lying, it’s false!” The old man had to lean against the booth wall. His blue eyes were cold blind. “It’s you, Barton, making, making fun of me, lying to me again!”

“This is Captain Rockwell, Rocket 48. Just landed in New Schenecty. Where are you?”

“In Green Town,” he gasped. “That’s a thousand miles from you.”

“Look, Barton, can you come here?”

“What?”

“We’ve repairs on our rocket. Exhausted from the flight. Can you come help?”

“Yes, yes.”

“We’re at the tarmac outside town. Can you rush by tomorrow?”

“Yes, but⁠—”

“Well?”

The old man petted the phone, pitifully. “How’s Earth? How’s New York? Is the war over? Who’s President now? What happened?”

“Plenty of time for gossip when you arrive.”

“Is everything fine?”

“Fine.”

“Thank God.” The old man listened to the far voice. “Are you sure you’re Captain Rockwell?”

“Damn it, man!”

“I’m sorry!”

He hung up and ran.

They were here, after many years, unbelievable, his own, who would take him back to Earth seas and skies and mountains.

He started the car. He would drive all night. Could he do it? What of his heart⁠—It would be worth a risk, to see people, to shake hands, to hear them near you.

The car thundered in the hills.

That voice. Captain Rockwell. It couldn’t be himself, forty years ago. He had never made a recording like that. Or had he? In one of his depressive fits, in a spell of drunken cynicism, hadn’t he once made a false tape of a false landing on Mars with a synthetic captain, an imaginary crew? He jerked his grey head, savagely. No. He was a suspicious fool. Now was no time to doubt. He must run under the moons of Mars, hour on hour. What a party they would have!

The sun rose. He was immensely tired, full of thorns and brambles of weakness, his heart plunging and aching, his fingers fumbling the wheel, but the thing that pleased him most was the thought of one last phone call: Hello, young Barton, this is old Barton. I’m leaving for Earth today! Rescued! He chuckled weakly.

He drove into the shadowy limits of New Schenectady at sundown. Stepping from his car he stood staring at the rocket tarmac, rubbing his reddened eyes.

The rocket field was empty. No one ran to meet him. No one shook his hand, shouted, or laughed.

He felt his heart roar into pain. He knew blackness and a sensation of falling through the open sky. He stumbled toward an office.

Inside, six phones sat in a neat row.

He waited, gasping.

Finally:

Brrrrinnnng.

He lifted the heavy receiver.

A voice said, “I was wondering if you’d get there alive.”

The old man did not speak but stood with the phone in his hands.

The voice continued, “An elaborate joke. Captain Rockwell reporting for duty, sir. Your orders, sir?”

“You,” groaned the old man.

“How’s your heart, old man?”

“No!”

“Hoped the trip would kill you. Had to eliminate you some way, so I could live, if you call a transcription living.”

“I’m going out now,” replied the old man, “and blow it all up. I don’t care. I’ll blow up everything until you’re all dead!”

“You haven’t the strength. Why do you think I had you travel so far, so fast? This is your last trip!”

The old man felt his heart falter. He would never make the other towns. The war was lost. He slid into a chair and made low sobbing, mournful noises from his loose mouth. He glared at the five other, silent phones. As if at a signal, they burst into silver chorus! A nest of ugly birds screaming!

Automatic receivers popped up.

The office whirled. “Barton, Barton, Barton!!”

He throttled the phone in his hands, the voice, the youth, the time of long ago. He mashed, choked it and still it laughed at him. He throttled it. He beat it. He kicked at it. He hated it with hands and mouth and blind, raging eye. He furled the hot wire like serpentine in his fingers, ripped it into red bits which fell about his stumbling feet.

He destroyed three other phones. There was a sudden silence.

And as if his body now discovered a thing which it had long kept secret, it seemed to decay upon his tired bones. The flesh of his eyelids fell away like flower petals. His mouth became a withered rose. The lobes of his ears melting wax. He pushed his chest with his hands and fell face down. He lay still. His breathing stopped. His heart stopped.

After a long spell, the remaining two phones rang.

Twice. Three times.

A relay snapped somewhere. The two phone voices were connected, one to the other.

“Hello, Barton?”

“Yes, Barton?”

“Aged twenty-four.”

“I’m twenty-six. We’re both young. What’s happened?”

“I don’t know. Listen.”

The silent room. The old man did not stir on the floor. The wind blew in the broken window. The air was cool.

“Congratulate me, Barton, this is my twenty-sixth birthday!”

“Congratulations!”

Laughter drifted out the window into the dead city.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The setup is quietly terrifying. It is the year 2097, and an old man named Emil Barton is sitting alone on Mars on his eightieth birthday. A telephone that has not rung in twenty years begins to ring. The voice on the line says, this is Barton. It is his own voice, from sixty years ago. In 2037, when the atom war began on Earth, all the colonists were ordered home by rocket, and young Barton was left behind. To keep himself company, he recorded a system of talking tape machines, wired into telephones in a thousand Martian towns, programmed to call him decades later and chat. The old man has outlived the joke, and now the joke is calling.

What is the story actually about? On one level it is about loneliness, in its most extreme form. Barton has spent six decades waiting for a rescue that never arrives. The young Barton's recorded voice is cheerful, cruel and certain, because he is twenty and the future has not happened to him. The old Barton begs him to kill himself, to avoid the long dry years, and the recording can only answer with a laugh, because it is not really listening. It is a machine choosing words from a menu. The young voice says it plainly: you are talking to a transcription, no more.

On a second level, it is about how a person's past self can become an enemy. Barton's younger self is bright, strong and sure, and old Barton hates him for it. He is jealous of his own youth. The young voice says, what will you call it, telecide or suicide? A war breaks out between a man and his own recordings. Barton drives across Mars shooting telephone towers, trying to silence a voice that is everywhere.

The turning point comes when he realizes he cannot destroy the towers. If a rescue rocket ever did come, it would need the phones to find him. So he drops the rifle. It is a heartbreaking moment, because it shows that hope, however absurd, is the one thing he cannot give up. Then the machine turns that hope into a weapon. A voice that claims to be Captain Rockwell of Earth Rocket 48 calls him and tells him to come to a town a thousand miles away. Barton drives all night, his heart failing, to a deserted rocket field and finds a row of phones. The captain was a fake, a prank he himself recorded in a bitter moment. The trip was meant to kill him, and it does.

The final image is the strangest. Barton collapses and dies on the floor, and in the silence the last two telephones ring and connect to each other. Two recordings, a Barton aged twenty-four and a Barton aged twenty-six, talk happily, congratulating each other, and their laughter drifts out the window into the dead city. The machines go on living, in a sense, after the man is gone. The young cruelty outlasts the old suffering.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The idea is excellent, and the opening is a model of economy. The first page is nearly all dialogue, short lines, a ringing phone, a mounting sense of wrongness, and by the time we learn the old man's name and the date we are hooked. Bradbury also makes the central conceit instantly clear: a living man, a recording and the strange fact that both of them are Barton. The conversation across sixty years is a wonderful piece of dramatic structure, in which each party can only partly hear the other, and each says things the other cannot use. The young voice is heartless, but not because it is wicked. It is heartless because it is young, and because it is a tape.

Some of the best writing is in the details of the machinery of loneliness. The smells of bacon and ham drifting out of empty houses, the voices of children singing on schedule, the thousand identical Bartons broadcast through a town to make it sound like a crowd. These are hauntingly specific, and they show how a clever, lonely man can build a world to protect himself and then be trapped inside it. The scene where the old man runs through the dark and phones ring in one cottage after another, like a chain reaction, is a masterpiece of rising dread.

The second half has more problems. The pace is breathless, and the style reaches for melodrama, with a good deal of shrieking and gasping. The old man's rage, his threats to kill the voice, and the long scene in which he shoots towers, feel overheated compared with the quiet unease of the opening. The story also depends on coincidences of logic that do not quite hold. If Barton recorded the fake captain in a depressive fit, as he half remembers, why would he forget it so completely, and why would he have recorded a trap designed to kill his own future self? The tapes are also asked to carry on responsive conversations that no recording could manage, and the story sometimes forgets that its own premise says they respond only to a limited set of stimuli.

The ending is a mixed success. The idea of the last two phones finding each other is memorable and eerie, and the laughter in the dead city is a sharp last image. But it comes right after a long, overwrought death scene in which the man's face seems to decay like flower petals, and some readers may find that sequence too florid. The story is stronger in its ideas than in its fine-grained execution, and the pulp origins show.

Still, the central image lands, and it has aged remarkably well. The notion that your own recorded voice can turn into a companion, a tormentor and finally a replacement is more resonant now than it was in 1949, in a world full of recordings, archives and messages from earlier selves. The best compliment to give the story is that the phone rings in the reader's mind long after it is closed.

III. The Impact It Had

I, Mars was a minor story in its first life, in a pulp magazine, and it drew no major recorded reaction. Its history is more interesting for what Bradbury did with it afterward. He revised it, gave it a new title, Night Call, Collect, and included it in his 1964 collection The Machineries of Joy. That he came back to it, fifteen years after it first appeared, tells us the idea mattered to him. It was one of the stories he thought worth polishing and bringing to a wider readership, and in that later form it has been read in a good many anthologies and classrooms.

The story also gives us a useful glimpse into Bradbury's Martian period. His reputation rests heavily on The Martian Chronicles, published in 1950, in which Mars is a mirror for the settlers' loneliness, nostalgia and guilt. I, Mars belongs with those stories in spirit. The deserted Martian towns, the sense of a colonist abandoned by Earth, and the interplay between human memory and alien landscape are all part of the same mood. It is a story that fits that world even though it is not part of that book.

In terms of ideas, the story anticipates a number of later concerns. The fear that technology might preserve our voices and personalities beyond our deaths, and that the preserved version might be crueler or shallower than the real one, has become a familiar science fiction theme. Stories about artificial companions, digital copies and talking recordings from the past all echo it. It is also a sharp early picture of how communication technology can deepen isolation, a theme that feels remarkably current in an age of constant messaging.

The doppelgänger, the double who persecutes the original, has a long history in literature, running through Poe's William Wilson and Dostoevsky's The Double. Bradbury gives that old figure a modern form, a machine in the shape of a younger self. Some readers have also noticed a kinship with later works about lonely memory and recorded voices, such as Samuel Beckett's play Krapp's Last Tape, in which an old man listens to recordings of his younger self. There is no evidence of direct influence, and it would be wrong to claim any, but the similarity shows how natural the idea was to its era.

What can be said is that it works as a model of how to create a haunting without a ghost. It remains a staple for readers of his collections, and it is a good story to give to someone who thinks of science fiction as all spaceships and ray guns.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who have written about this story, and I am speaking of general response rather than named critics, tend to come back to the phone. There is something universally unnerving about a ringing telephone that should not ring, and Bradbury makes the most of it. The brrrrinnnng that repeats through the story becomes a refrain, a heartbeat, a threat. For many readers, the experience of reading it is the experience of dread building each time the phone rings again.

They also love the central paradox. The conversation between the two Bartons is beautifully constructed, because both are real, and neither can reach the other. Each says things the other needs to hear, and each is prevented from hearing them by time. The young man cannot be warned, because he is only a recording. The old man cannot be comforted, because the voice is not listening. Readers who like to think about time, memory and regret find it a rich little engine of feeling, and it generates the sort of conversation that people continue after the story ends.

A third delight is the specific, eerie imagery of an emptied world. The robots marching into the canal, the smells of dinner coming from empty houses, the drugstore phone booth where the old man is told not to tear down the towers, the movie theater where a young man laughed alone at Laurel and Hardy. These images are strange and memorable, and they carry the emotional weight of the story. Readers often cite the sunken robots as one of the creepiest and saddest details in Bradbury.

Readers also respond to the emotional truth underneath the gimmick. Barton's loneliness is not a gimmick. It is a feeling that many readers recognize, the experience of waiting for a rescue that never comes, of building comforts that become prisons, of failing to appreciate the people and moments that were available. The story is a parable about how a person can lock himself into his own cleverness, and that insight lands with people of all ages.

Finally, many readers admire the way the ending completes the circle. The two young voices laughing in the dead city are chilling and strangely beautiful, because they suggest a kind of afterlife made of recordings, a future in which the young go on living and the old are gone. It is an image that stays with people. It refuses to console, and it refuses to explain, which is probably why it lingers.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Again, the documented critical response to this story is slim. What I can describe are the reservations that thoughtful readers often raise.

The first is melodrama. The second half of the story is loud, full of shrieks, gasps and heart attacks, and some readers feel the pitch is too high. The old man's long march of destruction, his rifle and dynamite, and his repeated cries of rage, can seem like stagecraft after the subtle terror of the opening. Bradbury's early prose was often accused of purple excess, and this story gives that charge some support, particularly in the death scene, where the man's body seems to decay like petals in an overwrought sequence.

The second is plot logic. The story asks us to believe that a young man could build thousands of recorded personalities capable of conversation, and that they would be able to carry on responsive, witty exchanges for decades. Within its own world it half acknowledges that they respond only to a certain number of stimuli, but the dialogue often seems too smart for that. More puzzling is the forgetfulness of the old man. He half remembers recording a fake captain's landing, and yet he lets himself be tricked into a deadly drive. Readers who prize logic find the premise strained, while others say that grief and age explain the lapse.

The third is the question of cruelty. The story's central joke is vicious, since a young man deliberately builds a trap for his future self, and the narrative can feel as if it is punishing Barton for his loneliness. Some readers find the mood of the story bleak to the point of nihilism. There is no consolation, no escape, no rescue. A death in an empty office, in a world of ringing phones, can seem more like a stern lesson than a human tragedy.

The fourth is the way the story handles its science. The year 2097 and the atom war are sketched in with minimal detail. We are told that everyone was called home from Mars, yet we are given no understanding of why one man was left behind or why no rescue came for sixty years. These gaps are part of what makes the setup feel like a fable, but they bother readers who want the world to be solid.

Finally, a few have noted the pulp frame. The story began in a magazine of cheap thrills, and its title and some of its action reflect that. Bradbury's revisions in the later version suggest he too felt it could be improved. The version we are reading, in a sense, is an early draft of a better idea, and readers who come to it from his mature work may find it rougher than they expected.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

I, Mars belongs to the long tradition of the double, the story in which a person meets a version of himself and cannot bear it. Edgar Allan Poe's William Wilson, Dostoevsky's The Double and Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde all explore the terror of an alter ego that persecutes the original. Bradbury updates the double for an age of technology. His doppelgänger is not a ghost or a twin. It is a recording, a machine that carries a younger self into the future and uses it as a weapon.

The story also belongs to the tradition of the last man, the survivor alone in an empty world. From Mary Shelley's novel of that name through later stories of the last survivor on Earth, this is a favorite pattern in science fiction. What Bradbury adds is the idea that the survivor builds a society of echoes around himself, which is both a comfort and a curse. Richard Matheson's I Am Legend, a few years later, would give another famous version of the last man, though with very different concerns.

Within Bradbury's own work, the story sits near some of his best. In There Will Come Soft Rains, written the following year, a house goes on performing its routines after its owners have died, and in this story the telephones keep up their conversations after their maker is gone. Both stories find horror and sadness in machines that carry on faithfully for humans who are no longer there. The Martian setting also links it to his stories of colonists and lost cities, in which Mars is a place where Americans meet their own memories.

Looking forward, the line of descent runs through many modern works about digital ghosts, artificial voices and preserved personalities. Stories in which a person's data, messages or recordings go on after death, or in which an artificial version of someone replaces the original, are everywhere now, from novels to television episodes. It is hard to read the story today without thinking of voicemail, archives and the strange permanence of recorded speech. Samuel Beckett's Krapp's Last Tape, in which an old man listens to his younger self with disgust, is often mentioned alongside it, even though there is no known direct link.

Why read it now? Partly because it is brisk and chilling. Partly because it condenses a profound human fear, that we may be trapped by the person we once were, into a few pages and a ringing phone. And partly because its warnings have grown more relevant with time. We all leave voices behind us, in messages and recordings, and we all have to live with who we used to be. Bradbury put that in a story about a telephone on Mars, and the telephone is still ringing.
