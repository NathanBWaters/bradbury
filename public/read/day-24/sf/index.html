Treasure Island's 1939 World's Fair and the Pan Am China Clipper Flying Boats

San Francisco — How the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers dredged 29 million cubic yards of bay sand onto Yerba Buena Shoals to build an Art Deco fairground and the harbor for Pan American's giant flying boats (New Deal Engineering & Golden Age of Flying Boats (1936–1945)) • 20 min read

“At 3:47 p.m., the Martin M-130 China Clipper lifted off the waters of San Francisco Bay, dipped beneath the unfinished cables of the Bay Bridge, and pointed her nose toward Manila.” — San Francisco Chronicle (November 22, 1935)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Unlike rocky Alcatraz or Yerba Buena Island, flat 400-acre Treasure Island (37.8160° N, 122.3708° W) is 100 percent human-made: between February 1936 and November 1937, eleven U.S. Army Corps of Engineers dredges pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of bay sand inside a 17,760-foot quarry-stone seawall over the shallow Yerba Buena Shoals. Built with Works Progress Administration (WPA) funds to celebrate the completion of both the Golden Gate and Bay Bridges at the 1939–1940 Golden Gate International Exposition before serving as San Francisco's municipal airport, its sheltered lagoon—Clipper Cove—became the legendary home port for Pan American Airways' Martin M-130 and Boeing 314 'China Clipper' flying boats. Today, visitors walking Building 1 (Administration Building) and the seaplane ramps of Hangars 2 and 3 along Clipper Cove in Treasure Island (north of Yerba Buena Island in central San Francisco Bay) can trace every physical milestone of this transformation firsthand.

Standing today at Building 1 (Administration Building) and the seaplane ramps of Hangars 2 and 3 along Clipper Cove in the heart of Treasure Island (north of Yerba Buena Island in central San Francisco Bay) (37.8160° N, 122.3708° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of New Deal Engineering & Golden Age of Flying Boats (1936–1945). Before 1936, mariners steering north of rocky Yerba Buena Island had to watch their depth soundings carefully across the Yerba Buena Shoals—a treacherous submarine sandspit covered by only two to twenty-six feet of bay water that had snagged deep-draft hulls since the Gold Rush. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along Building 1 (Administration Building) and the seaplane ramps of Hangars 2 and 3 along Clipper Cove.

Over the next eighteen months, the giant dredge pipes pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of black bay sand and gravel from the floor of San Francisco Bay and the Sacramento River delta into the stone enclosure, raising the new island thirteen feet above mean lower low water and naming it 'Treasure Island' because the dredged sediments contained trace flakes of Sierra Nevada placer gold. At 3:47 p.m. on November 22, 1935, as 25,000 spectators cheered from the waterfront and the Marin hills, Captain Ed Musick and navigator Fred Noonan (who would vanish two years later with Amelia Earhart) throttled up the four 830-horsepower Pratt & Whitney Twin Wasp engines of the *China Clipper* on San Francisco Bay; when the heavily loaded 26-ton flying boat could not clear the unfinished catwalks of the Bay Bridge, Musick coolly dove *underneath* the bridge cables and flew 2,400 miles overnight to Honolulu. 1. Hydraulic Dredge Reclamation & Soil Desalination (1936–1938)

How did the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers create 400 acres of dry land in the middle of a tidal strait in eighteen months? Using a rock-filled perimeter dike as a giant settling basin, suction dredges pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of submerged sand onto the Yerba Buena Shoals. To prevent the salt-saturated marine sand from killing the 4,000 trees and 40,000 shrubs planted for the 1939 Exposition, engineers installed hundreds of well-points and flushed the island with freshwater until the saline water table dropped below the root zone.
