Treasure Island's 1939 World's Fair and the Pan Am China Clipper Flying Boats

San Francisco — How the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers dredged 29 million cubic yards of bay sand onto Yerba Buena Shoals to build an Art Deco fairground and the harbor for Pan American's giant flying boats (New Deal Engineering & Golden Age of Flying Boats (1936–1945)) • 9 min read

“At 3:47 p.m., the Martin M-130 China Clipper lifted off the waters of San Francisco Bay, dipped beneath the unfinished cables of the Bay Bridge, and pointed her nose toward Manila.” — San Francisco Chronicle (November 22, 1935)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Unlike rocky Alcatraz or Yerba Buena Island, flat 400-acre Treasure Island is 100 percent human-made: between February 1936 and November 1937, eleven U.S. Army Corps of Engineers dredges pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of bay sand inside a 17,760-foot quarry-stone seawall over the shallow Yerba Buena Shoals. Built with Works Progress Administration (WPA) funds to celebrate the completion of both the Golden Gate and Bay Bridges at the 1939–1940 Golden Gate International Exposition before serving as San Francisco's municipal airport, its sheltered lagoon—Clipper Cove—became the legendary home port for Pan American Airways' Martin M-130 and Boeing 314 'China Clipper' flying boats.

Before 1936, mariners steering north of rocky Yerba Buena Island had to watch their depth soundings carefully across the Yerba Buena Shoals—a treacherous submarine sandspit covered by only two to twenty-six feet of bay water that had snagged deep-draft hulls since the Gold Rush. In 1931, San Francisco civic boosters led by Junior Chamber of Commerce aviation enthusiasts proposed an audacious two-part plan to President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Works Progress Administration (WPA): dredge up the hazardous Yerba Buena Shoals to build a 400-acre flat island in the middle of the bay, use it first to host the 1939 Golden Gate International Exposition celebrating the two new bridges, and then convert it into a grand downtown municipal airport.

Beginning on February 11, 1936, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers assembled a fleet of nine hopper and pipeline suction dredges—including the Sacramento, San Joaquin, and Multnomah—while barges dumped 287,000 tons of Sonoma and Marin quarry rock to build a three-and-a-half-mile-long perimeter seawall enclosing a 5,520-foot-by-3,400-foot rectangle. Over the next eighteen months, the giant dredge pipes pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of black bay sand and gravel from the floor of San Francisco Bay and the Sacramento River delta into the stone enclosure, raising the new island thirteen feet above mean lower low water and naming it 'Treasure Island' because the dredged sediments contained trace flakes of Sierra Nevada placer gold.

Before a single palm tree or bird-of-paradise flower could grow for the World's Fair, soil engineers faced a massive chemical obstacle: the twenty-nine million cubic yards of bay sand were saturated with toxic marine salt; using deep drainage wells and tens of millions of gallons of fresh Hetch Hetchy water, crews leached the salt table down six feet below the surface and barged in 100,000 cubic yards of fertile Delta peat loam. Meanwhile, across the bay at Alameda and into the newly formed forty-acre sheltered lagoon between Yerba Buena Island and Treasure Island—christened the 'Port of the Trade Winds' (today's Clipper Cove)—Pan American Airways founder Juan Trippe and chief pilot Captain Edwin C. Musick were conquering the last great ocean barrier in aviation history: the 8,200-mile expanse of the Pacific.

Because no landplane of the 1930s possessed the fuel range or Pacific island airstrips to fly from California to Asia, Pan Am commissioned Glenn L. Martin to build three silver four-engine flying boats—the China Clipper (NC14716), Philippine Clipper, and Hawaiian Clipper—and sent construction crews to build radio direction-finding stations and luxury hotels on tiny coral atolls at Midway, Wake, and Guam. At 3:47 p.m. on November 22, 1935, as 25,000 spectators cheered from the waterfront and the Marin hills, Captain Ed Musick and navigator Fred Noonan (who would vanish two years later with Amelia Earhart) throttled up the four 830-horsepower Pratt & Whitney Twin Wasp engines of the China Clipper on San Francisco Bay; when the heavily loaded 26-ton flying boat could not clear the unfinished catwalks of the Bay Bridge, Musick coolly dove underneath the bridge cables and flew 2,400 miles overnight to Honolulu.

By October 1938, Pan Am transferred its entire Pacific Division headquarters into the permanent, Streamline Moderne concrete Administration Building (Building 1, designed by George W. Kelham and William Peyton Day) and the two gigantic steel-arched seaplane hangars (Buildings 2 and 3) at the southern entrance of Treasure Island. When the Golden Gate International Exposition ('Pageant of the Pacific') opened on Treasure Island on February 18, 1939, visitors stepping off the ferry or driving off the Bay Bridge entered a magical walled 'Magic City' designed by Arthur Brown Jr., Timothy Pflueger, and Ernest Weihe in a fusion of Mayan, Incan, Cambodian, and Art Deco architecture illuminated at night by fluorescent pastel floodlights.

Towering 400 feet above the Court of the Moon stood Brown's octagonal Tower of the Sun crowned by a golden phoenix, surrounded by Ralph Stackpole's colossal eighty-foot statue Pacifica and Diego Rivera painting his monumental ten-panel Pan American Unity fresco live in front of fairgoers inside the Palace of Fine and Decorative Arts. Yet the most thrilling living exhibit of the 1939–1940 World's Fair was not a plaster statue: every week, fairgoers walked into the glassy observation gallery of the Hall of Air Transportation (Hangar 2) to watch white-uniformed Pan Am mechanics service the triple-tailed, double-deck Boeing 314 Clippers (California Clipper, Honolulu Clipper, Anzac Clipper) before the giant seventy-four-passenger flying boats taxied out into Clipper Cove and thundered airborne toward Hong Kong and Auckland.

As war clouds gathered over Europe and Asia after the Exposition closed on September 29, 1940, the United States Navy realized that Treasure Island's strategic mid-bay position was indispensable for fleet logistics; in early 1941, the Navy leased—and in 1942 permanently acquired—Treasure Island in exchange for building San Francisco heavy-runway landplane expansions down at Mills Field (today's SFO). Hours after Japanese aircraft attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, Pan Am's Pacific Clipper (a Boeing 314 caught in New Zealand under Captain Robert Ford) embarked on an epic 31,500-mile westbound flight completely around the globe via Australia, India, Africa, and Brazil to return safely to the United States, while the remaining Clippers at Treasure Island were painted dull navy gray and drafted into wartime military transport.

Throughout World War II, Naval Station Treasure Island processed more than four million American sailors and Marines—serving twelve thousand meals an hour inside its mess halls—while the three permanent 1938 concrete aviation buildings (Building 1 and Hangars 2 and 3) anchored the base until its decommissioning in 1997. Today, as Treasure Island undergoes a long-term geotechnical compaction and sustainable neighborhood transformation, Kelham's semi-circular Streamline Moderne Administration Building (Treasure Island Museum) and the calm waters of Clipper Cove stand as the last physical monuments to the era when San Francisco built an island out of bay sand and launched flying boats across the Pacific.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Hydraulic Dredge Reclamation & Soil Desalination (1936–1938)

How did the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers create 400 acres of dry land in the middle of a tidal strait in eighteen months? Using a rock-filled perimeter dike as a giant settling basin, suction dredges pumped twenty-nine million cubic yards of submerged sand onto the Yerba Buena Shoals. To prevent the salt-saturated marine sand from killing the 4,000 trees and 40,000 shrubs planted for the 1939 Exposition, engineers installed hundreds of well-points and flushed the island with freshwater until the saline water table dropped below the root zone.

2. Why Flying Boats Ruled the Pacific Before Long-Range Runways

In 1935, crossing the 2,400 miles of open ocean between San Francisco and Honolulu—let alone the 8,200 miles to Manila—was impossible for wheeled landplanes, both because no paved airstrips existed on remote coral atolls like Wake and Midway and because any engine failure over water meant death. Pan Am's Martin M-130 and Boeing 314 flying boats turned every protected lagoon—starting with Treasure Island's Clipper Cove—into an instant international airport with dining salons and sleeping berths.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this island's story with three surviving landmarks: first, Building 1 (the 1938 Administration Building), a National Register Streamline Moderne masterpiece that served as Pan Am's Pacific terminal; second, Hangars 2 and 3 (the Hall of Air Transportation and Palace of Fine Arts in 1939), where fairgoers watched Boeing 314 Clippers being overhauled and Diego Rivera painting Pan American Unity (now at SFMOMA/CCSF); and third, Clipper Cove, the sheltered seaplane harbor between Yerba Buena and Treasure Island.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take Muni bus 25-Treasure Island from the Salesforce Transit Center or the electric ferry from the Ferry Building to the Treasure Island Ferry Terminal just outside Building 1 at Avenue of the Palms. Admire the curved Streamline Moderne facade and bas-relief sculptures of Building 1, walk past the colossal steel portals of Hangars 2 and 3 toward the calm beach of Clipper Cove, and take in the sweeping panoramic view of the San Francisco skyline across the water.
