Busan's Gamcheon Terraces & Jagalchi Fish Market: How 1950 Refugees Built a Vertical City by the Sea

Korea — Where 800,000 displaced families stacked pastel houses up Mount Cheonmasan so no roof blocked a neighbor's sunlight, and women ruled the gravel docks (Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present) • 9 min read

“Do not build your eaves one inch higher than the porch behind you; even a refugee’s doorway deserves to see the southern sea and the morning sun.” — Taegeukdo Village Building Rule, Gamcheon (1955)

Key Context & Why It Matters

For 1,023 days between August 1950 and August 1953, the southeastern port city of Busan served as the Provisional Capital (Piran Sudo) of the Republic of Korea, absorbing nearly one million war refugees from Seoul, Hamgyeong, and Pyongan into a city built for 400,000. Lacking flat land along the narrow coastal strip, families and 4,000 members of the Taegeukdo religious community climbed the steep slopes of Mount Cheonmasan and Mount Ongnyeobong in Gamcheon-dong, stacking miniature terraced houses out of driftwood, flattened US Army C-ration tins, and cement blocks. Down on the pebble shore of Nampo-dong, war widows and mothers known as the Jagalchi Ajumma laid wooden planks over beach gravel (jagal) to sell mackerel and squid, forging the economic backbone of post-war Busan.

Climb the narrow concrete stairways of Gamcheon-dong in Saha-gu, Busan—stairways so steep that locals call them the 'Stairs to the Stars' (Byeol-bogo geotneun gyedan, counting 148 vertical steps that leave a climber dizzy enough to see stars in broad daylight)—and look out across the horseshoe-shaped amphitheater between Mount Cheonmasan and Mount Ongnyeobong. Over 1,400 miniature box-shaped houses painted in Robin’s-egg blue, lemon yellow, mint green, and coral pink cascade down the mountain slope like Mediterranean Lego blocks toward the cargo ships of Gamcheon Port. Why does not a single house in Gamcheon block the ocean view or the sunlight of the house directly behind it, even though 1,400 homes are packed shoulder-to-shoulder on a thirty-five-degree mountain incline? That stepped architectural geometry was not designed by a European urban planner; it was born out of wartime survival and a remarkable communal ethic in the 1950s.

In the summer of 1950, as North Korean forces pushed south toward the Nakdong River Perimeter, Busan became the Provisional Capital (Piran Sudo) of South Korea for 1,023 days. Overnight, the port city’s population exploded from 400,000 to over 1.2 million as trains, fishing boats, and American LST landing ships (including the miraculous December 1950 Meredith Victory evacuation of 14,000 refugees from Hungnam) unloaded hundreds of thousands of penniless families onto the Busan docks. Every square meter of flat ground around Busan Station and Yeongdo Bridge quickly filled with tar-paper shanties (panjachon). Forced upward, refugees climbed the barren hillsides of Ami-dong (where desperate families built houses directly atop Japanese colonial tombstones, using carved stone grave markers as foundation blocks and stair steps in what is today Ami-dong Tombstone Culture Village), Sanbokdoro ('Mountain Belly Road'), and the steep coastal valley of Gamcheon.

In 1955–1958, roughly 4,000 displaced followers of Taegeukdo —a syncretic Korean religious movement founded in the late Joseon era by Cho Cheol-je emphasizing cosmic harmony (Eum-Yang) and mutual aid—relocated collectively to the steep, rocky slopes of Gamcheon-dong, establishing Taegeukdo Village. Working together without heavy machinery, the community carved nine horizontal contour alleys across the bowl of the mountain (forming the radial strokes of the Taegeuk trigram symbol when viewed from the air) linked by dozens of vertical fishbone staircases (golmok). Crucially, the village elders enforced a binding moral architectural rule: no household may build its roof high enough to cast a shadow over the front window of the neighbor on the terrace above.

Because construction materials were scarce in 1950s post-war Korea, families scavenged wooden ammunition crates from the US military port, flattened empty tin cans of American C-rations and powdered milk to nail over wooden rafters as waterproof roof shingles, mixed seashore sand with cement into hand-pressed blocks, and built compact one- and two-room homes measuring just four to six pyeong (13 to 20 square meters) with shared communal latrines at the end of each alley. While the hillsides of Gamcheon and Sanbokdoro sheltered the sleeping families at night, the engine that fed them by day roared just over the ridge of Cheonmasan on the rocky southern shoreline of Nampo-dong and Chungmu-dong: Jagalchi Market.

The name Jagalchi comes from jagal —the fist-sized, wave-worn black pebbles that originally covered this stretch of the Busan shoreline—combined with the fish suffix -chi (as in galchi hairtail, myeolchi anchovy, and kkongchi saury). During the Korean War and the hungry 1950s, tens of thousands of women who had lost their husbands to war, conscription, or injury walked down to the pebble beach before dawn with wooden washbasins (dara-i) balanced on their heads. Waiting for wooden trawlers to unload in the freezing salt spray of the Korea Strait, these women—immortalized in Korean culture as the Jagalchi Ajumma (or Oiso, Boiso, Saiso—'Come, Look, Buy!' in resonant Gyeongsang dialect)—bought boxes of chub mackerel (godeungeo), squid (ojingeo), sea squirt (meongge), hagfish (kkiruk-jangeo), and red sea bream on dawn credit, gutted and filleted them with lightning razor strokes on wooden crates set over the wet pebbles, and sold out by afternoon to pay for their children’s schoolbooks and rice.

Nowhere is the culinary resourcefulness of wartime Busan more tangible than in two dishes born right around Jagalchi and the adjacent Gukje ('International') Market. Along the outdoor coal-briquette (yeontan) grills behind Jagalchi, women took tough, cartilaginous inshore hagfish (kkomjangeo)—which had previously been discarded after stripping its skin to make leather wallets and combat boots—and stir-fried it with fiery red gochujang chili paste, crushed garlic, and scallions into a smoky street feast that fueled dockworkers for pennies. Similarly, across the hillside refugee camps, families longing for the cold buckwheat noodles (naengmyeon) of their lost northern hometowns in Hamhung and Pyongyang lacked buckwheat flour; instead, they mixed abundant US military food-aid wheat flour (milgaru) with sweet-potato starch, extruded the chewy yellow noodles through iron presses into icy 24-hour pork-bone and herbal broth, and invented Busan Milmyeon —alongside Dwaeji-gukbap (milky pork-bone rice soup simmered around the clock).

By the early 2000s, as South Korea grew prosperous and high-rise apartments rose across Busan, Gamcheon’s aging hillside alleys faced abandonment; over three hundred miniature houses sat vacant as young residents moved away from the steep stairs. Rather than allowing wrecking balls to raze the historic refugee terraces for generic apartment blocks, a coalition of Busan artists, architects, and local residents launched the 2009 Village Art Project (Dreaming of Machu Picchu in Busan). Working hand-in-hand with the grandmothers still living along the narrow golmok lanes, artists renovated empty shanties into neighborhood book cafés, ceramic studios, a community bathhouse museum, and small galleries; painted the stepped facades in luminous pastel hues inspired by the sea and sky; and perched sculptor Shinuri’s bronze Little Prince and Desert Fox on a guardrail overlooking the harbor.

Down at the waterfront, the old pebble-beach stalls of Jagalchi were joined in 2006 by a soaring seven-story glass-and-steel pavilion designed in the undulating silhouette of three seagulls taking flight over the Korea Strait (Oiso, Boiso, Saiso)—where hundreds of veteran Jagalchi Ajumma still preside over oxygenated seawater tanks on the first floor while visitors dine on fresh hoe (raw fish) and grilled mackerel upstairs. Stand on the terrace of Gamcheon Culture Village at sunset as the warm lights of 1,400 tiny houses flicker on across the bowl of Mount Cheonmasan and fishing trawlers blink out in the harbor toward Japan. Every pastel wall and every 148-step staircase is a monument to a generation that arrived in Busan with nothing but the clothes on their backs—and still remembered to leave the morning sunlight unobstructed for the neighbor behind them.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Vernacular Solar Urbanism: How Gamcheon's Terraced Geometry Prevented Overshadowing

Architectural surveys of Gamcheon Culture Village by Pusan National University researchers highlight its extraordinary passive solar and spatial efficiency. Built along nine contour rings on a 30-to-40-degree valley slope facing south-southwest toward Gamcheon Bay, each tier of single-story flat-roofed houses uses the retaining wall of the terrace behind it as its rear load-bearing spine while its flat concrete roof serves as an open drying terrace or porch for the neighbor above—achieving high urban density with zero solar blockage.

2. Economic History: How the 'Jagalchi Ajumma' Financed Post-War Reconstruction

In the aftermath of the Korean War (1950–1953), when hundreds of thousands of households in Busan were headed by war widows or had disabled breadwinners, the informal maritime economy of Jagalchi Market became a vital engine of female-led capital accumulation. Operating through rotating micro-credit circles (gye) and direct dawn auctions with coastal fishing boats, the Jagalchi Ajumma transformed Busan into Northeast Asia's premier seafood processing and cold-chain hub.

3. Key Korean & Busan Dialect Concepts to Remember

Keep three indelible Busan phrases in mind: first, Oiso, Boiso, Saiso (—'Come here, Look at this, Buy it!'), the warm Gyeongsang dialect motto of Jagalchi Market; second, Piran Sudo (Refugee Provisional Capital), describing Busan's 1,023 days as the seat of survival during the Korean War; and third, Milmyeon and Dwaeji-gukbap, the wheat-noodle and pork-bone soup dishes invented by displaced northern refugees in 1950s Busan.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Start at the Little Prince Museum and Haneul Maru Observatory at the top of Gamcheon Culture Village in Saha-gu, Busan, descending the 148 'Stairs to the Stars' through the labyrinth of pastel alleys. From there, walk or ride the local minibus over the Ami-dong Tombstone Village ridge down to Jagalchi Market in Jung-gu to watch the dawn fish auction and eat charcoal-grilled mackerel and kkomjangeo.
