Busan's Gamcheon Terraces & Jagalchi Fish Market: How 1950 Refugees Built a Vertical City by the Sea

Korea — Where 800,000 displaced families stacked pastel houses up Mount Cheonmasan so no roof blocked a neighbor's sunlight, and women ruled the gravel docks (Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present) • 20 min read

“Do not build your eaves one inch higher than the porch behind you; even a refugee’s doorway deserves to see the southern sea and the morning sun.” — Taegeukdo Village Building Rule, Gamcheon (1955)

Key Context & Why It Matters

For 1,023 days between August 1950 and August 1953, the southeastern port city of Busan served as the Provisional Capital (Piran Sudo) of the Republic of Korea, absorbing nearly one million war refugees from Seoul, Hamgyeong, and Pyongan into a city built for 400,000. Lacking flat land along the narrow coastal strip, families and 4,000 members of the Taegeukdo religious community climbed the steep slopes of Mount Cheonmasan and Mount 옥녀봉 in Gamcheon-dong, stacking miniature terraced houses out of driftwood, flattened US Army C-ration tins, and cement blocks. Down on the pebble shore of Nampo-dong, war widows and mothers known as the Jagalchi Ajumma laid wooden planks over beach gravel (*jagal*) to sell mackerel and squid, forging the economic backbone of post-war Busan.

Climb the narrow concrete stairways of **Gamcheon-dong** (감천동) in Saha-gu, Busan—stairways so steep that locals call them the 'Stairs to the Stars' (*Byeol-bogo geotneun gyedan*, counting 148 vertical steps that leave a climber dizzy enough to see stars in broad daylight)—and look out across the horseshoe-shaped amphitheater between Mount Cheonmasan and Mount Ongnyeobong. Over 1,400 miniature box-shaped houses painted in Robin’s-egg blue, lemon yellow, mint green, and coral pink cascade down the mountain slope like Mediterranean Lego blocks toward the cargo ships of Gamcheon Port. When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Gamcheon Culture Village & Jagalchi Market, Saha-gu & Jung-gu, Busan (35.0975° N, 129.0106° E) to study Gamcheon Culture Village (감천문화마을 stepped hillside refugee village), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Gamcheon and Jagalchi in Busan shaped every decision made during the Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present. Nothing in this setting is accidental: the orientation of the ground, the seasonal angle of sunlight, and the acoustic stillness surrounding Jagalchi Ajumma (자갈치 아줌마 matriarchs of Korea's largest fish market) establish an immediate sensory bridge between twenty-first-century observers and the historical artisans who worked here.

Why does not a single house in Gamcheon block the ocean view or the sunlight of the house directly behind it, even though 1,400 homes are packed shoulder-to-shoulder on a thirty-five-degree mountain incline? That stepped architectural geometry was not designed by a European urban planner; it was born out of wartime survival and a remarkable communal ethic in the 1950s. In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present, the rise of Gamcheon Culture Village (감천문화마을 stepped hillside refugee village) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. In the summer of 1950, as North Korean forces pushed south toward the Nakdong River Perimeter, Busan became the **Provisional Capital** (*Piran Sudo*, 피란수도) of South Korea for 1,023 days. Overnight, the port city’s population exploded from 400,000 to over 1.2 million as trains, fishing boats, and American LST landing ships (including the miraculous December 1950 *Meredith Victory* evacuation of 14,000 refugees from Hungnam) unloaded hundreds of thousands of penniless families onto the Busan docks. Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Gamcheon and Jagalchi in Busan stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Jagalchi Ajumma (자갈치 아줌마 matriarchs of Korea's largest fish market) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Piran Sudo Busan (피란수도 부산 1,023-day wartime provisional capital) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

Every square meter of flat ground around 부산역 (Busan Station) and Yeongdo Bridge quickly filled with tar-paper shanties (*panjachon*, 판자촌). Forced upward, refugees climbed the barren hillsides of Ami-dong (where desperate families built houses directly atop Japanese colonial tombstones, using carved stone grave markers as foundation blocks and stair steps in what is today *Ami-dong Tombstone Culture Village*), Sanbokdoro ('Mountain Belly Road'), and the steep coastal valley of Gamcheon. In 1955–1958, roughly 4,000 displaced followers of **Taegeukdo** (태극도)—a syncretic Korean religious movement founded in the late Joseon era by Cho Cheol-je emphasizing cosmic harmony (*Eum-Yang*) and mutual aid—relocated collectively to the steep, rocky slopes of Gamcheon-dong, establishing *Taegeukdo Village*. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Every raw element gathered near Gamcheon and Jagalchi in Busan had to be tested for density, moisture response, and structural resilience before it could be incorporated into Gamcheon Culture Village (감천문화마을 stepped hillside refugee village), ensuring that the interplay between Jagalchi Ajumma (자갈치 아줌마 matriarchs of Korea's largest fish market) and Piran Sudo Busan (피란수도 부산 1,023-day wartime provisional capital) would withstand centuries of monsoon humidity and freezing winter temperatures.

Working together without heavy machinery, the community carved nine horizontal contour alleys across the bowl of the mountain (forming the radial strokes of the *Taegeuk* trigram symbol when viewed from the air) linked by dozens of vertical fishbone staircases (*golmok*). Crucially, the village elders enforced a binding moral architectural rule: **no household may build its roof high enough to cast a shadow over the front window of the neighbor on the terrace above**. Because construction materials were scarce in 1950s post-war Korea, families scavenged wooden ammunition crates from the US military port, flattened empty tin cans of American C-rations and powdered milk to nail over wooden rafters as waterproof roof shingles, mixed seashore sand with cement into hand-pressed blocks, and built compact one- and two-room homes measuring just four to six *pyeong* (13 to 20 square meters) with shared communal latrines at the end of each alley. While the hillsides of Gamcheon and Sanbokdoro sheltered the sleeping families at night, the engine that fed them by day roared just over the ridge of Cheonmasan on the rocky southern shoreline of Nampo-dong and Chungmu-dong: **Jagalchi Market** (자갈치시장). That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Gamcheon and Jagalchi in Busan. The name *Jagalchi* comes from *jagal* (자갈)—the fist-sized, wave-worn black pebbles that originally covered this stretch of the Busan shoreline—combined with the fish suffix *-chi* (as in *galchi* hairtail, *myeolchi* anchovy, and *kkongchi* saury). During the Korean War and the hungry 1950s, tens of thousands of women who had lost their husbands to war, conscription, or injury walked down to the pebble beach before dawn with wooden washbasins (*dara-i*) balanced on their heads. Primary documentary evidence from the Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present confirms the extraordinary care devoted to recording these breakthroughs. Waiting for wooden trawlers to unload in the freezing salt spray of the Korea Strait, these women—immortalized in Korean culture as the **Jagalchi Ajumma** (자갈치 아줌마, or *Oiso, Boiso, Saiso*—'Come, Look, Buy!' in resonant Gyeongsang dialect)—bought boxes of chub mackerel (*godeungeo*, 고등어), squid (*ojingeo*), sea squirt (*meongge*), hagfish (*kkiruk-jangeo*), and red sea bream on dawn credit, gutted and filleted them with lightning razor strokes on wooden crates set over the wet pebbles, and sold out by afternoon to pay for their children’s schoolbooks and rice. Nowhere is the culinary resourcefulness of wartime Busan more tangible than in two dishes born right around Jagalchi and the adjacent Gukje ('International') Market. Along the outdoor coal-briquette (*yeontan*) grills behind Jagalchi, women took tough, cartilaginous inshore hagfish (*kkomjangeo*, 꼼장어)—which had previously been discarded after stripping its skin to make leather wallets and combat boots—and stir-fried it with fiery red *gochujang* chili paste, crushed garlic, and scallions into a smoky street feast that fueled dockworkers for pennies. 1. Vernacular Solar Urbanism: How Gamcheon's Terraced Geometry Prevented Overshadowing

Architectural surveys of Gamcheon Culture Village by Pusan National University researchers highlight its extraordinary passive solar and spatial efficiency. Built along nine contour rings on a 30-to-40-degree valley slope facing south-southwest toward Gamcheon Bay, each tier of single-story flat-roofed houses uses the retaining wall of the terrace behind it as its rear load-bearing spine while its flat concrete roof serves as an open drying terrace or porch for the neighbor above—achieving high urban density with zero solar blockage. Studying the structural interplay between Gamcheon Culture Village (감천문화마을 stepped hillside refugee village) and Jagalchi Ajumma (자갈치 아줌마 matriarchs of Korea's largest fish market) within the context of the Korean War Provisional Capital Era (1950–1953) to Present demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint.

2. Economic History: How the 'Jagalchi Ajumma' Financed Post-War Reconstruction

In the aftermath of the Korean War (1950–1953), when hundreds of thousands of households in Busan were headed by war widows or had disabled breadwinners, the informal maritime economy of Jagalchi Market became a vital engine of female-led capital accumulation. Operating through rotating micro-credit circles (gye, 계) and direct dawn auctions with coastal fishing boats, the Jagalchi Ajumma transformed Busan into Northeast Asia's premier seafood processing and cold-chain hub. Keep three indelible Busan phrases in mind: first, Oiso, Boiso, Saiso (오이소, 보이소, 사이소—'Come here, Look at this, Buy it!'), the warm Gyeongsang dialect motto of Jagalchi Market; second, Piran Sudo (피란수도, 'Refugee Provisional Capital'), describing Busan's 1,023 days as the seat of survival during the Korean War; and third, Milmyeon and Dwaeji-gukbap (밀면과 돼지국밥), the wheat-noodle and pork-bone soup dishes invented by displaced northern refugees in 1950s Busan.
