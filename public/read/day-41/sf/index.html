San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • Indigenous & Colonial History, Black Military Pioneers & Conservation

Captain Charles Young, the Buffalo Soldiers of the Presidio, and Juana Briones at El Polín Spring

San Francisco — How an ancient Ohlone watershed and a Mexican Californio healer's homestead met the African American cavalrymen who became America's first National Park Rangers in 1903 (Ohlone Era, Mexican Rancho Period (1830s) & Buffalo Soldier Era (1899–1904)) • 9 min read

“A journey through the parks where the 9th Cavalry patrolled is a lesson in what disciplined men can preserve for a nation that has not yet given them their full due.” — Captain Charles Young, 9th U.S. Cavalry (1903)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Follow the restored willow-lined creek of the Tennessee Hollow Watershed to El Polín Spring in the Presidio of San Francisco, and two profound chapters of American resilience converge on the same hillside. Here, beside a perennial serpentine freshwater spring tended for millennia by the Yelamu Ramaytush Ohlone, Afro-Indigenous and Spanish Californio healer, rancher, and domestic-violence survivor Juana Briones de Miranda (1802–1889) built her 1830s adobe and dairy farm before moving to North Beach's Washington Square. Six decades later, between 1899 and 1904, the brick barracks of the Presidio housed four regiments of African American Buffalo Soldiers—most famously Troop I of the 9th U.S. Cavalry under Captain Charles Young (the third Black graduate of West Point), who rode horseback 260 miles from the Presidio in May 1903 to become the first Black superintendent of a U.S. National Park at Sequoia and General Grant.

Why did every human community that ever settled the northern tip of the San Francisco Peninsula gravitate toward the sheltered valley of Tennessee Hollow in the central Presidio? Because where porous coastal dune sand meets impermeable green Franciscan serpentinite bedrock at 180 feet elevation, subterranean groundwater is forced to the surface at El Polín Spring—forming the principal perennial freshwater watershed feeding the Crissy Field tidal marsh. For thousands of years before Spanish ships entered the Golden Gate, the Yelamu Ohlone village of Petlenuc harvested basketry willow shoots, medicinal mugwort, and fresh spring water along thethree tributaries of this creek; when Captain Juan Bautista de Anza and Lieutenant José Joaquín Moraga established the Spanish Presidio in 1776, they sited their adobe fort immediately downstream from the watershed.

Born in 1802 at the Villa de Branciforte (Santa Cruz) to families of Spanish, Indigenous, and Afro-Mexican heritage who had marched north with the Anza and Rivera expeditions, Juana Briones de Miranda grew up at the Presidio of San Francisco and mastered two bodies of knowledge: European pharmacopoeia and native Ohlone herbal medicine, becoming the most trusted midwife, bone-setter, and smallpox caregiver on the northern peninsula. In the 1830s, while enduring years of severe physical abuse from her husband, Presidio cavalry soldier Apolinario Miranda, Juana Briones took a step virtually unheard of in nineteenth-century patriarchal society: she petitioned Mexican civil and ecclesiastical authorities for a legal separation and independent household headship, establishing her own cattle ranch and vegetable farm at Ojo de Agua de Figueroa next to El Polín Spring in the Presidio.

From her adobe beside El Polín Spring and later from her second home on Powell and Filbert streets in North Beach (where Washington Square Park was originally known as 'Juana Briones Park'), she sold fresh milk, vegetables, and hides to Boston whaling ships anchored in Yerba Buena Cove, sheltered runaway sailors and Indigenous servants, and later defended her 4,400-acre Santa Clara Valley land grant (Rancho La Purísima Concepción) all the way to the United States Supreme Court. More than half a century after Juana Briones tended her orchards beside El Polín Spring, in the spring of 1899, a new generation of pioneers marched into the Presidio of San Francisco: the four all-Black regular regiments of the United States Army—the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the 24th and 25th Infantry, known since their post-Civil War frontier campaigns as the Buffalo Soldiers.

Why was the Presidio of San Francisco the central hub for the Buffalo Soldiers between 1898 and 1904? Following the Spanish-American War in Cuba (where the 9th and 10th Cavalry charged up San Juan and Kettle Hills alongside Theodore Roosevelt) and during the Philippine-American War, the Presidio served as the U.S. Army's primary Pacific embarkation depot and home garrison. Quartered in the red-brick Montgomery Street Barracks on the Main Post and tending their horses in the Cavalry Stables above Crissy Field, the Buffalo Soldiers were also assigned an extraordinary peacetime mission by the Secretary of the Interior and the Secretary of War every spring between 1899 and 1904.

Because Congress had created Yosemite, Sequoia, and General Grant (now Kings Canyon) National Parks in 1890 without appropriating funds for a civilian National Park Service (which was not founded until 1916), the U.S. Army cavalry stationed at the Presidio in San Francisco served as America's first National Park Rangers. Every May in 1899, 1903, and 1904, roughly five hundred Buffalo Soldiers saddled their mounts at the Presidio of San Francisco and rode 260 to 320 miles south and east across the San Joaquin Valley and up into the High Sierra—spending sixteen days in the saddle to protect the giant sequoias and mountain meadows from illegal timber poachers, sheepherders ('hoofed locusts'), and wildfire.

In May 1903, leading Troops I and M of the 9th U.S. Cavalry out of the Presidio gates was thirty-nine-year-old Captain Charles Young—born into slavery in Kentucky in 1864, fluent in six languages, a gifted classical musician and cartographer, only the third African American to graduate from West Point (Class of 1889), and the only Black commissioned line officer in the U.S. Army at the turn of the century. Appointed Acting Military Superintendent of Sequoia and General Grant National Parks for the summer of 1903—making him the first African American superintendent of a U.S. National Park—Captain Young and his Presidio Buffalo Soldiers achieved more in a single summer season than all three previous white military superintendents combined.

Working with pickaxes, dynamite, and grading teams at 7,000 feet elevation, Young's 9th Cavalry troopers built the first eighteen-foot wagon road directly into the Giant Forest—allowing average visitors to reach the world's largest living tree, the General Sherman Tree, by wagon for the first time in history—constructed a perimeter fence to protect the giant sequoia roots from compaction, and negotiated the historic sale of private land holdings inside the park back to the public. When prominent local citizens proposed naming a giant sequoia tree in honor of Captain Charles Young in August 1903, the modest officer declined and requested instead that the tree be named for civil-rights leader Booker T. Washington (and a century later, in 2004, the National Park Service officially dedicated the Colonel Charles Young Tree nearby in Giant Forest).

Returning to the Presidio of San Francisco in the autumn of 1903, Captain Young served as one of the earliest professors of military science, went on to serve as military attaché to Haiti and Liberia and commander in the 1916 Punitive Expedition in Mexico, and in 2021 was posthumously promoted to Brigadier General. Today, over 450 Buffalo Soldiers rest beneath white marble headstones in the San Francisco National Cemetery on Lincoln Boulevard in the Presidio, while just down the slope at El Polín Spring, archaeological exhibits honor both Juana Briones's 1830s homestead and the Black cavalrymen who rode out from the Golden Gate to build the American conservation movement.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Hydrogeology of El Polín Spring and the Tennessee Hollow Watershed

What makes El Polín Spring flow year-round even during California's dry, rainless summers? Rainwater and dense summer fog-drip soaking into the sandy soils and forested ridges of the upper Presidio percolate downward until hitting an underlying faulted block of impermeable green Franciscan serpentinite bedrock. Forced laterally along the fracture zone, the cool filtered water emerges at El Polín Spring, sustaining three surface creeks that flow north for one mile into the restored Crissy Field tidal salt marsh.

2. How the Presidio's Buffalo Soldiers Built Sequoia and Yosemite Infrastructure

Thirteen years before the National Park Service was created in 1916, the African American troopers of the 9th Cavalry and 24th Infantry stationed at the Presidio of San Francisco pioneered modern park stewardship. In Yosemite (1899 and 1904), 24th Infantry soldiers built one of the first back-country arboretums in the Sierra; in Sequoia (1903), Captain Charles Young's 9th Cavalry built more miles of mountain road into the Giant Forest in one summer than the three previous years combined—proving that Black soldiers endured Jim Crow to preserve a national birthright for all Americans.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three historical figures and sites: first, Juana Briones de Miranda (1802–1889), the Afro-Indigenous and Spanish Californio healer and independent landowner whose 1830s homestead stood beside El Polín Spring; second, Captain (later Brigadier General) Charles Young, America's first Black National Park Superintendent in 1903; and third, the San Francisco National Cemetery and Cavalry Stables in the Presidio.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Begin at the Main Post of the Presidio along Montgomery Street to see the 1890s red-brick barracks where the Buffalo Soldiers were quartered, then walk up to the San Francisco National Cemetery on Sheridan Avenue to honor the 450 Buffalo Soldier graves. Finally, walk south along the Tennessee Hollow Trail to El Polín Spring to read the interpretive plaques at the archaeological site of Juana Briones's homestead.
