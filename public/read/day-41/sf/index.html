Key Context & Why It Matters

Follow the restored willow-lined creek of the Tennessee Hollow Watershed to **El Polín Spring** in the Presidio of San Francisco (37.7952° N, 122.4561° W), and two profound chapters of American resilience converge on the same hillside. Here, beside a perennial serpentine freshwater spring tended for millennia by the Yelamu Ramaytush Ohlone, Afro-Indigenous and Spanish *Californio* healer, rancher, and domestic-violence survivor **Juana Briones de Miranda** (1802–1889) built her 1830s adobe and dairy farm before moving to North Beach's Washington Square. Six decades later, between 1899 and 1904, the brick barracks of the Presidio housed four regiments of African American **Buffalo Soldiers**—most famously **Troop I of the 9th U.S. Cavalry** under **Captain Charles Young** (the third Black graduate of West Point), who rode horseback 260 miles from the Presidio in May 1903 to become the first Black superintendent of a U.S. National Park at Sequoia and General Grant.

Why did every human community that ever settled the northern tip of the San Francisco Peninsula gravitate toward the sheltered valley of Tennessee Hollow in the central Presidio? Because where porous coastal dune sand meets impermeable green Franciscan serpentinite bedrock at 180 feet elevation, subterranean groundwater is forced to the surface at **El Polín Spring**—forming the principal perennial freshwater watershed feeding the Crissy Field tidal marsh. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how healer and landowner Juana Briones de Miranda (1802–1889) and Captain Charles Young (1864–1922) of the 9th U.S. Cavalry Buffalo Soldiers and a generation of San Franciscans confronted securing independent female land ownership and herbal medical sanctuary in 1830s Mexican California and pioneering federal conservation management in 1903 despite Jim Crow segregation, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

Born in 1802 at the Villa de Branciforte (Santa Cruz) to families of Spanish, Indigenous, and Afro-Mexican heritage who had marched north with the Anza and Rivera expeditions, **Juana Briones de Miranda** grew up at the Presidio of San Francisco and mastered two bodies of knowledge: European pharmacopoeia and native Ohlone herbal medicine, becoming the most trusted midwife, bone-setter, and smallpox caregiver on the northern peninsula. Contemporary observers who walked past El Polín Spring at the head of MacArthur Avenue in the Tennessee Hollow Watershed and the Montgomery Street Barracks in the Presidio noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

In the 1830s, while enduring years of severe physical abuse from her husband, Presidio cavalry soldier Apolinario Miranda, Juana Briones took a step virtually unheard of in nineteenth-century patriarchal society: she petitioned Mexican civil and ecclesiastical authorities for a legal separation and independent household headship, establishing her own cattle ranch and vegetable farm at *Ojo de Agua de Figueroa* next to El Polín Spring in the Presidio. From her adobe beside El Polín Spring and later from her second home on Powell and Filbert streets in North Beach (where Washington Square Park was originally known as 'Juana Briones Park'), she sold fresh milk, vegetables, and hides to Boston whaling ships anchored in Yerba Buena Cove, sheltered runaway sailors and Indigenous servants, and later defended her 4,400-acre Santa Clara Valley land grant (*Rancho La Purísima Concepción*) all the way to the United States Supreme Court. More than half a century after Juana Briones tended her orchards beside El Polín Spring, in the spring of 1899, a new generation of pioneers marched into the Presidio of San Francisco: the four all-Black regular regiments of the United States Army—the **9th and 10th Cavalry** and the **24th and 25th Infantry**, known since their post-Civil War frontier campaigns as the **Buffalo Soldiers**. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Why was the Presidio of San Francisco the central hub for the Buffalo Soldiers between 1898 and 1904? Following the Spanish-American War in Cuba (where the 9th and 10th Cavalry charged up San Juan and Kettle Hills alongside Theodore Roosevelt) and during the Philippine-American War, the Presidio served as the U.S. Army's primary Pacific embarkation depot and home garrison. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of El Polín Spring, the Presidio Cavalry Stables (Buildings 661–667), and the San Francisco National Cemetery toward private speculation—forcing healer and landowner Juana Briones de Miranda (1802–1889) and Captain Charles Young (1864–1922) of the 9th U.S. Cavalry Buffalo Soldiers and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

Quartered in the red-brick Montgomery Street Barracks on the Main Post and tending their horses in the Cavalry Stables above Crissy Field, the Buffalo Soldiers were also assigned an extraordinary peacetime mission by the Secretary of the Interior and the Secretary of War every spring between 1899 and 1904. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of El Polín Spring, the Presidio Cavalry Stables (Buildings 661–667), and the San Francisco National Cemetery was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of El Polín Spring at the head of MacArthur Avenue in the Tennessee Hollow Watershed and the Montgomery Street Barracks in the Presidio. Every May in 1899, 1903, and 1904, roughly five hundred Buffalo Soldiers saddled their mounts at the Presidio of San Francisco and rode 260 to 320 miles south and east across the San Joaquin Valley and up into the High Sierra—spending sixteen days in the saddle to protect the giant sequoias and mountain meadows from illegal timber poachers, sheepherders ('hoofed locusts'), and wildfire. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Hydrogeology of El Polín Spring and the Tennessee Hollow Watershed

What makes El Polín Spring flow year-round even during California's dry, rainless summers? Rainwater and dense summer fog-drip soaking into the sandy soils and forested ridges of the upper Presidio percolate downward until hitting an underlying faulted block of impermeable green Franciscan serpentinite bedrock. Forced laterally along the fracture zone, the cool filtered water emerges at El Polín Spring, sustaining three surface creeks that flow north for one mile into the restored Crissy Field tidal salt marsh. At El Polín Spring at the head of MacArthur Avenue in the Tennessee Hollow Watershed and the Montgomery Street Barracks in the Presidio in the Presidio of San Francisco (Tennessee Hollow and Main Post), this synthesis of serpentine fracture-spring hydrogeology, Mexican *diseño* land-grant law, and early National Park trail and fire-suppression engineering and civic memory remains visible at every turn.

2. How the Presidio's Buffalo Soldiers Built Sequoia and Yosemite Infrastructure

Thirteen years before the National Park Service was created in 1916, the African American troopers of the 9th Cavalry and 24th Infantry stationed at the Presidio of San Francisco pioneered modern park stewardship. In Yosemite (1899 and 1904), 24th Infantry soldiers built one of the first back-country arboretums in the Sierra; in Sequoia (1903), Captain Charles Young's 9th Cavalry built more miles of mountain road into the Giant Forest in one summer than the three previous years combined—proving that Black soldiers endured Jim Crow to preserve a national birthright for all Americans.
