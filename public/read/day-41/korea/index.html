Yun Dong-ju's 'Sky, Wind, and Stars' (1941–1945): The Poems Hid Under Floorboards That Outlived Prison

Korea — How a twenty-four-year-old poet who felt shame at the rustle of a leaf in the wind entrusted nineteen handwritten poems to a mother's porcelain jar before dying in Fukuoka (Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945)) • 10 min read

“Until the day I die, may I look up toward heaven without a single speck of shame. Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, I suffered.” — Yun Dong-ju, Prologue (Seosi, November 20, 1941)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In every public poll conducted across South Korea over the past half-century asking citizens to name the most beloved Korean poet and poem of all time, one name wins by an overwhelming margin: Yun Dong-ju (1917–1945) and his nine-line masterpiece 'Seosi' ('Prologue,' written on November 20, 1941). Refusing to publish in Japanese when the colonial government banned the Korean language in schools and press, twenty-four-year-old Yun hand-bound three manuscript copies of nineteen pure-Hangul poems titled 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem' before leaving Seoul. Arrested in Kyoto in 1943 for Korean independence thought and dying in Fukuoka Prison six months before Liberation, his voice survived because a friend's mother hid one manuscript inside a porcelain jar beneath a wooden maru floor in Gwangyang.

Walk up the pine-scented slope of Mount Inwangsan at the top of Buam-dong and Cheongun-dong in Jongno-gu, Seoul, where a decommissioned 1974 concrete municipal water-pressure tank has been converted by architect Lee So-jin into a luminous white courtyard sanctuary: the Yun Dong-ju Literature House. Inside the first room, displayed under soft light beside a weathered wooden well-head, rests a reproduction of a humble, thread-bound manuscript notebook brushed in fountain pen on squared grid paper (wongoji). On its cover are written nine quiet Hangul syllables: Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si—'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem').

Had that single handwritten bundle of grid paper been discovered by Japanese military police between 1941 and 1945, or rotted in damp soil, modern Korea would have lost its moral compass in verse: Yun Dong-ju (December 30, 1917 – February 16, 1945). Born in Myeongdong Village in North Jiandao (Bukgando, modern Longjing, Jilin Province)—a Christian Korean exile community across the Tumen River where families had established Korean-language schools to resist Japanese rule—Yun Dong-ju grew up alongside his lifelong cousin, friend, and fellow resistance fighter Song Mong-gyu (born in the same house just three months earlier!).

In the spring of 1938, twenty-year-old Yun Dong-ju and Song Mong-gyu traveled south to Seoul to enroll in the Literature Department of Yonhi College (today’s Yonsei University in Sinchon). There, studying under the great Korean linguist Choe Hyeon-bae (who was repeatedly imprisoned by Japanese authorities for defending the Korean Hangul alphabet), Yun fell in love with the pure music of native Korean words. Yet the years 1938 to 1941 were the darkest midnight of Japanese colonial cultural erasure. Under Governor-General Minami Jiro, the regime banned Korean-language instruction in schools, shut down Korean newspapers (Dong-A Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo in August 1940), forced Koreans to adopt Japanese surnames (Changssi-gaemyeong), and pressured prominent older Korean writers to write pro-war propaganda in Japanese.

While established literary giants compromised under coercion, twenty-three-year-old student Yun Dong-ju and his younger Yonhi roommate Jung Byeong-uk (1922–1982, later a renowned professor of classical Korean literature at Seoul National University) rented a small boarding room in Nusang-dong at the foot of Mount Inwangsan. Every morning before dawn, the two youths hiked up the granite trails of Inwangsan, washed their faces in cold mountain spring water, and read banned French poetry (Francis Jammes, Rainer Maria Rilke, Paul Valéry) and Korean verse by Baek Seok—whose rare 1936 poetry collection Deer Yun loved so deeply that he borrowed a copy from the library and copied the entire book out by hand in fountain pen!

Across the autumn of 1941, preparing to graduate early from Yonhi College in December because of wartime mobilization, Yun Dong-ju wrote a sequence of poems in pure Hangul—knowing that not a single printer in Korea would dare publish a Korean-language book under colonial censorship. Instead of roaring political slogans, Yun turned his lens inward in agonizing moral self-examination. Look at 'Self-Portrait' (Jahwasang, September 1939): a youth walks out to a lonely village well surrounded by autumn rice paddies and looks down into the dark water. Reflected in the well are the moon, passing clouds, the clear sky, a blue wind, autumn—and a man. At first he hates the helpless man in the water and turns away; walking back, he pities the man; returning to peer into the well again, he misses the man as if he were a lost memory.

Fifteen days later, on November 20, 1941, Yun wrote the nine lines that every Korean schoolchild and elder knows by heart—'Seosi' (Prologue): 'Until the day I die, / may I look up to heaven / without a single speck of shame. / Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, / I suffered. / With a heart that sings the stars, / I shall love all dying things. / And I must walk the path / that has been given to me. / Tonight, again, the stars are brushed by the wind.' To experience Yun Dong-ju (1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light.

Pause over that fourth line: 'Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, I suffered.' Why did a gentle breeze stirring a leaf cause twenty-four-year-old Yun Dong-ju acute moral anguish? Because in a world where his country was chained and millions were dying in war, he felt that merely breathing quietly under a tree without having yet sacrificed his life for truth was a compromise of conscience! In December 1941, selecting nineteen of his finest poems and titling the collection Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem, Yun Dong-ju hand-copied three identical manuscript booklets: one for himself, one for his mentor Lee Yang-ha (who begged him not to try publishing it for fear the Japanese police would arrest him on the spot), and one for his beloved roommate Jung Byeong-uk.

In 1942, crossing to Japan to study English literature at Rikkyo University in Tokyo and Doshisha University in Kyoto (where he wrote his final surviving poem in June 1942, 'Easily Written Poem', asking in heartbreak: 'While others lose their freedom, why is a poem written so easily? It is a shameful thing'), Yun Dong-ju and his cousin Song Mong-gyu were arrested by the Kyoto Tokko (Special Higher Police) on July 14, 1943, charged with organizing a Korean student independence movement. Sentenced to two years at Fukuoka Prison in Kyushu, the two cousins were subjected to starvation rations and mysterious repeated saline/blood injections. On February 16, 1945—just six months before Korea’s Liberation on August 15—twenty-seven-year-old Yun Dong-ju died in his cell with a final cry, followed twenty-two days later by Song Mong-gyu in the same prison.

How did Yun’s poems survive? Before Jung Byeong-uk was conscripted as a student soldier into the Japanese army in 1944, he handed the handwritten 1941 manuscript of Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem to his mother, Park Cha-jeong, at their family house (an old brewery house on Mangdeok Island in Gwangyang, South Jeolla Province), saying: 'If Dong-ju and I both die in the war, bury this with our bones; if we live, keep it safer than your life.' Jung’s mother wrapped the manuscript in a silk cloth, sealed it inside a white porcelain jar filled with dried silkworm cocoons to absorb moisture, and hid the jar underneath the wooden maru floorboards of the house!

When Jung Byeong-uk survived the war and returned to Gwangyang in autumn 1945 to learn that Yun Dong-ju had died in Fukuoka Prison, his mother pried up the floorboards and lifted out the porcelain jar. Together with poet Kang Cheo-jung (who had preserved late poems Yun mailed from Tokyo in a photo album), they published the first edition of Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem on January 30, 1948—fulfilling Yun’s 1941 prophecy that green grass would sprout proudly over the hill where his name was buried.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Poetics of the 'Pure Hangul' Manuscript under Totalitarian Erasure

Literary historians emphasize that writing exclusively in Korean Hangul between 1940 and 1942 was in itself an act of quiet, non-violent civil disobedience. After the colonial regime abolished Korean-language newspapers in August 1940 and enforced Japanese-only literary publication (Kokugo bungaku), Yun Dong-ju's refusal to abandon his mother tongue—refining crystal-clear everyday Korean diction in 'Seosi,' 'Self-Portrait,' and 'Star-Counting Night'—kept the lyrical bloodstream of modern Korean literature alive.

2. The Gwangyang Floorboard Jar: How One Mother Saved Korea's Most Beloved Book

The physical survival of Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem (Registered Cultural Heritage No. 712) depended on the courage of Jung Byeong-uk and his mother at 23 Mangdeok-gil in Jinwol-myeon, Gwangyang (Registered Cultural Heritage No. 341). By wrapping Yun's 1941 handwritten wongoji booklet in silk, placing it inside a porcelain jar lined with desiccating silkworm cocoons, and concealing it beneath the elevated maru wooden floorboards from 1943 to 1945, she protected the manuscript from both police raids and coastal humidity.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three poetic touchstones in mind: first, Seosi ('Prologue,' Nov 20, 1941), Yun's nine-line vow to live without a speck of shame before heaven; second, Byeol Heneun Bam (Star-Counting Night), his elegiac roll call of memory, mother, and spring resurrection; and third, Wongoji, the 200-square manuscript grid paper on which Yun hand-lettered his nineteen poems.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Begin at the Yun Dong-ju Literature House in Cheongun-dong, Jongno-gu, Seoul—where a quiet outdoor courtyard called the 'Open Well' frames a square of open sky directly above your head—and walk the Poet's Hill trail along Inwangsan down to his Nusang-dong boarding house site. Then visit the Yun Dong-ju Memorial Room inside Pinson Hall (the stone dormitory where he wrote 'Seosi') on the Yonsei University campus, and the preserved Jung Byeong-uk House on Mangdeok Port in Gwangyang.
