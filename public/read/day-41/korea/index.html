Yun Dong-ju's 'Sky, Wind, and Stars' (1941–1945): The Poems Hid Under Floorboards That Outlived Prison

Korea — How a twenty-four-year-old poet who felt shame at the rustle of a leaf in the wind entrusted nineteen handwritten poems to a mother's porcelain jar before dying in Fukuoka (Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945)) • 20 min read

“Until the day I die, may I look up toward heaven without a single speck of shame. Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, I suffered.” — Yun Dong-ju, Prologue (Seosi, November 20, 1941)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In every public poll conducted across South Korea over the past half-century asking citizens to name the most beloved Korean poet and poem of all time, one name wins by an overwhelming margin: Yun Dong-ju (1917–1945) and his nine-line masterpiece 'Seosi' ('Prologue,' written on November 20, 1941). Refusing to publish in Japanese when the colonial government banned the Korean language in schools and press, twenty-four-year-old Yun hand-bound three manuscript copies of nineteen pure-Hangul poems titled 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem' before leaving Seoul. Arrested in Kyoto in 1943 for Korean independence thought and dying in Fukuoka Prison six months before Liberation, his voice survived because a friend's mother hid one manuscript inside a porcelain jar beneath a wooden maru floor in Gwangyang.

Walk up the pine-scented slope of **Mount Inwangsan** at the top of Buam-dong and Cheongun-dong in Jongno-gu, Seoul, where a decommissioned 1974 concrete municipal water-pressure tank has been converted by architect Lee So-jin into a luminous white courtyard sanctuary: the **Yun Dong-ju Literature House** (윤동주문학관). Inside the first room, displayed under soft light beside a weathered wooden well-head (*자하문 고개 우물*), rests a reproduction of a humble, thread-bound manuscript notebook brushed in fountain pen on squared grid paper (*원고지*, *wongoji*). On its cover are written nine quiet Hangul syllables: **하늘과 바람과 별과 시** (*Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si*—**'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem'**). When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Yun Dong-ju Literature House (Cheongun-dong, Jongno-gu, Seoul) & Gwangyang Manuscript House (37.5921° N, 126.9669° E) to study Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul shaped every decision made during the Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945).

Had that single handwritten bundle of grid paper been discovered by Japanese military police between 1941 and 1945, or rotted in damp soil, modern Korea would have lost its moral compass in verse: **Yun Dong-ju** (윤동주, 尹東柱, December 30, 1917 – February 16, 1945). In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945), the rise of Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') and Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Born in **Myeongdong Village** in North Jiandao (Bukgando, modern Longjing, Jilin Province)—a Christian Korean exile community across the Tumen River where families had established Korean-language schools to resist Japanese rule—Yun Dong-ju grew up alongside his lifelong cousin, friend, and fellow resistance fighter **Song Mong-gyu** (송몽규, born in the same house just three months earlier!). Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') before daybreak or debating the finer points of Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

In the spring of **1938**, twenty-year-old Yun Dong-ju and Song Mong-gyu traveled south to Seoul to enroll in the Literature Department of **Yonhi College** (*연희전문학교*, today’s **Yonsei University** in Sinchon). There, studying under the great Korean linguist **Choe Hyeon-bae** (최현배, who was repeatedly imprisoned by Japanese authorities for defending the Korean *Hangul* alphabet), Yun fell in love with the pure music of native Korean words. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) aligned Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

Yet the years 1938 to 1941 were the darkest midnight of Japanese colonial cultural erasure (*민족말살기*). Under Governor-General Minami Jiro, the regime banned Korean-language instruction in schools, shut down Korean newspapers (*Dong-A Ilbo* and *Chosun Ilbo* in August 1940), forced Koreans to adopt Japanese surnames (*Changssi-gaemyeong*), and pressured prominent older Korean writers to write pro-war propaganda in Japanese. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. While established literary giants compromised under coercion, twenty-three-year-old student Yun Dong-ju and his younger Yonhi roommate **Jung Byeong-uk** (정병욱, 1922–1982, later a renowned professor of classical Korean literature at Seoul National University) rented a small boarding room in **Nusang-dong** (누상동 9번지) at the foot of Mount Inwangsan. Every morning before dawn, the two youths hiked up the granite trails of Inwangsan, washed their faces in cold mountain spring water, and read banned French poetry (Francis Jammes, Rainer Maria Rilke, Paul Valéry) and Korean verse by **Baek Seok**—whose rare 1936 poetry collection *Deer* (*사슴*) Yun loved so deeply that he borrowed a copy from the library and **copied the entire book out by hand** in fountain pen! Across the autumn of **1941**, preparing to graduate early from Yonhi College in December because of wartime mobilization, Yun Dong-ju wrote a sequence of poems in pure *Hangul*—knowing that not a single printer in Korea would dare publish a Korean-language book under colonial censorship. Instead of roaring political slogans, Yun turned his lens inward in agonizing moral self-examination. Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. When temperature, airflow, or human movement shifted around Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem'), the subtle geometry of Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) absorbed the stress smoothly, turning potential environmental hazards into sources of long-term stability.

Look at **'Self-Portrait'** (*자화상*, *Jahwasang*, September 1939): a youth walks out to a lonely village well surrounded by autumn rice paddies and looks down into the dark water. Reflected in the well are the moon, passing clouds, the clear sky, a blue wind, autumn—and a man. At first he hates the helpless man in the water and turns away; walking back, he pities the man; returning to peer into the well again, he misses the man as if he were a lost memory. That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul. Whenever an early prototype or seasonal cycle threatened to derail Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience), the masters refined their approach to Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') and recalibrated Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941), passing hard-won empirical lessons down from mentor to apprentice.

Look at **'Star-Counting Night'** (*별 헤는 밤*, *Byeol Heneun Bam*, November 5, 1941): standing under the late-autumn stars above Yonhi hill, the poet counts one star for memory, one star for love, one star for loneliness, one star for longing, one star for poetry, and one star for **'Mother, Mother'**—calling out the names of schoolgirls from his childhood, poor neighbors, and poets like Francis Jammes and Rilke, before burying his own name under the dark winter sod like a shameful black beetle, trusting that **'when spring comes to my star, green grass will sprout proudly over the hill where my name is buried.'** Primary documentary evidence from the Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945) confirms the extraordinary care devoted to recording these breakthroughs. Fifteen days later, on **November 20, 1941**, Yun wrote the nine lines that every Korean schoolchild and elder knows by heart—**'Seosi'** (서시, 序詩, **'Prologue'**): *'Until the day I die, / may I look up to heaven / without a single speck of shame. / Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, / I suffered. / With a heart that sings the stars, / I shall love all dying things. / And I must walk the path / that has been given to me. / Tonight, again, the stars are brushed by the wind.'* To experience Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. From the tactile grain of Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') beneath the fingers to the rhythmic cadence of Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) echoing across Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul, every detail reinforced a shared sense of civic and spiritual belonging.

Pause over that fourth line: **'Even at the wind rustling in the leaves, I suffered.'** (*잎새에 이는 바람에도 나는 괴로워했다.*) Why did a gentle breeze stirring a leaf cause twenty-four-year-old Yun Dong-ju acute moral anguish? Because in a world where his country was chained and millions were dying in war, he felt that merely breathing quietly under a tree without having yet sacrificed his life for truth was a compromise of conscience! Yet history repeatedly tested the survival of Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) through the crucible of war, regime change, and rapid modernization. When external upheavals swept across Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul, the fragile equilibrium between Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') and Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) faced existential threats that could easily have erased centuries of accumulated knowledge in a single generation.

In December 1941, selecting **nineteen of his finest poems** and titling the collection *Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem*, Yun Dong-ju hand-copied **three identical manuscript booklets**: one for himself, one for his mentor Lee Yang-ha (who begged him not to try publishing it for fear the Japanese police would arrest him on the spot), and **one for his beloved roommate Jung Byeong-uk**. That Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion. Ordinary guardians, local families, and dedicated researchers risked their livelihoods to shelter Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') and preserve the oral and written manuals of Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) until a peaceful era could appreciate their full value once more.

In 1942, crossing to Japan to study English literature at Rikkyo University in Tokyo and Doshisha University in Kyoto (where he wrote his final surviving poem in June 1942, *'Easily Written Poem'*, asking in heartbreak: *'While others lose their freedom, why is a poem written so easily? It is a shameful thing'*), Yun Dong-ju and his cousin Song Mong-gyu were arrested by the Kyoto Tokko (Special Higher Police) on **July 14, 1943**, charged with organizing a Korean student independence movement. Sentenced to two years at **Fukuoka Prison** in Kyushu, the two cousins were subjected to starvation rations and mysterious repeated saline/blood injections. On **February 16, 1945**—just **six months before Korea’s Liberation** on August 15—twenty-seven-year-old Yun Dong-ju died in his cell with a final cry, followed twenty-two days later by Song Mong-gyu in the same prison. How did Yun’s poems survive? Before Jung Byeong-uk was conscripted as a student soldier into the Japanese army in 1944, he handed the handwritten 1941 manuscript of *Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem* to his mother, **Park Cha-jeong**, at their family house (an old brewery house on **Mangdeok Island in Gwangyang, South Jeolla Province**), saying: 'If Dong-ju and I both die in the war, bury this with our bones; if we live, keep it safer than your life.' Jung’s mother wrapped the manuscript in a silk cloth, sealed it inside a **white porcelain jar (*항아리*) filled with dried silkworm cocoons** to absorb moisture, and **hid the jar underneath the wooden *maru* floorboards** of the house! Placed in comparative global perspective, the story of Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) challenges Eurocentric narratives of scientific and artistic history.

When Jung Byeong-uk survived the war and returned to Gwangyang in autumn 1945 to learn that Yun Dong-ju had died in Fukuoka Prison, his mother pried up the floorboards and lifted out the porcelain jar. Together with poet **Kang Cheo-jung** (who had preserved late poems Yun mailed from Tokyo in a photo album), they published the first edition of *Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem* on **January 30, 1948**—fulfilling Yun’s 1941 prophecy that green grass would sprout proudly over the hill where his name was buried.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Poetics of the 'Pure Hangul' Manuscript under Totalitarian Erasure

Literary historians emphasize that writing exclusively in Korean Hangul between 1940 and 1942 was in itself an act of quiet, non-violent civil disobedience. After the colonial regime abolished Korean-language newspapers in August 1940 and enforced Japanese-only literary publication (Kokugo bungaku), Yun Dong-ju's refusal to abandon his mother tongue—refining crystal-clear everyday Korean diction in 'Seosi,' 'Self-Portrait,' and 'Star-Counting Night'—kept the lyrical bloodstream of modern Korean literature alive. Studying the structural interplay between Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) and Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem') within the context of the Late Colonial Dark Period (1917–February 16, 1945) demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint.

2. The Gwangyang Floorboard Jar: How One Mother Saved Korea's Most Beloved Book

The physical survival of Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem (Registered Cultural Heritage No. 712) depended on the courage of Jung Byeong-uk and his mother at 23 Mangdeok-gil in Jinwol-myeon, Gwangyang (Registered Cultural Heritage No. 341). By wrapping Yun's 1941 handwritten wongoji booklet in silk, placing it inside a porcelain jar lined with desiccating silkworm cocoons, and concealing it beneath the elevated maru wooden floorboards from 1943 to 1945, she protected the manuscript from both police raids and coastal humidity. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience) to Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941) across the centuries.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three poetic touchstones in mind: first, Seosi (서시, 'Prologue,' Nov 20, 1941), Yun's nine-line vow to live without a speck of shame before heaven; second, Byeol Heneun Bam (별 헤는 밤, 'Star-Counting Night'), his elegiac roll call of memory, mother, and spring resurrection; and third, Wongoji (원고지), the 200-square manuscript grid paper on which Yun hand-lettered his nineteen poems. Mastering these three Korean terms—Yun Dong-ju (윤동주 1917–1945 Korea's most beloved lyric poet of conscience), Haneul-gwa Baram-gwa Byeol-gwa Si (하늘과 바람과 별과 시 'Sky, Wind, and Stars and Poem'), and Seosi & Byeol Heneun Bam (서시와 별 헤는 밤 'Prologue' and 'Star-Counting Night', Nov 1941)—equips any traveler or reader to decode the deeper cultural grammar embedded in the landscape of Inwangsan and Yonhi in Seoul.
