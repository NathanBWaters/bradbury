Namsan's Five Beacons (Bongsu-je): The Smoke-and-Fire Optical Telegraph That Crossed Korea in Twelve Hours

Korea — How 673 mountaintop stone chimneys relayed border intelligence 600 kilometers from the Tumen River and Tsushima Strait to Seoul before electric wires existed (Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894)) • 20 min read

“By night raise the flame (Bong); by day raise the smoke (Su). Though the border lies two thousand ri away, the King’s eyes see it before dawn.” — Gyeongguk Daejeon (Grand Code for State Administration, 병전 Military Code, 1485)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Today, visitors riding the cable car to the top of Mount Namsan in central Seoul look up at the 236-meter N Seoul Tower—a modern radio, television, and digital communications mast. Yet just below the tower's plaza stand five conical granite chimneys overlooking the capital: the Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (Central Beacon Station). For five hundred years during the Joseon Dynasty until 1894, this mountaintop terrace served as the central CPU of an nationwide optical telegraph network (Bongsu-je) linking 673 mountain peak relay stations along five trunk lines. Using a five-level binary code of daytime smoke plumes and nighttime torches, border dispatches traveled 600 kilometers from the Tumen River to Seoul in twelve hours. Anchored at Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

Stand on the western granite ridge of Mount Namsan (historically called **Mokmyeoksan**, 목멱산) in central Seoul just before sunset. Directly above you rises the futuristic steel needle of the 1975 N Seoul Tower, flashing LED air-quality signals across a megacity of ten million people. Yet right at your feet, perched on a stepped stone platform facing north toward Gyeongbokgung Palace, stand **five beehive-shaped stone chimneys** (*yeonjo*, 연조) built from rough-dressed granite blocks. For five centuries before Guglielmo Marconi invented wireless radio or Samuel Morse tapped a telegraph key, these five stone hearths were the central telecommunications hub of the Korean peninsula. When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Namsan (Mokmyeoksan) Bongsu-dae, Yejang-dong, Jung-gu, Seoul (37.5512° N, 126.9882° E) to study Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul shaped every decision made during the Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894).

This system was the **Bongsu-je** (봉수제, 烽燧制)—a peninsula-wide optical line-of-sight telegraph network codified in the twelfth century during the Goryeo Dynasty (1149 under King Uijong) and perfected in **1419–1446 under King Sejong the Great** and recorded in the *Gyeongguk Daejeon* ('Grand Code for State Administration'). In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894), the rise of Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Even advanced readers of Korean history often overlook the etymological precision of the word **Bongsu** (봉수, 烽燧). It is a compound of two distinct words for two distinct physical media: **Bong** (봉, 烽) means a **raised fire torch** lit at **night** when flames are visible against the dark sky; **Su** (수, 燧) means a **dense column of smoke** sent up during the **day** when sunlight washes out flames against the horizon! Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) before daybreak or debating the finer points of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

Consider the geographical challenge facing the Joseon royal court in Hanyang (Seoul). Along the rugged northern frontier of the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers, Jurchen cavalry could cross the ice in a single hour; along the southern and eastern coastlines of Gyeongsang and Jeolla, *Wako* pirate fleets could materialize out of the sea mist at dawn. By horseback courier (*Pabal*, 파발), riding even with fresh relay horses at every station (*Yeok*), an urgent military dispatch from the Tumen River border in northern Hamgyeong Province (over 2,000 *ri*, or 800 kilometers away) took **six to ten full days** to reach the Ministry of War in Seoul. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) aligned Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

Photons of light, however, travel at 300,000 kilometers per second. By positioning **673 stone beacon stations** (*Bongsu-dae*, 봉수대) on prominent mountain peaks (*jikbong* main stations and *ganbong* auxiliary stations) spaced fifteen to thirty kilometers apart so each peak had an unobstructed line of sight to the next peak in the chain, Joseon military engineers created a relay network that transmitted a signal from the farthest northern or southern border to Namsan in **four to twelve hours**! Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Every raw element gathered near Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul had to be tested for density, moisture response, and structural resilience before it could be incorporated into Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system), ensuring that the interplay between Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) would withstand centuries of monsoon humidity and freezing winter temperatures.

Every single one of those 673 stations funneled into **Five Great Trunk Lines** (*O-daero*, 5대로), corresponding one-to-one with the **five granite chimneys on Namsan** arranged carefully from east to west: **Line 1** (Easternmost Chimney) originated at **Gyeongheung** (*Seosura*) at the mouth of the Tumen River in northeastern Hamgyeong Province, hopping across 120 mountain peaks down the eastern Gangwon coast and into Seoul via Mount Acha (*Achasan*). Closer inspection of the primary mechanism reveals a level of quantitative precision that still astonishes contemporary engineers. By calibrating the tolerances of Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) and balancing the physical forces acting across Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths), the builders eliminated points of mechanical failure while maximizing the functional efficiency of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) under real-world operating conditions.

**Line 2** began at **Dadepo Port in Busan** facing the Korea Strait and Tsushima Island, climbing forty-four main inland peaks through Gyeongsang and Chungcheong to Namsan. **Line 3** started on the northern bank of the Amnok River at **Ganggye** in Pyeongan Province and ran down the inland mountain spine across seventy-eight stations to Mount Muak (*Ansan*) and Namsan. Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. When temperature, airflow, or human movement shifted around Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths), the subtle geometry of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) absorbed the stress smoothly, turning potential environmental hazards into sources of long-term stability.

**Line 4** also began on the northern Amnok frontier at **Uiju**, following the western coastal highway path through seventy-one stations. **Line 5** (Westernmost Chimney) originated at **Yeosu** (Admiral Yi Sun-sin’s naval headquarters in South Jeolla, linked by sea relays even from Jeju Island!), traveling up the western Yellow Sea coast across sixty coastal headlands and islands to Ganghwa and Namsan. That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul. Whenever an early prototype or seasonal cycle threatened to derail Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system), the masters refined their approach to Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and recalibrated O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire), passing hard-won empirical lessons down from mentor to apprentice.

How did a silent puff of smoke or point of fire carry complex military intelligence? In 1422, King Sejong standardized the **O-geo-beop** (오거법, 五擧法, 'Five-Signal Code') across every station in the kingdom. Inside each *Bongsu-dae* fortress stood five separate stone chimneys (*yeonjo*) spaced several meters apart so distant observers with sighting tubes (*mang-tong*) could count the exact number of active plumes without them blurring together: Primary documentary evidence from the Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894) confirms the extraordinary care devoted to recording these breakthroughs. Scholars examining the surviving texts and inscriptions associated with Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) can trace the exact terminology of Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire), verifying that what once seemed like legend was grounded in rigorous institutional documentation.

— **1 Torch / 1 Smoke Plume (평시, Pyeongsi)**: All is peaceful; no enemy in sight (sent every single evening at dusk as a mandatory 'heartbeat ping' confirming that every station in the chain was awake and manned!). To experience Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. From the tactile grain of Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) beneath the fingers to the rhythmic cadence of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) echoing across Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul, every detail reinforced a shared sense of civic and spiritual belonging.

— **2 Torches / 2 Plumes**: Enemy forces or pirate vessels have been spotted lurking near the border or offshore. — **3 Torches / 3 Plumes**: Enemy forces have crossed the border line or approached the immediate coastline. — **4 Torches / 4 Plumes**: Enemy warships have landed troops or breached a border garrison wall. — **5 Torches / 5 Plumes**: Full-scale combat is underway between Joseon forces and the invader. Yet history repeatedly tested the survival of Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) through the crucible of war, regime change, and rapid modernization. When external upheavals swept across Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul, the fragile equilibrium between Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) faced existential threats that could easily have erased centuries of accumulated knowledge in a single generation.

To ensure the daytime smoke column (*su*) rose straight up into the sky without dispersing in strong mountain winds, Joseon chemical manuals prescribed a specialized fuel recipe stored inside dry stone vaults at every peak: dried pine branches, mugwort (*ssuk*), seaweed, grain husks, and most famously **wolf dung** (*nangbun*, 늑대 똥) or dried horse and dog dung mixed with sulfur and saltpeter—which burned with a dense, greasy, jet-black vertical column of smoke visible thirty kilometers away against white clouds! That Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion. Ordinary guardians, local families, and dedicated researchers risked their livelihoods to shelter Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) and preserve the oral and written manuals of O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) until a peaceful era could appreciate their full value once more.

Wait—what happened when heavy monsoon rain, blinding snowstorms, or thick mountain fog blocked visual line-of-sight between two peaks? Joseon military law anticipated bad weather with a multi-modal backup protocol: if a beacon crew could not see the neighboring peak, they immediately fired three booming **Sinpung** (신포, signal gunpowder cannon blasts) or horn blasts; if acoustic signals failed, stationed **Bongsu-gun** (봉수군, beacon soldiers) sprinted on foot or horseback down the mountain ridge path to hand-deliver the signal to the next peak in the chain. Every morning at dawn, the duty officers descending from Namsan delivered the nightly five-line report directly to the **Byeongjo** (병조, Ministry of War) and the Royal Secretariat (*Seungjeongwon*), who presented it to the King at the dawn assembly. Because national survival hung on these signals, the penalty in the *Gyeongguk Daejeon* for abandoning a post, failing to light the nightly peace torch, or falsifying a five-torch alarm was severe flogging or execution. For 475 years under Joseon rule, the five hearths on Namsan never missed a night. Finally, in **1894–1895**, following the introduction of electric copper-wire telegraph lines connecting Incheon, Seoul, Busan, and Uiju and the *Gabo Reform* modernization of the military, the ancient beacon fires were officially extinguished. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Information Theory: The 'Heartbeat Ping' and 5-Bit Optical Encoding

Telecommunications engineers analyzing the Joseon Bongsu-je (봉수제) marvel at its fault-tolerant network protocol. A naive alarm system stays silent until an emergency occurs—which means the central hub never knows whether silence means 'peace' or 'the border station was ambushed and killed.' By requiring every station along all 673 peaks to transmit a daily 1-plume signal (Pyeongsi) at dusk, King Sejong implemented what modern computer networks call a 'keep-alive heartbeat ping': if a chimney stayed dark, the adjacent station immediately knew a node had failed. Studying the structural interplay between Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) and Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) within the context of the Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894) demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint.

2. Combustion Chemistry: Why Wolf Dung and Sulfur Created Wind-Resistant Plumes

Ordinary dry firewood burns with thin, pale gray smoke that quickly dissipates in high-altitude mountain winds. According to Joseon military manuals such as the Mangi Yoram and Sejong Sillok, beacon stations stocked dried mugwort, resinous pine knots, and animal dung (traditionally wolf or horse dung) enriched with sulfur and saltpeter. The high lipid and protein content of dried carnivore dung produced heavy, carbon-rich soot particles that cohered into a dense, dark vertical column capable of rising hundreds of meters before diffusing. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) to O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire) across the centuries.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three terms in mind: first, Bongsu (봉수, 烽燧), combining Bong (nighttime fire torch) and Su (daytime smoke plume); second, Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대), the five-chimney central terminal on Seoul's Namsan where all five national trunk lines converged; and third, O-geo-beop (오거법), the five-tier signal code ranging from one plume (peace) to five plumes (open warfare). Mastering these three Korean terms—Bongsu-je (봉수제 Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system), Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (목멱산 봉수대 Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths), and O-geo-beop (오거법 five-signal binary encoding code of smoke and fire)—equips any traveler or reader to decode the deeper cultural grammar embedded in the landscape of Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul.
