Namsan's Five Beacons (Bongsu-je): The Smoke-and-Fire Optical Telegraph That Crossed Korea in Twelve Hours

Korea — How 673 mountaintop stone chimneys relayed border intelligence 600 kilometers from the Tumen River and Tsushima Strait to Seoul before electric wires existed (Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894)) • 10 min read

“By night raise the flame (Bong); by day raise the smoke (Su). Though the border lies two thousand ri away, the King’s eyes see it before dawn.” — Gyeongguk Daejeon (Grand Code for State Administration, Military Code, 1485)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Today, visitors riding the cable car to the top of Mount Namsan in central Seoul look up at the 236-meter N Seoul Tower—a modern radio, television, and digital communications mast. Yet just below the tower's plaza stand five conical granite chimneys overlooking the capital: the Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (Central Beacon Station). For five hundred years during the Joseon Dynasty until 1894, this mountaintop terrace served as the central CPU of an nationwide optical telegraph network (Bongsu-je) linking 673 mountain peak relay stations along five trunk lines. Using a five-level binary code of daytime smoke plumes and nighttime torches, border dispatches traveled 600 kilometers from the Tumen River to Seoul in twelve hours.

Stand on the western granite ridge of Mount Namsan (historically called Mokmyeoksan) in central Seoul just before sunset. Directly above you rises the futuristic steel needle of the 1975 N Seoul Tower, flashing LED air-quality signals across a megacity of ten million people. Yet right at your feet, perched on a stepped stone platform facing north toward Gyeongbokgung Palace, stand five beehive-shaped stone chimneys (yeonjo) built from rough-dressed granite blocks. For five centuries before Guglielmo Marconi invented wireless radio or Samuel Morse tapped a telegraph key, these five stone hearths were the central telecommunications hub of the Korean peninsula. Bongsu-dae, Yejang-dong, Jung-gu, Seoul to study Bongsu-je (Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Namsan Beacon Station in Seoul shaped every decision made during the Goryeo & Joseon Dynasties (1149–1894).

This system was the Bongsu-je —a peninsula-wide optical line-of-sight telegraph network codified in the twelfth century during the Goryeo Dynasty (1149 under King Uijong) and perfected in 1419–1446 under King Sejong the Great and recorded in the Gyeongguk Daejeon ('Grand Code for State Administration'). Even advanced readers of Korean history often overlook the etymological precision of the word Bongsu. It is a compound of two distinct words for two distinct physical media: Bong means a raised fire torch lit at night when flames are visible against the dark sky; Su means a dense column of smoke sent up during the day when sunlight washes out flames against the horizon!

Consider the geographical challenge facing the Joseon royal court in Hanyang (Seoul). Along the rugged northern frontier of the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers, Jurchen cavalry could cross the ice in a single hour; along the southern and eastern coastlines of Gyeongsang and Jeolla, Wako pirate fleets could materialize out of the sea mist at dawn. By horseback courier (Pabal), riding even with fresh relay horses at every station (Yeok), an urgent military dispatch from the Tumen River border in northern Hamgyeong Province (over 2,000 ri, or 800 kilometers away) took six to ten full days to reach the Ministry of War in Seoul.

Photons of light, however, travel at 300,000 kilometers per second. By positioning 673 stone beacon stations (Bongsu-dae) on prominent mountain peaks (jikbong main stations and ganbong auxiliary stations) spaced fifteen to thirty kilometers apart so each peak had an unobstructed line of sight to the next peak in the chain, Joseon military engineers created a relay network that transmitted a signal from the farthest northern or southern border to Namsan in four to twelve hours! Every single one of those 673 stations funneled into Five Great Trunk Lines (O-daero, 5), corresponding one-to-one with the five granite chimneys on Namsan arranged carefully from east to west: Line 1 (Easternmost Chimney) originated at Gyeongheung (Seosura) at the mouth of the Tumen River in northeastern Hamgyeong Province, hopping across 120 mountain peaks down the eastern Gangwon coast and into Seoul via Mount Acha (Achasan).

Line 2 began at Dadepo Port in Busan facing the Korea Strait and Tsushima Island, climbing forty-four main inland peaks through Gyeongsang and Chungcheong to Namsan. Line 3 started on the northern bank of the Amnok River at Ganggye in Pyeongan Province and ran down the inland mountain spine across seventy-eight stations to Mount Muak (Ansan) and Namsan. Line 4 also began on the northern Amnok frontier at Uiju, following the western coastal highway path through seventy-one stations. Line 5 (Westernmost Chimney) originated at Yeosu (Admiral Yi Sun-sin’s naval headquarters in South Jeolla, linked by sea relays even from Jeju Island!), traveling up the western Yellow Sea coast across sixty coastal headlands and islands to Ganghwa and Namsan.

How did a silent puff of smoke or point of fire carry complex military intelligence? In 1422, King Sejong standardized the O-geo-beop (Five-Signal Code) across every station in the kingdom. — 1 Torch / 1 Smoke Plume (Pyeongsi): All is peaceful; no enemy in sight (sent every single evening at dusk as a mandatory 'heartbeat ping' confirming that every station in the chain was awake and manned!). — 2 Torches / 2 Plumes: Enemy forces or pirate vessels have been spotted lurking near the border or offshore. — 3 Torches / 3 Plumes: Enemy forces have crossed the border line or approached the immediate coastline. — 4 Torches / 4 Plumes: Enemy warships have landed troops or breached a border garrison wall. — 5 Torches / 5 Plumes: Full-scale combat is underway between Joseon forces and the invader.

To ensure the daytime smoke column (su) rose straight up into the sky without dispersing in strong mountain winds, Joseon chemical manuals prescribed a specialized fuel recipe stored inside dry stone vaults at every peak: dried pine branches, mugwort (ssuk), seaweed, grain husks, and most famously wolf dung (nangbun) or dried horse and dog dung mixed with sulfur and saltpeter—which burned with a dense, greasy, jet-black vertical column of smoke visible thirty kilometers away against white clouds! Wait—what happened when heavy monsoon rain, blinding snowstorms, or thick mountain fog blocked visual line-of-sight between two peaks? Joseon military law anticipated bad weather with a multi-modal backup protocol: if a beacon crew could not see the neighboring peak, they immediately fired three booming Sinpung (signal gunpowder cannon blasts) or horn blasts; if acoustic signals failed, stationed Bongsu-gun (beacon soldiers) sprinted on foot or horseback down the mountain ridge path to hand-deliver the signal to the next peak in the chain.

Every morning at dawn, the duty officers descending from Namsan delivered the nightly five-line report directly to the Byeongjo (Ministry of War) and the Royal Secretariat (Seungjeongwon), who presented it to the King at the dawn assembly. Because national survival hung on these signals, the penalty in the Gyeongguk Daejeon for abandoning a post, failing to light the nightly peace torch, or falsifying a five-torch alarm was severe flogging or execution. For 475 years under Joseon rule, the five hearths on Namsan never missed a night. Finally, in 1894–1895, following the introduction of electric copper-wire telegraph lines connecting Incheon, Seoul, Busan, and Uiju and the Gabo Reform modernization of the military, the ancient beacon fires were officially extinguished. In 2023, recognizing the extraordinary preservation of dozens of intact stone Bongsu-dae fortresses along the historic routes, the Korea Heritage Service designated the Five-Route Beacon Network (Joseon Hugi Bongsu-jeuk) as a National Historic Site series.

Stand beside the five stone chimneys of the Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae on Namsan today as the afternoon ceremonial guard in blue and red Joseon military tunics lights a symbolic plume of white smoke beneath the shadow of N Seoul Tower. Look up from the fifteenth-century granite chimney to the twenty-first-century microwave antennas directly overhead: the technology changed from wolf-dung smoke to fiber optics, but the mountain peak watching over Seoul is still the exact same. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Information Theory: The 'Heartbeat Ping' and 5-Bit Optical Encoding

Telecommunications engineers analyzing the Joseon Bongsu-je marvel at its fault-tolerant network protocol. A naive alarm system stays silent until an emergency occurs—which means the central hub never knows whether silence means 'peace' or 'the border station was ambushed and killed.' By requiring every station along all 673 peaks to transmit a daily 1-plume signal (Pyeongsi) at dusk, King Sejong implemented what modern computer networks call a 'keep-alive heartbeat ping': if a chimney stayed dark, the adjacent station immediately knew a node had failed.

2. Combustion Chemistry: Why Wolf Dung and Sulfur Created Wind-Resistant Plumes

Ordinary dry firewood burns with thin, pale gray smoke that quickly dissipates in high-altitude mountain winds. According to Joseon military manuals such as the Mangi Yoram and Sejong Sillok, beacon stations stocked dried mugwort, resinous pine knots, and animal dung (traditionally wolf or horse dung) enriched with sulfur and saltpeter. The high lipid and protein content of dried carnivore dung produced heavy, carbon-rich soot particles that cohered into a dense, dark vertical column capable of rising hundreds of meters before diffusing.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three terms in mind: first, Bongsu, combining Bong (nighttime fire torch) and Su (daytime smoke plume); second, Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae, the five-chimney central terminal on Seoul's Namsan where all five national trunk lines converged; and third, O-geo-beop, the five-tier signal code ranging from one plume (peace) to five plumes (open warfare).

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Hike or ride the cable car up Mount Namsan in Jung-gu, Seoul, where the reconstructed five-hearth Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae stands just below the N Seoul Tower observation plaza (with a daily traditional beacon-lighting ceremony around noon except Mondays). You can also visit the Achasan Bongsu-dae on the eastern ridge of Seoul (Line 1's final relay into Namsan) or the Seongsan Ilchulbong and Dumo-ri beacon sites on Jeju. Bongsu-dae, Yejang-dong, Jung-gu, Seoul transforms abstract chronology into lived physical memory, allowing you to witness Bongsu-je (Joseon five-line optical beacon-fire telegraph system) and Mokmyeoksan Bongsu-dae (Namsan central terminal of five stone hearths) in their authentic geographical setting.
