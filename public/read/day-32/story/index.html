Nightly Short Story • Satire & Wit

The Eyes Have It

Philip K. Dick (1953) • 5 min read

“… his eyes slowly roved about the room.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Here is one of the shortest and silliest pieces Philip K. Dick ever published, and one of the most reprinted. It appeared in a pulp magazine called Science Fiction Stories in 1953, when Dick was in his mid-twenties and turning out stories at a furious pace in Berkeley, California. It is a joke, and it is built on a single trick. A man finds a paperback novel on a bus and reads it with total literal-mindedness, so that every ordinary figure of speech becomes proof of a secret alien invasion. The date matters. Early-1950s America was thick with stories and rumors about hidden infiltrators, from Martians to communists, and the narrator is a perfect portrait of the fearful mind that sees conspiracy everywhere. Listen to how the language itself becomes the villain, and to how a story this small can poke fun at paranoia, at bureaucracy, and at the way science fiction itself was written. And watch for the last line.

It was quite by accident I discovered this incredible invasion of Earth by lifeforms from another planet. As yet, I haven’t done anything about it; I can’t think of anything to do. I wrote to the Government, and they sent back a pamphlet on the repair and maintenance of frame houses. Anyhow, the whole thing is known; I’m not the first to discover it. Maybe it’s even under control.

I was sitting in my easy-chair, idly turning the pages of a paperbacked book someone had left on the bus, when I came across the reference that first put me on the trail. For a moment I didn’t respond. It took some time for the full import to sink in. After I’d comprehended, it seemed odd I hadn’t noticed it right away.

The reference was clearly to a nonhuman species of incredible properties, not indigenous to Earth. A species, I hasten to point out, customarily masquerading as ordinary human beings. Their disguise, however, became transparent in the face of the following observations by the author. It was at once obvious the author knew everything. Knew everything⁠—and was taking it in his stride. The line (and I tremble remembering it even now) read:

… his eyes slowly roved about the room.

Vague chills assailed me. I tried to picture the eyes. Did they roll like dimes? The passage indicated not; they seemed to move through the air, not over the surface. Rather rapidly, apparently. No one in the story was surprised. That’s what tipped me off. No sign of amazement at such an outrageous thing. Later the matter was amplified.

… his eyes moved from person to person.

There it was in a nutshell. The eyes had clearly come apart from the rest of him and were on their own. My heart pounded and my breath choked in my windpipe. I had stumbled on an accidental mention of a totally unfamiliar race. Obviously non-Terrestrial. Yet, to the characters in the book, it was perfectly natural⁠—which suggested they belonged to the same species.

And the author? A slow suspicion burned in my mind. The author was taking it rather too easily in his stride. Evidently, he felt this was quite a usual thing. He made absolutely no attempt to conceal this knowledge. The story continued:

… presently his eyes fastened on Julia.

Julia, being a lady, had at least the breeding to feel indignant. She is described as blushing and knitting her brows angrily. At this, I sighed with relief. They weren’t all non-Terrestrials. The narrative continues:

… slowly, calmly, his eyes examined every inch of her.

Great Scott! But here the girl turned and stomped off and the matter ended. I lay back in my chair gasping with horror. My wife and family regarded me in wonder.

“What’s wrong, dear?” my wife asked.

I couldn’t tell her. Knowledge like this was too much for the ordinary run-of-the-mill person. I had to keep it to myself. “Nothing,” I gasped. I leaped up, snatched the book, and hurried out of the room.

In the garage, I continued reading. There was more. Trembling, I read the next revealing passage:

… he put his arm around Julia. Presently she asked him if he would remove his arm. He immediately did so, with a smile.

It’s not said what was done with the arm after the fellow had removed it. Maybe it was left standing upright in the corner. Maybe it was thrown away. I don’t care. In any case, the full meaning was there, staring me right in the face.

Here was a race of creatures capable of removing portions of their anatomy at will. Eyes, arms⁠—and maybe more. Without batting an eyelash. My knowledge of biology came in handy, at this point. Obviously they were simple beings, unicellular, some sort of primitive single-celled things. Beings no more developed than starfish. Starfish can do the same thing, you know.

I read on. And came to this incredible revelation, tossed off coolly by the author without the faintest tremor:

… outside the movie theater we split up. Part of us went inside, part over to the café for dinner.

Binary fission, obviously. Splitting in half and forming two entities. Probably each lower half went to the café, it being farther, and the upper halves to the movies. I read on, hands shaking. I had really stumbled onto something here. My mind reeled as I made out this passage:

… I’m afraid there’s no doubt about it. Poor Bibney has lost his head again.

Which was followed by:

… and Bob says he has utterly no guts.

Yet Bibney got around as well as the next person. The next person, however, was just as strange. He was soon described as:

… totally lacking in brains.

There was no doubt of the thing in the next passage. Julia, whom I had thought to be the one normal person, reveals herself as also being an alien life form, similar to the rest:

… quite deliberately, Julia had given her heart to the young man.

It didn’t relate what the final disposition of the organ was, but I didn’t really care. It was evident Julia had gone right on living in her usual manner, like all the others in the book. Without heart, arms, eyes, brains, viscera, dividing up in two when the occasion demanded. Without a qualm.

… thereupon she gave him her hand.

I sickened. The rascal now had her hand, as well as her heart. I shudder to think what he’s done with them, by this time.

… he took her arm.

Not content to wait, he had to start dismantling her on his own. Flushing crimson, I slammed the book shut and leaped to my feet. But not in time to escape one last reference to those carefree bits of anatomy whose travels had originally thrown me on the track:

… her eyes followed him all the way down the road and across the meadow.

I rushed from the garage and back inside the warm house, as if the accursed things were following me. My wife and children were playing Monopoly in the kitchen. I joined them and played with frantic fervor, brow feverish, teeth chattering.

I had had enough of the thing. I want to hear no more about it. Let them come on. Let them invade Earth. I don’t want to get mixed up in it.

I have absolutely no stomach for it.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story is a monologue from a man who believes he has discovered an invasion of Earth. He tells us he found a paperback book that someone left on a bus, and that while idly turning the pages he came across a reference that "first put me on the trail." The reference is the sentence "his eyes slowly roved about the room."

The joke is in how he reads it. Ordinary readers know that to say someone's eyes roved is just a way of saying he looked around. The narrator takes it at face value. He pictures the eyes themselves, detached from the head, floating through the air. He notices that nobody in the book seems surprised, and this, he says, "is what tipped me off." The characters must be of the same strange species, and the author must be in on it too.

From that single misreading, he builds an entire theory. "His eyes moved from person to person" proves the eyes are on their own. An arm put around a girl and then "removed" shows that these beings can detach their limbs at will. Someone "lost his head," someone else has "utterly no guts," a third is "totally lacking in brains," and Julia "had given her heart to the young man." The narrator concludes that these creatures are primitive, single-celled things, no more developed than starfish. When the characters say that outside the movie theater "we split up," he decides they reproduce by dividing in two, with the upper halves presumably going to the film and the lower halves to the cafe. Every idiom of ordinary English is turned, line by line, into a clinical observation.

The pleasure is that the reader is always one step ahead. We know what each phrase really means, and we enjoy watching a mind that cannot see it. But there is a second layer. The narrator is not stupid in the usual way. His reasoning is orderly, even scientific in tone. He cites biology and he tracks clues. What is wrong is his starting point, the unshakable belief that something sinister is hidden and that the evidence is staring him in the face. Once you assume that, everything confirms it.

The opening and ending carry the real satire. He wrote to the Government and received a pamphlet on the maintenance of frame houses, which tells us how seriously the authorities treat him. He then says it is possibly "under control" already, a line that shows how readily the paranoid mind accepts that someone else must be in charge. At the end he runs home, joins his family playing Monopoly, and declares that he wants no more to do with it. "I have absolutely no stomach for it." That last sentence is a final pun, and also a final comment on the comedy: the man cannot even end his panic without using the kind of figure of speech that began it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of craft, this is a clever, tightly controlled trick, and the main thing to admire is its discipline. Dick finds one joke and never lets it go. Every example of the narrator's "evidence" is a stock phrase of the sort that fills cheap fiction, and he chooses them with an expert ear: eyes that roved, a heart given away, a head lost, a fellow with no guts. The progression is smart, too. It begins with the most obviously odd one, the roving eyes, builds through arms and splitting in half, and ends on organs that cannot sensibly be given away. The pace is brisk and there is a new twist on nearly every paragraph.

The voice is the second asset. The narrator is pompous and breathless in just the right mixture. He announces that he "hasten[s] to point out" certain details, he says he trembles at the memory of a sentence, he congratulates himself on his knowledge of biology. The staid, scientific diction clashes beautifully with the absurd content. Dick does not wink at us. He lets the narrator be entirely serious, and that is where the comedy comes from.

There are also weaknesses, and a fair assessment has to admit them. The piece is one idea, stretched. After the first several examples the pattern is clear, and a reader may feel the later ones are only repetition with different body parts. Some of the jokes are slightly strained, such as the business about which half of a person goes to the cafe, and the wordplay can feel more like a list than a story. There is no real plot and no character other than the narrator. The wife and children are shadows who exist to be kept in the dark.

The satire is also fairly gentle. The narrator is harmless, so the story never has to face what real paranoia can do. A sharper writer could have shown a man whose delusion hurts someone. Dick prefers to keep it light, which is entirely his right, but it means the piece is amusing rather than deep.

There is also an issue of dating. The comedy depends on the reader's knowing the set phrases of pulp romance and adventure. Most people still know these expressions, but the sort of paperback that would be full of them is a little remote now, and some of the references, like the family gathered around Monopoly, mark the story as a period piece. That said, the piece is short enough that none of this does much damage. It is a sketch, and a sketch is judged by whether it makes you laugh. For most readers, this one does, and it does so more reliably than many longer comic stories from the same magazines.

III. The Impact It Had

It is difficult to measure the impact of a very short comic story, but a few things can be said with confidence. First, it is one of the pieces by which many readers first meet Dick's humor. He is mostly known for his dark and anxious novels, and a reader who comes to him through the movies based on his work may be surprised to find that he could be genuinely funny. This story is a standard example of that other side, and it appears in many collections of his short fiction.

Second, it is a fixture in discussions of science fiction humor. Because it is so short and so easy to grasp, it has been used in classrooms to illustrate several things at once: how language can mislead, how point of view shapes what we see, and how a genre can parody itself. A teacher can read it aloud in three minutes and have a discussion ready. That teaching life is probably its most lasting practical influence.

Third, the story fits into a long tradition of jokes about literal-mindedness. Comedy built on taking figures of speech at face value was not new, and it has remained popular, from children's books about a housekeeper who does exactly what her instructions say, to sketches and sitcom routines in which a character misunderstands an idiom. It would be wrong to claim that this story caused any of those. But it is an early and unusually pure example of the device applied to a science fiction premise, and it has the advantage of an actual argument underneath the gag.

Fourth, the story shows early signs of themes that would grow in Dick's work. The unreliable narrator whose version of reality is cracked, but who is entirely sincere, is central to his later novels. The sense that a hidden truth lies behind ordinary appearances, and that no one in authority will help, is the engine of countless Dick plots. Here it is treated as farce, but the structure of the thought is the same, and it is striking how easily the farce turns serious when later stories adopt the same shape. In the decades after it appeared, as Dick's reputation grew, readers went back to early pieces like this one and found in them a miniature version of his concerns.

There is little documented about how the story was received at the time, beyond the fact that it was published and has since been reprinted often. Like most of his early magazine work, it was a modest piece in a modest venue. Its reputation has grown slowly, through anthologies and collections, not through any single famous event.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers love most about this story is how quickly it makes them feel clever. Almost everyone gets the joke within the first two paragraphs, and then the pleasure becomes one of anticipation. Each new phrase, such as "lost his head" or "gave her his heart", is a small test. You see it coming, and then you watch the narrator misread it in exactly the way you expected, and that confirmation feels good. A story that lets the reader be one step ahead and then rewards that foresight is a very satisfying thing.

People also enjoy the narrator's voice. He is absurd, but he is also oddly lovable. He is a husband and father who gets frightened, hides in the garage with the book, and then rushes back to the warm kitchen to play a board game with his family. That image, the man shaking with fear over a paperback and gripping a Monopoly piece "with frantic fervor," is the human heart of the story. It would be easy to dislike a character who sees monsters everywhere, but Dick makes him a figure of fun rather than scorn. We laugh at him and also recognize a little of ourselves in him, because everyone has at some point jumped to a ridiculous conclusion and then dug in.

The wordplay is another joy. Readers who love language tend to love this story, because it shows how rich and strange ordinary speech is. We say that people lose their heads, split up, or give their hearts away without ever thinking about what we are saying. The story makes us hear our own language as a foreigner might, and that is a kind of delight. It reminds us that English is full of dead metaphors that only seem normal because we have forgotten how weird they are.

Science fiction fans in particular tend to enjoy the way the story pokes at their own genre. Pulp magazines of the period were full of tales of hidden invasions and secret alien infiltrators, and the narrator's solemn deductions read like a parody of those stories. The deadpan treatment, in which the narrator treats his discovery as grave and cosmic, is exactly the tone that many real stories of the time adopted without irony. Fans who know the type appreciate being let in on the joke.

And finally, readers like the economy. It takes only a few minutes. Nothing is wasted, and the closing pun gives the whole thing a neat snap. A story that knows exactly how long it should be is rarer than it seems.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

There is little record of serious public criticism of this story, which is not surprising for a light comic piece in a pulp magazine. It was not the kind of work that attracted reviews in newspapers or provoked debate. So the objections that follow are mostly the sort that later readers and critics make when they discuss Dick's early work, and they are mild.

The most obvious complaint is that the story is slight. It is a single gag developed over a page or two. Readers who go to Dick for the large, disorienting visions of his novels may feel that this is a throwaway, amusing but of little consequence. Critics who take a stern view of his short fiction sometimes treat pieces like this as exercises, evidence of a young writer's wit and facility but not of his depth. There is a reasonable case for that view. Nothing in the story is at stake. No one is changed, and there is no moment of feeling.

A second objection concerns repetition. The humor is based on a pattern, and patterns wear thin. Some readers feel the narrator's catalog of body parts goes on a few examples longer than it needs to, and that the later examples are weaker than the early ones. A tighter version might have stopped sooner or varied the structure.

A third is about the target. The story makes fun of paranoia, but it does so in a way that is safe. The narrator is an obvious fool, and no one is threatened by his delusion, so the satire has no teeth. Compared with real-world paranoia in the early 1950s, which ruined careers and lives, this is very mild stuff. A reader who wants satire to bite may be disappointed. Similarly, someone might say the story fits a condescending habit of mocking the ordinary reader, the man in his easy chair with a paperback, as a gullible dolt. That is a stretch, since the narrator is so clearly a comic invention, but it is the kind of objection that can be raised.

Finally, some readers have noted that the joke has become easier to anticipate over time. Literalist humor is now so common that the story can feel familiar even on first reading. That is a sign of how successful the device became, but it means the story has less of the freshness it once had. None of this is a serious indictment. It simply places the story honestly: it is a good joke, well told, and a good joke does not need to carry more weight than that.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The simplest way to place this story is as a parody. Science fiction in the 1950s was awash in tales of secret alien invasions, stories in which ordinary people were not what they seemed and a lone hero discovered the truth. The cultural moment encouraged them. The Cold War, the fear of hidden communist infiltrators, and a general sense that enemies might look just like neighbors fed a whole genre of paranoid fiction. The most famous examples came a little later, such as Jack Finney's novel The Body Snatchers, serialized in 1954, which became the classic film Invasion of the Body Snatchers. Dick's story is a comic cousin of these works. It takes their central premise, that the invaders are among us and that only one observant person has noticed, and shows what that premise looks like from the inside of a mind that wants it to be true.

The story also belongs to a tradition of wordplay and literal-mindedness in comedy. Writers from Lewis Carroll onward have enjoyed taking language at its word, and the technique has been used by humorists in every period. In science fiction, a number of short-story writers of the same era, such as Fredric Brown and Robert Sheckley, specialized in brief, witty pieces built on a single twist. Dick's story is among the best of that family. Later comic science fiction, including the work of Douglas Adams, shares its delight in what happens when strange ideas are treated with a straight face.

There is also a faint echo of something larger. The story is, in a way, about reading itself. The narrator is a reader who misreads, and the story asks how much the meaning of any text depends on what the reader brings to it. That concern with interpretation, and with the gap between what is written and what is understood, connects the piece to broader literary themes, and in particular to the long tradition of unreliable narrators, from Poe's raving murderers to the self-deceiving voices of modern fiction. Dick's narrator is a lightweight member of that family, but he belongs to it.

Looking at Dick's own career, the story sits at the lighter end of the early work. It lacks the grandeur of the novels, but it exhibits the same suspicion that reality is not what it appears and that the person describing it cannot be trusted. In his later fiction that suspicion would turn dark and personal. Here it is purely comic.

As for why to read it now, the answer is easy. It takes less than five minutes, it is genuinely funny, and it carries a small lasting lesson. In an age when every conspiracy theory comes with its own set of confident clues, a story about a man who assembles a grand theory out of misread phrases feels remarkably current. The story reminds us that a pattern found by someone determined to find it is not the same as a truth.
