Willis Polk's 1918 Hallidie Building at 130 Sutter Street: The World's First Glass Curtain Wall

San Francisco — How San Francisco's flamboyant master builder cantilevered a seven-story sheet of glass three feet ahead of a concrete frame—and crowned it in University of California blue and gold (World War I Architectural Modernism (1917–1918)) • 20 min read

“Why disguise a modern concrete frame behind fake Renaissance stones when you can hang a curtain of pure California daylight across the street?” — Willis Jefferson Polk (1918)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk down the narrow financial canyon of Sutter Street between Montgomery and Kearny streets to 130 Sutter Street (37.7897° N, 122.4033° W) and look up at one of the most audacious leaps in world architectural history: Willis Jefferson Polk's 1918 Hallidie Building. Completed thirty-four years before New York's Lever House, the seven-story office building—developed by the Regents of the University of California and named for UC Regent and cable-car inventor Andrew Smith Hallidie—features the first large-scale glass curtain wall in the Western Hemisphere: a shimmering, non-load-bearing grid of 2,500 glass panes cantilevered three feet out in front of its reinforced-concrete structural columns and framed by delicate Gothic iron balconies painted in UC Berkeley's official blue and gold.

Standing today at 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District in the heart of the Sutter Street Financial and Gallery District (37.7897° N, 122.4033° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of World War I Architectural Modernism (1917–1918). By 1917, fifty-year-old architect Willis Jefferson Polk was already a legend of San Francisco's post-1906 reconstruction: a fiery, witty veteran of A. Page Brown's and Daniel Burnham's offices who had rebuilt the Chronicle Building, the Pacific-Union Club atop Nob Hill, the neoclassical Sunol Water Temple, and the D. O. Mills Bank. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how architect Willis Jefferson Polk (1867–1924) and the Regents of the University of California (honoring Andrew Smith Hallidie) and a generation of San Franciscans confronted maximizing natural daylight inside a deep narrow urban office lot during wartime steel shortages by suspending an all-glass skin three feet beyond the concrete columns, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the 1918 Hallidie Building (San Francisco Designated Landmark #37, Home of AIA San Francisco) emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Sutter Street Financial and Gallery District. In early 1917, the Regents of the University of California approached Polk with a strictly pragmatic real-estate problem: the university owned a mid-block parcel at 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets as an endowment investment, and needed a seven-story speculative commercial building erected quickly and cheaply on a tight $275,000 budget. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during March 1918 (completion and opening of the Hallidie Building at 130 Sutter Street). The Regents decided to name the building in honor of Andrew Smith Hallidie (1836–1900), the Scottish-born wire-rope manufacturer who had not only invented the Clay Street Hill cable car in 1873 but had also served for thirty-two years as chairman of the Finance Committee of the UC Board of Regents. Contemporary observers who walked past 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood architect Willis Jefferson Polk (1867–1924) and the Regents of the University of California (honoring Andrew Smith Hallidie), whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of cantilevered reinforced-concrete slab engineering, non-load-bearing steel-mullion glass curtain walls, and ornamental pressed-metal fire escapes proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Polk faced two immediate engineering constraints in 1917: first, America's entry into World War I had diverted structural rolled steel I-beams to naval shipyards, forcing Polk to frame the building in reinforced concrete; second, 130 Sutter Street was hemmed in on both sides by tall masonry buildings, meaning deep interior floors would be dark and unrentable unless the street facade admitted maximum daylight. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of cantilevered reinforced-concrete slab engineering, non-load-bearing steel-mullion glass curtain walls, and ornamental pressed-metal fire escapes imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the 1918 Hallidie Building (San Francisco Designated Landmark #37, Home of AIA San Francisco). Instead of punching conventional windows into a heavy masonry wall seated on the perimeter columns, Polk and his structural engineers executed a brilliant tectonic inversion: they set the line of reinforced-concrete supporting columns three feet *back* from the Sutter Street property line and cantilevered the concrete floor slabs outward toward the sidewalk. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through March 1918 (completion and opening of the Hallidie Building at 130 Sutter Street), the surrounding streets of the Sutter Street Financial and Gallery District became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. From the outer lips of those cantilevered concrete floor slabs, Polk hung a continuous, two-dimensional vertical screen of steel mullions and plate glass stretching across the entire 100-foot width and seven-story height of the facade—creating a true 'curtain wall' that carried zero structural floor load and turned 100 percent of the street wall into a collector of northern sky light. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Because the glass curtain hung three feet clear in front of the octagonal reinforced-concrete columns, office workers inside 130 Sutter Street enjoyed uninterrupted bands of daylight without dark masonry piers casting shadows across drafting tables or bookkeeping ledgers. A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District. Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the 1918 Hallidie Building (San Francisco Designated Landmark #37, Home of AIA San Francisco), the day-to-day craft of cantilevered reinforced-concrete slab engineering, non-load-bearing steel-mullion glass curtain walls, and ornamental pressed-metal fire escapes produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the Sutter Street Financial and Gallery District. The human texture of the 1918 Hallidie Building (San Francisco Designated Landmark #37, Home of AIA San Francisco) was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets in the Financial District.
