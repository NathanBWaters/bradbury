Willis Polk's 1918 Hallidie Building at 130 Sutter Street: The World's First Glass Curtain Wall

San Francisco — How San Francisco's flamboyant master builder cantilevered a seven-story sheet of glass three feet ahead of a concrete frame—and crowned it in University of California blue and gold (World War I Architectural Modernism (1917–1918)) • 7 min read

“Why disguise a modern concrete frame behind fake Renaissance stones when you can hang a curtain of pure California daylight across the street?” — Willis Jefferson Polk (1918)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk down the narrow financial canyon of Sutter Street between Montgomery and Kearny streets to 130 Sutter Street and look up at one of the most audacious leaps in world architectural history: Willis Jefferson Polk's 1918 Hallidie Building. Completed thirty-four years before New York's Lever House, the seven-story office building—developed by the Regents of the University of California and named for UC Regent and cable-car inventor Andrew Smith Hallidie—features the first large-scale glass curtain wall in the Western Hemisphere: a shimmering, non-load-bearing grid of 2,500 glass panes cantilevered three feet out in front of its reinforced-concrete structural columns and framed by delicate Gothic iron balconies painted in UC Berkeley's official blue and gold.

By 1917, fifty-year-old architect Willis Jefferson Polk was already a legend of San Francisco's post-1906 reconstruction: a fiery, witty veteran of A. Page Brown's and Daniel Burnham's offices who had rebuilt the Chronicle Building, the Pacific-Union Club atop Nob Hill, the neoclassical Sunol Water Temple, and the D. O. Mills Bank. In early 1917, the Regents of the University of California approached Polk with a strictly pragmatic real-estate problem: the university owned a mid-block parcel at 130 Sutter Street between Kearny and Montgomery streets as an endowment investment, and needed a seven-story speculative commercial building erected quickly and cheaply on a tight $275,000 budget.

The Regents decided to name the building in honor of Andrew Smith Hallidie (1836–1900), the Scottish-born wire-rope manufacturer who had not only invented the Clay Street Hill cable car in 1873 but had also served for thirty-two years as chairman of the Finance Committee of the UC Board of Regents. Polk faced two immediate engineering constraints in 1917: first, America's entry into World War I had diverted structural rolled steel I-beams to naval shipyards, forcing Polk to frame the building in reinforced concrete; second, 130 Sutter Street was hemmed in on both sides by tall masonry buildings, meaning deep interior floors would be dark and unrentable unless the street facade admitted maximum daylight.

Instead of punching conventional windows into a heavy masonry wall seated on the perimeter columns, Polk and his structural engineers executed a brilliant tectonic inversion: they set the line of reinforced-concrete supporting columns three feet back from the Sutter Street property line and cantilevered the concrete floor slabs outward toward the sidewalk. From the outer lips of those cantilevered concrete floor slabs, Polk hung a continuous, two-dimensional vertical screen of steel mullions and plate glass stretching across the entire 100-foot width and seven-story height of the facade—creating a true 'curtain wall' that carried zero structural floor load and turned 100 percent of the street wall into a collector of northern sky light.

Because the glass curtain hung three feet clear in front of the octagonal reinforced-concrete columns, office workers inside 130 Sutter Street enjoyed uninterrupted bands of daylight without dark masonry piers casting shadows across drafting tables or bookkeeping ledgers. To ventilate the offices and allow window washers to clean the exterior without scaffolding, Polk engineered the steel window frames so that every single pane of glass could pivot horizontally on central trunnions—allowing the entire seven-story facade to open in unison on warm San Francisco afternoons. Yet Polk was too deeply steeped in the Beaux-Arts tradition of urban ornament to leave the glass grid completely bare, and the San Francisco Fire Marshal mandated that the building include two exterior iron fire-escape stairways along the Sutter Street frontage.

Rather than bolting ugly utilitarian zigzag fire escapes across his pristine glass wall, Polk integrated the fire escapes into the aesthetic frame of the building: flanking both edges of the facade with symmetrical filigree cast-iron balconies and delicate Venetian Gothic friezes at the cornice and mezzanine lines. In a playful tribute to his client, the Regents of the University of California, Polk ordered the ornamental cast-iron spandrels, Gothic cornice lacework, and twin exterior fire-escape balconies painted in UC Berkeley's official colors—royal blue and California gold. When the scaffolding came down in March 1918, passersby on Sutter Street stopped in astonishment: in an era when every surrounding office building wore heavy rusticated granite or terra-cotta classical columns, the Hallidie Building looked like a weightless waterfall of clear glass and gilded iron lace.

Decades ahead of its time—preceding Walter Gropius's 1926 Bauhaus workshop wall in Dessau by eight years and Skidmore, Owings & Merrill's 1952 Lever House in Manhattan by thirty-four years—the Hallidie Building demonstrated the complete separation of structural skeleton and glass skin that would define twentieth-century skyscrapers. Over the middle decades of the twentieth century, neglect and misguided alterations took a toll on Polk's masterpiece: the ground-floor cast-iron store frieze was ripped out in the 1950s, rust corroded the thin steel window mullions as water seeped behind the spandrels, and falling pieces of cast-iron ornament forced owners in the 1990s to shroud the cornice in protective netting.

In 1971, the San Francisco Board of Supervisors designated the Hallidie Building as City Landmark #37, and between 2011 and 2013 a meticulous multi-million-dollar historic restoration by local architects and engineers repaired every pivoting steel casement sash, recast missing cast-iron Gothic filigree, and restored Polk's original 1918 UC blue-and-gold color palette. Fittingly, the ground floor and mezzanine of 130 Sutter Street today house the headquarters and public gallery of the San Francisco chapter of the American Institute of Architects (AIA San Francisco) and the Center for Architecture + Design—inviting visitors to step behind the world's first glass curtain wall.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Tectonic Anatomy of a True Curtain Wall (1918)

What makes Willis Polk's 1918 Hallidie Building a true modern curtain wall rather than just a building with big windows, like Chicago's 1890s Reliance Building? In Chicago school frame buildings, the windows sat between the perimeter structural columns and spandrel beams. At 130 Sutter Street, Polk pulled the reinforced-concrete structural columns three feet back inside the building and suspended a continuous steel-and-glass plane in front of the floor slabs—meaning the exterior wall hangs like a curtain and supports only its own dead weight.

2. Merging Beaux-Arts Filigree with Industrial Daylight

Why did Willis Polk wrap an ultra-modern glass skin in Venetian Gothic cast-iron balconies painted blue and gold? Polk belonged to a transitional generation that saw no contradiction between structural radicalism and civic ornament. By transforming the code-mandated exterior fire escapes and roofline cornice into lace-like pressed metal and cast iron painted in the University of California's school colors, he gave the flat glass plane depth, shadow, and human scale on a narrow Financial District street.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this architectural story with three facts: first, the building's namesake Andrew Smith Hallidie (inventor of the cable car and longtime UC Regent); second, the three-foot setback between the interior octagonal concrete columns and the suspended glass curtain wall; and third, the 2011–2013 historic restoration that returned the building's ironwork to its authentic 1918 UC Berkeley blue-and-gold livery.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk to 130 Sutter Street between Montgomery and Kearny streets and stand first across the street on the south sidewalk of Sutter to admire the full seven-story glass grid and blue-and-gold Gothic fire escapes. Then cross the street and step inside the ground-floor gallery of the Center for Architecture + Design (AIA San Francisco) to inspect how the concrete columns stand three feet behind the glass skin.
