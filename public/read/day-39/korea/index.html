Kim Jeong-ho's Daedongyeojido (1861): The 22-Tier Woodblock Map That Put Korea in a Traveler's Pocket

Korea — How a self-taught geographer carved 126 linden-wood blocks into a 6.7-meter collapsible accordion atlas with 10-ri road ticks and standardized symbols (Late Joseon Dynasty (1834–1861)) • 9 min read

“When the world is in turmoil, a map guides the defense of the nation; when the world is at peace, it guides governance and the footsteps of the people.” — Kim Jeong-ho, Map Preface (1861)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Unfold all twenty-two horizontal tiers of the Daedongyeojido ('Territorial Map of the Great East,' Treasure No. 850) side by side on a museum floor, and you stand before a monumental cartographic tapestry of the Korean peninsula measuring 6.7 meters tall and 3.8 meters wide at a scale of roughly 1:162,000. Completed and printed from 126 double-sided linden-wood blocks in 1861 by self-taught geographer Kim Jeong-ho (pen name Gosanja, c. 1804–1866), the map revolutionized Korean cartography not only through its mathematical topographic precision, but by slicing the peninsula into twenty-two portable folding accordion booklets stamped with standardized icons and ten-ri (4-kilometer) road ticks so any merchant or traveler could carry their province in a coat sleeve.

Stand before the towering two-story display wall in the History Gallery of the National Museum of Korea in Yongsan, where all twenty-two horizontal tiers (cheop) of the 1861 Daedongyeojido are assembled into a single unified map of the Korean peninsula. Measuring 6.7 meters from Mount Baekdu at the top to Jeju Island at the bottom and 3.8 meters wide, its bold black woodblock lines trace the unbroken mountain spine of the Baekdudaegan like the branching roots of a giant oak while meandering rivers and dotted highways weave through 330 counties. Hold an original 1861 woodblock print of the Daedongyeojido side by side with a twenty-first-century NASA Landsat satellite photograph of the Korean peninsula, and the coastline contours, river confluences, and island positions match with a geometric fidelity that leaves modern GIS cartographers speechless.

The creator of this masterpiece was Kim Jeong-ho (c. 1804–1866), known by his pen name Gosanja (Man of the Old Mountain). Born of humble commoner or jungin (technical middle-class) status in Hwanghae Province and living in a small house outside Namdaemun near Mapo in Seoul, Kim Jeong-ho held no royal cabinet rank. Yet in his twenties he became consumed by a lifelong obsession: why were all of Joseon’s finest maps locked away inside the Royal Palace and the Ministry of War as handwritten silk scrolls that ordinary scholars, merchants, and local magistrates could never afford to copy or carry?

Paired with his close friend and patron—the brilliant Silhak polymath and astronomical cartographer Choe Han-gi (1803–1877)—Kim Jeong-ho spent thirty years cross-referencing every historical map compiled in Korea since the fifteenth century: from Jeong Cheok and Yang Seong-ji’s 1463 Dongguk Jido and Jeong Sang-gi’s eighteenth-century Dongguk Daejido (which introduced the 100-ri grid scale) to royal border surveys, Sejong Sillok Jiriji gazetteers, and astronomical latitude measurements. In 1834, Kim Jeong-ho completed his first monumental manuscript atlas, the Cheonggudo ('Map of the Green Hills,' in two massive hand-drawn volumes dividing the nation into a longitudinal and latitudinal grid), paired with his lifetime geographical encyclopedia, the Daedong Jiji (32 volumes). Yet Kim realized that a hand-copied book atlas still suffered from two flaws: when a reader opened a page of the Cheonggudo, they could not see how a mountain range or river continued into the adjacent county, and copying by hand introduced scribe errors and kept the price out of reach of commoners.

For the next twenty-seven years—through the 1850s into 1861—Kim Jeong-ho redesigned the map from scratch into the Daedongyeojido. First, how did he make a 6.7-meter-tall wall map portable enough for a traveler walking on foot? He invented a modular 22-tier folding accordion system (buncheop-jeolcheopsik)! Kim sliced the Korean peninsula horizontally from north to south into twenty-two latitudinal strips (each strip representing 120 ri north-to-south, or roughly 47 kilometers). Each of those twenty-two strips was printed on sheets that folded sideways like an accordion into a compact booklet measuring just 20 centimeters wide by 30 centimeters tall—the exact size of a modern tablet computer! A merchant traveling from Seoul to Busan did not need to haul a twenty-foot scroll; he simply slipped Tier 13 through Tier 19 into his saddlebag, unfolding them along the road and joining them edge-to-edge on an inn floor whenever he wanted to see the whole country!

Second, to eliminate the hours it took scribes to write out long place-name descriptions on a map, Kim Jeong-ho printed a standardized cartographic legend (Jido-pyo) on Tier 1 using twenty-two geometric symbols: distinct visual icons for walled fortresses, un-walled county seats, naval garrisons, army camps, post-horse relay stations, granaries, mountaintop beacon stations, royal tombs, and historic battlefields. One glance at a symbol told the reader the exact administrative and military infrastructure of a town! Third—and most beloved by nineteenth-century travelers—Kim Jeong-ho engraved tiny dots every ten ri (roughly 4 kilometers, or one hour’s brisk walk) along every single highway and mountain road across the entire peninsula: the Sip-ri-jeom ! Because mountainous roads twist and climb up switchbacks, measuring straight-line 'crow-flies' distance with a ruler on a flat map is useless to a hiker. With Kim’s ten-ri dots, a traveler going from Hanyang to Andong simply counted the dots along the road line with a fingernail to know the exact walking distance and where they would reach an inn by nightfall!

Fourth, look at how Kim Jeong-ho depicted mountains and rivers. Rather than drawing individual picturesque mountain peaks like landscape paintings, he rendered mountain ranges as continuous, interconnected black caterpillar-like ridges whose thickness corresponded to the height and watershed importance of the ridge—visually proving the traditional Korean geographical philosophy of San-ja Su-myeong (: 'Mountains never cross water, and water never cuts through a mountain spine,' so every ridge defines a natural river basin!). Even more remarkably, Kim Jeong-ho distinguished between navigable river channels (drawn as double lines, indicating where cargo boats and grain barges could sail upstream!) and shallow upper mountain streams (drawn as single lines, where boats could not pass).

To make the Daedongyeojido affordable through mass printing instead of expensive hand-painting, Kim Jeong-ho and his workshop personally gouged and chiseled 126 seasoned linden-wood blocks (pinamu, Tilia amurensis) on both sides (60 to 70 blocks, engraved front and back). Every tiny ten-ri dot, every beacon-station triangle, and over 11,000 Korean place names had to be carved in mirror-image reverse into the hardwood without a single slip of the chisel, printing the first edition in 1861 (Sin-yu-bon) and a corrected second printing in 1864 (Gap-ja-bon). For decades during the Japanese colonial period, textbooks spread a tragic melodramatic myth: that the conservative Joseon regent Heungseon Daewongun arrested Kim Jeong-ho for leaking state military secrets on his map, burned all the woodblocks, and executed him in prison. Modern Korean historical archives proved that colonial story completely false!

Not only did Joseon court ministers and royal offices eagerly purchase and use the Daedongyeojido (and eleven of Kim’s original 1861 carved linden-wood blocks still survive today as Treasure No. 1581 in the National Museum of Korea and Soongsil University!), the colonial Governor-General’s office fabricated the 'persecuted genius' legend to portray the Joseon Dynasty as anti-scientific. In 2023, a pristine 1864 Sin-yu/Gap-ja woodblock edition of the Daedongyeojido enriched with handwritten annotations from the Dongyeodo was repatriated from Japan to South Korea, where conservators displayed its twenty-two tiers in full glory. Trace your finger today across Tier 13 of the Daedongyeojido where the double-lined Han River curves past Mapo Naru and the ten-ri dots march south over Namtaeryeong Pass. Every carved wooden dot is a footstep of Gosanja Kim Jeong-ho—the man who shrank three thousand ri of mountains and rivers into twenty-two folding books so the land would belong to everyone who walked it.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Cartographic Information Design: The 22-Symbol 'Jido-pyo' & 'Sip-ri-jeom' Road Ticks

From an information-visualization perspective, Kim Jeong-ho's 1861 Daedongyeojido anticipated modern GIS layering and road-network topology. By moving administrative descriptions into a 22-symbol geometric legend (Jido-pyo) and encoding non-linear road travel distance directly onto the path lines via 10-ri (~4 km) tick dots (Sip-ri-jeom), Kim solved the projection distortion problem of mountainous terrain: even where a winding mountain pass was compressed visually on the flat woodblock, the dot count preserved true travel time.

2. Debunking the Colonial Myth: The Surviving Woodblocks (Treasure No. 1581)

Historians led by Professor Bae Woo-sung and Lee Sang-tae dismantled the twentieth-century colonial textbook myth that the Joseon court burned Kim Jeong-ho's woodblocks and executed him for treason. Archival records show Kim collaborated closely with high-ranking court officials like Choe Han-gi and Shin Heon, and twelve original double-sided linden-wood printing blocks (Treasure No. 1581) survived intact in Korean collections—proving the map was celebrated rather than suppressed.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three cartographic terms in mind: first, Daedongyeojido ('Territorial Map of the Great East,' 1861), Kim Jeong-ho's 22-tier collapsible woodblock atlas; second, Buncheop-jeolcheopsik, the modular accordion-folding book system that made a 6.7-meter wall map pocket-portable; and third, Sip-ri-jeom, the 10-ri (~4 km) distance dots engraved along every highway.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit the Joseon Cartography Room on the 1st Floor of the National Museum of Korea in Yongsan-gu, Seoul, where both a full 6.7-meter assembled display of the 22 tiers of the Daedongyeojido and the original carved linden-wood printing blocks (Treasure No. 1581) are exhibited. You can also visit the National Geographic Information Institute Map Museum in Suwon.
