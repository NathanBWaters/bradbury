Jimbo's Bop City and Marcus Books: When the Postwar Fillmore Was the 'Harlem of the West'

San Francisco — How Charles 'Jimbo' Edwards's 2:00 a.m. backroom jam sessions at 1690 Post Street hosted Charlie Parker, Billie Holiday, and John Coltrane—and how the 1894 Victorian survived moving down Fillmore Street (Post-WWII Bebop Golden Age (1945–1965) to Urban Renewal & Preservation) • 8 min read

“After the downtown clubs closed at two in the morning, every jazz giant in the world walked up the stairs of Jimbo’s Bop City on Post Street; if you couldn’t play your horn, they’d tell you to sit down.” — Pony Poindexter (1985)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stand before the ornate 1894 Queen Anne Victorian at 1712 Fillmore Street between Post andutter streets—San Francisco City Landmark #241—and you are looking at the physical heart of the postwar 'Harlem of the West.' Originally built at 1690 Post Street and rolled around the corner onto Fillmore Street in 1979 to escape the bulldozers of the San Francisco Redevelopment Agency, this building housed Charles 'Jimbo' Edwards's legendary Waffle Shop and backroom after-hours jazz sanctuary, Jimbo's Bop City, from 1950 to 1965—where Billie Holiday, Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, John Coltrane, Ella Fitzgerald, Duke Ellington, and Sammy Davis Jr. jammed from 2:00 a.m. until dawn—before serving for nearly four decades as the home of Marcus Books, the nation's oldest independent Black bookstore.

Before World War II, the blocks around Post, Buchanan, and Fillmore streets in the Western Addition formed the vibrant center of San Francisco's Japantown (Nihonmachi), nestled inside a collection of late-nineteenth-century Queen Anne and Stick-style Victorians that had survived the April 1906 fire because firefighters dynamited a firebreak along Van Ness Avenue. In the spring of 1942, after President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 forcibly removing and incarcerating 5,000 Japanese American residents of the Western Addition into wartime internment camps, tens of thousands of African American workers from Texas, Louisiana, Oklahoma, and Arkansas arrived in the Bay Area during the Second Great Migration to build Liberty ships at the Hunters Point, Marinship, and Kaiser Richmond shipyards.

Because restrictive racial covenants barred Black families from buying or renting homes across most of San Francisco's outer neighborhoods, these newly arrived shipyard workers settled in the vacant Victorians of the Fillmore District—living briefly alongside returning Japanese American families after 1945 and transforming a twenty-block corridor along Fillmore, Post, and Geary streets into a dazzling cultural capital known across America as the 'Harlem of the West.' By 1950, a pedestrian walking ten blocks down Fillmore Street between Eddy and Bush streets passed more than two hundred Black-owned businesses, churches, clothiers, barber shops, and two dozen live music venues—including Jack's Tavern on Sutter, the Club Alabam, the Texas Playhouse, the Blue Mirror, the Long Bar, and the Booker T. Washington Hotel lounge.

At the center of that musical galaxy stood 1690 Post Street, a three-story 1894 Queen Anne Victorian where former shipyard worker and shiny Cadillac salesman Charles 'Jimbo' Edwards opened Jimbo's Waffle Shop in 1949—serving crispy sixty-cent golden waffles and fried chicken through the night from a small kitchen in the front of the building. In 1950, pianist and musical director Slim Jenkins and trumpeter Johnnie Ingram convinced Jimbo Edwards to open the large back banquet room behind the Waffle Shop—formerly a Japanese social hall during the prewar Nihonmachi years—as an after-hours jazz club running from 2:00 a.m. to 6:00 a.m. after downtown San Francisco alcohol licenses shut down for the night.

Christened Jimbo's Bop City, the back room had strict rules enforced personally by Jimbo Edwards at the door: no liquor was sold at the tables (patrons brought bottles in brown paper bags and bought setups or waffles), nobody got in unless they respected the music, and the bandstand belonged to the fierce harmonic revolution of modern jazz—bebop. When touring jazz stars finished their early commercial sets at the Black Hawk in the Tenderloin or the Fairmont Hotel atop Nob Hill—where strict de facto segregation often prevented Black musicians from staying as overnight hotel guests—they headed straight to 1690 Post Street at 2:00 a.m. to play without commercial time limits.

On any given night during the 1950s inside Jimbo's Bop City, the wooden walls vibrated as Charlie 'Bird' Parker traded blistering alto-sax choruses with Sonny Stitt, Dexter Gordon, or local tenor prodigy Pony Poindexter; John Coltrane and Miles Davis worked out modal explorations until sunrise; and Dinah Washington, Sarah Vaughan, or Ella Fitzgerald stepped up to the microphone while Billie Holiday sat at her favorite corner table eating Jimbo's waffles. Photographers Steve Jackson Jr. and David Johnson (the first Black student of Ansel Adams at the California School of Fine Arts) captured unforgettable silver-gelatin portraits inside Bop City: Duke Ellington and Louis Armstrong laughing between sets, Sammy Davis Jr. tap-dancing across the hardwood floor at 4:00 a.m., and twenty-year-old Clint Eastwood and Beat writers Jack Kerouac and Neal Cassady squeezed into the back booths listening in awe.

Yet even as the music soared inside Bop City, a devastating bureaucratic machine was leveling the neighborhood outside: under the federal Housing Act of 1949, the San Francisco Redevelopment Agency (SFRA) led by Justin Herman declared the Western Addition 'blighted' and launched the A-1 and A-2 Urban Renewal projects. Between the late 1950s and the 1970s, despite courageous grassroots resistance from the Western Addition Community Organization (WACO) led by Mary Rogers and Hannibal Williams, Redevelopment Agency bulldozers demolished 2,500 Victorian homes, displaced more than 13,500 predominantly Black residents, and shuttered 883 neighborhood businesses—forcing Jimbo's Bop City to close its doors in 1965 as its patrons and neighbors were scattered across the Bay Area.

In 1979, as the final blocks around Post and Buchanan streets were cleared, preservationists saved the historic 1894 Bop City Victorian from the wrecking ball by jacking the entire three-story wooden building onto steel dollies and rolling it around the corner to a new foundation at 1712 Fillmore Street. Two years later, in 1981, Dr. Raye Richardson and her husband Julian Richardson—a Tuskegee printer who had founded the Success Printing Company on Fillmore Street in 1946 and opened Marcus Books (named for Marcus Garvey) in 1960—moved the flagship of the nation's oldest independent Black bookstore into the former Bop City building at 1712 Fillmore Street.

For thirty-three years inside that historic Victorian at 1712 Fillmore Street, the Richardson and Johnson family turned the former jazz sanctuary into a literary temple—hosting Maya Angelou, Toni Morrison, James Baldwin, Angela Davis, Octavia Butler, and Walter Mosley before real-estate speculation forced Marcus Books to consolidate to its Oakland store in 2014. Protected today as San Francisco City Landmark #241 (designated in 2004 specifically for its dual legacy as Jimbo's Bop City and Marcus Books), the house at 1712 Fillmore Street stands as an indestructible witness to the music and literature of Black San Francisco.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The 2:00 a.m. Cutting Contest: How Bop City Shaped West Coast Jazz

Why was an after-hours room behind a waffle shop at 1690 Post Street more artistically influential than the big downtown supper clubs? In commercial clubs, bands had to play three-minute radio arrangements for dancing tourists and stop at 2:00 a.m. At Jimbo's Bop City, from 2:00 a.m. to 6:00 a.m., touring masters like Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and John Coltrane stood shoulder to shoulder with Bay Area players in twenty-minute harmonic 'cutting contests' where only musical invention mattered.

2. 'Urban Renewal Is Negro Removal': The Western Addition A-1 & A-2 Projects

When writer James Baldwin visited San Francisco inside KQED camera vans in May 1963 to film the documentary Take This Hammer, he looked at the leveled blocks of the Fillmore and coined the phrase 'Urban renewal means Negro removal.' Under the Western Addition A-1 and A-2 redevelopment zones, the San Francisco Redevelopment Agency widened Geary Boulevard into an eight-lane moat and bulldozed 2,500 Victorian homes and 883 businesses, leaving blocks vacant for over a decade and destroying the economic ecosystem of the 'Harlem of the West.'

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three cultural touchstones: first, Charles 'Jimbo' Edwards's Waffle Shop and backroom Bop City (1950–1965); second, Julian and Raye Richardson's Marcus Books (founded 1960), the oldest independent Black bookstore in the United States; and third, City Landmark #241 at 1712 Fillmore Street, the 1894 Victorian that housed both institutions after being moved from 1690 Post Street in 1979.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk up Fillmore Street from Geary Boulevard to 1712 Fillmore Street between Post and Sutter streets to stand before the restored 1894 Queen Anne Victorian of Jimbo's Bop City and Marcus Books. Then walk one block south to the corner of Fillmore and Post streets to trace where the building originally stood at 1690 Post Street, and inspect the bronze plaques embedded in the Fillmore Jazz Heritage sidewalk.
