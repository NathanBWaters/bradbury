The Last Leaf

O. Henry (1907) • 12 min read

“One morning the busy doctor invited Sue into the hallway with a shaggy, gray eyebrow.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Last Leaf is one of the best-loved short stories by O. Henry, the pen name of William Sydney Porter. It was published in 1907 and gathered in his collection The Trimmed Lamp. Porter lived a rough, restless life. He worked in Texas, spent time in prison, and then settled in New York, where he wrote stories about ordinary city people, clerks, shop girls, struggling artists, and drifters, with sympathy and a gift for the surprise ending. This story is set among poor young painters in Greenwich Village, at a time when pneumonia was a dreaded killer and doctors had few cures. A sick young woman decides she will die when the last leaf falls from the ivy outside her window. What happens next is quiet, moving, and a little miraculous. It is a story about hope, and about the idea that the greatest work of art may not be hanging in a gallery at all.

In a little district west of Washington Square the streets have run crazy and broken themselves into small strips called “places.” These “places” make strange angles and curves. One street crosses itself a time or two. An artist once discovered a valuable possibility in this street. Suppose a collector with a bill for paints, paper and canvas should, in traversing this route, suddenly meet himself coming back, without a cent having been paid on account!

So, to quaint old Greenwich Village the art people soon came prowling, hunting for north windows and eighteenth-century gables and Dutch attics and low rents. Then they imported some pewter mugs and a chafing dish or two from Sixth Avenue, and became a “colony.”

At the top of a squatty, three-story brick Sue and Johnsy had their studio. “Johnsy” was familiar for Joanna. One was from Maine; the other from California. They had met at the table d’hôte of an Eighth Street “Delmonico’s,” and found their tastes in art, chicory salad and bishop sleeves so congenial that the joint studio resulted.

That was in May. In November a cold, unseen stranger, whom the doctors called Pneumonia, stalked about the colony, touching one here and there with his icy fingers. Over on the east side this ravager strode boldly, smiting his victims by scores, but his feet trod slowly through the maze of the narrow and moss-grown “places.”

Mr. Pneumonia was not what you would call a chivalric old gentleman. A mite of a little woman with blood thinned by California zephyrs was hardly fair game for the red-fisted, short-breathed old duffer. But Johnsy he smote; and she lay, scarcely moving, on her painted iron bedstead, looking through the small Dutch windowpanes at the blank side of the next brick house.

One morning the busy doctor invited Sue into the hallway with a shaggy, gray eyebrow.

“She has one chance in⁠—let us say, ten,” he said, as he shook down the mercury in his clinical thermometer. “And that chance is for her to want to live. This way people have of lining-up on the side of the undertaker makes the entire pharmacopeia look silly. Your little lady has made up her mind that she’s not going to get well. Has she anything on her mind?”

“She⁠—she wanted to paint the Bay of Naples some day,” said Sue.

“Paint?⁠—bosh! Has she anything on her mind worth thinking about twice⁠—a man, for instance?”

“A man?” said Sue, with a jew’sharp twang in her voice. “Is a man worth⁠—but, no, doctor; there is nothing of the kind.”

“Well, it is the weakness, then,” said the doctor. “I will do all that science, so far as it may filter through my efforts, can accomplish. But whenever my patient begins to count the carriages in her funeral procession I subtract 50 percent from the curative power of medicines. If you will get her to ask one question about the new winter styles in cloak sleeves I will promise you a one-in-five chance for her, instead of one in ten.”

After the doctor had gone Sue went into the workroom and cried a Japanese napkin to a pulp. Then she swaggered into Johnsy’s room with her drawing board, whistling ragtime.

Johnsy lay, scarcely making a ripple under the bedclothes, with her face toward the window. Sue stopped whistling, thinking she was asleep.

She arranged her board and began a pen-and-ink drawing to illustrate a magazine story. Young artists must pave their way to Art by drawing pictures for magazine stories that young authors write to pave their way to Literature.

As Sue was sketching a pair of elegant horseshow riding trousers and a monocle on the figure of the hero, an Idaho cowboy, she heard a low sound, several times repeated. She went quickly to the bedside.

Johnsy’s eyes were open wide. She was looking out the window and counting⁠—counting backward.

“Twelve,” she said, and a little later “eleven;” and then “ten,” and “nine;” and then “eight” and “seven,” almost together.

Sue looked solicitously out the window. What was there to count? There was only a bare, dreary yard to be seen, and the blank side of the brick house twenty feet away. An old, old ivy vine, gnarled and decayed at the roots, climbed halfway up the brick wall. The cold breath of autumn had stricken its leaves from the vine until its skeleton branches clung, almost bare, to the crumbling bricks.

“What is it, dear?” asked Sue.

“Six,” said Johnsy, in almost a whisper. “They’re falling faster now. Three days ago there were almost a hundred. It made my head ache to count them. But now it’s easy. There goes another one. There are only five left now.”

“Five what, dear. Tell your Sudie.”

“Leaves. On the ivy vine. When the last one falls I must go, too. I’ve known that for three days. Didn’t the doctor tell you?”

“Oh, I never heard of such nonsense,” complained Sue, with magnificent scorn. “What have old ivy leaves to do with your getting well? And you used to love that vine so, you naughty girl. Don’t be a goosey. Why, the doctor told me this morning that your chances for getting well real soon were⁠—let’s see exactly what he said⁠—he said the chances were ten to one! Why, that’s almost as good a chance as we have in New York when we ride on the street cars or walk past a new building. Try to take some broth now, and let Sudie go back to her drawing, so she can sell the editor man with it, and buy port wine for her sick child, and pork chops for her greedy self.”

“You needn’t get any more wine,” said Johnsy, keeping her eyes fixed out the window. “There goes another. No, I don’t want any broth. That leaves just four. I want to see the last one fall before it gets dark. Then I’ll go, too.”

“Johnsy, dear,” said Sue, bending over her, “will you promise me to keep your eyes closed, and not look out the window until I am done working? I must hand those drawings in by tomorrow. I need the light, or I would draw the shade down.”

“Couldn’t you draw in the other room?” asked Johnsy, coldly.

“I’d rather be here by you,” said Sue. “Besides I don’t want you to keep looking at those silly ivy leaves.”

“Tell me as soon as you have finished,” said Johnsy, closing her eyes, and lying white and still as a fallen statue, “because I want to see the last one fall. I’m tired of waiting. I’m tired of thinking. I want to turn loose my hold on everything, and go sailing down, down, just like one of those poor, tired leaves.”

“Try to sleep,” said Sue. “I must call Behrman up to be my model for the old hermit miner. I’ll not be gone a minute. Don’t try to move ’till I come back.”

Old Behrman was a painter who lived on the ground floor beneath them. He was past sixty and had a Michelangelo’s Moses beard curling down from the head of a satyr along the body of an imp. Behrman was a failure in art. Forty years he had wielded the brush without getting near enough to touch the hem of his Mistress’s robe. He had been always about to paint a masterpiece, but had never yet begun it. For several years he had painted nothing except now and then a daub in the line of commerce or advertising. He earned a little by serving as a model to those young artists in the colony who could not pay the price of a professional. He drank gin to excess, and still talked of his coming masterpiece. For the rest he was a fierce little old man, who scoffed terribly at softness in anyone, and who regarded himself as especial mastiff-in-waiting to protect the two young artists in the studio above.

Sue found Behrman smelling strongly of juniper berries in his dimly lighted den below. In one corner was a blank canvas on an easel that had been waiting there for twenty-five years to receive the first line of the masterpiece. She told him of Johnsy’s fancy, and how she feared she would, indeed, light and fragile as a leaf herself, float away when her slight hold upon the world grew weaker.

Old Behrman, with his red eyes, plainly streaming, shouted his contempt and derision for such idiotic imaginings.

“Vass!” he cried. “Is dere people in de world mit der foolishness to die because leafs dey drop off from a confounded vine? I haf not heard of such a thing. No, I will not bose as a model for your fool hermit-dunderhead. Vy do you allow dot silly pusiness to come in der prain of her? Ach, dot poor lettle Miss Johnsy.”

“She is very ill and weak,” said Sue, “and the fever has left her mind morbid and full of strange fancies. Very well, Mr. Behrman, if you do not care to pose for me, you needn’t. But I think you are a horrid old⁠—old flibbertigibbet.”

“You are just like a woman!” yelled Behrman. “Who said I will not bose? Go on. I come mit you. For half an hour I haf peen trying to say dot I am ready to bose. Gott! dis is not any blace in which one so goot as Miss Yohnsy shall lie sick. Some day I vill baint a masterpiece, and ve shall all go away. Gott! yes.”

Johnsy was sleeping when they went upstairs. Sue pulled the shade down to the windowsill, and motioned Behrman into the other room. In there they peered out the window fearfully at the ivy vine. Then they looked at each other for a moment without speaking. A persistent, cold rain was falling, mingled with snow. Behrman, in his old blue shirt, took his seat as the hermit-miner on an upturned kettle for a rock.

When Sue awoke from an hour’s sleep the next morning she found Johnsy with dull, wide-open eyes staring at the drawn green shade.

“Pull it up; I want to see,” she ordered, in a whisper.

Wearily Sue obeyed.

But, lo! after the beating rain and fierce gusts of wind that had endured through the livelong night, there yet stood out against the brick wall one ivy leaf. It was the last on the vine. Still dark green near its stem, but with its serrated edges tinted with the yellow of dissolution and decay, it hung bravely from a branch some twenty feet above the ground.

“It is the last one,” said Johnsy. “I thought it would surely fall during the night. I heard the wind. It will fall today, and I shall die at the same time.”

“Dear, dear!” said Sue, leaning her worn face down to the pillow, “think of me, if you won’t think of yourself. What would I do?”

But Johnsy did not answer. The lonesomest thing in all the world is a soul when it is making ready to go on its mysterious, far journey. The fancy seemed to possess her more strongly as one by one the ties that bound her to friendship and to earth were loosed.

The day wore away, and even through the twilight they could see the lone ivy leaf clinging to its stem against the wall. And then, with the coming of the night the north wind was again loosed, while the rain still beat against the windows and pattered down from the low Dutch eaves.

When it was light enough Johnsy, the merciless, commanded that the shade be raised.

The ivy leaf was still there.

Johnsy lay for a long time looking at it. And then she called to Sue, who was stirring her chicken broth over the gas stove.

“I’ve been a bad girl, Sudie,” said Johnsy. “Something has made that last leaf stay there to show me how wicked I was. It is a sin to want to die. You may bring me a little broth now, and some milk with a little port in it, and⁠—no; bring me a hand-mirror first, and then pack some pillows about me, and I will sit up and watch you cook.”

An hour later she said.

“Sudie, some day I hope to paint the Bay of Naples.”

The doctor came in the afternoon, and Sue had an excuse to go into the hallway as he left.

“Even chances,” said the doctor, taking Sue’s thin, shaking hand in his. “With good nursing you’ll win. And now I must see another case I have downstairs. Behrman, his name is⁠—some kind of an artist, I believe. Pneumonia, too. He is an old, weak man, and the attack is acute. There is no hope for him; but he goes to the hospital today to be made more comfortable.”

The next day the doctor said to Sue: “She’s out of danger. You’ve won. Nutrition and care now⁠—that’s all.”

And that afternoon Sue came to the bed where Johnsy lay, contentedly knitting a very blue and very useless woolen shoulder scarf, and put one arm around her, pillows and all.

“I have something to tell you, white mouse,” she said. “Mr. Behrman died of pneumonia today in the hospital. He was ill only two days. The janitor found him on the morning of the first day in his room downstairs helpless with pain. His shoes and clothing were wet through and icy cold. They couldn’t imagine where he had been on such a dreadful night. And then they found a lantern, still lighted, and a ladder that had been dragged from its place, and some scattered brushes, and a palette with green and yellow colors mixed on it, and⁠—look out the window, dear, at the last ivy leaf on the wall. Didn’t you wonder why it never fluttered or moved when the wind blew? Ah, darling, it’s Behrman’s masterpiece⁠—he painted it there the night that the last leaf fell.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The Last Leaf looks like a simple tale about a sick girl and a vine, but it is really about what keeps a person alive, and what a work of art can truly be for. The setting is a cramped corner of Greenwich Village, in the early twentieth century, where young painters rent cheap rooms. Two women, Sue and Johnsy, share a studio at the top of a brick building. In November, pneumonia moves through the neighborhood. Johnsy catches it, and the doctor tells Sue that her friend has a one-in-ten chance, and that the chance depends on whether she wants to live. Johnsy has given up. From her bed she counts the leaves falling from an old ivy vine on the wall opposite, and tells Sue, "When the last one falls I must go, too."

This is a familiar idea, and Henry knows it. People in the nineteenth century were fond of stories in which a sickly heroine's fate was tied to nature, as if her life were a flower or a leaf. Johnsy has absorbed that romantic picture and now believes it. Sue, who is practical and warm, tries to argue her out of it, and cannot.

The turning point comes through a third character, the old painter Behrman who lives downstairs. He is a failure. For forty years he has been about to paint a masterpiece, and the blank canvas in his room has been waiting twenty-five years for its first line. He drinks gin, grumbles in a thick German accent, and calls himself the protector of the two young women. When Sue tells him about Johnsy's fancy, he shouts that it is foolishness. But he is the one who takes it seriously. During a night of freezing rain and wind, the last leaf somehow does not fall. Johnsy, shamed by its stubborn courage, says "It is a sin to want to die," and begins to recover.

Then comes the reveal. Behrman has died of pneumonia, and the janitor found a lantern, a ladder, scattered brushes, and a palette with green and yellow paint. Sue tells Johnsy, "it's Behrman's masterpiece—he painted it there the night that the last leaf fell." The leaf on the wall was a painting, so true to life that it fooled a sick woman for two days, and its painter gave his own life for it. The deepest layer is the meaning of "masterpiece." Behrman spent his life talking about one, and when he finally makes it, it is not on canvas and no one will hang it in a museum. It is a single painted leaf, and it saves a life. The story suggests that art matters most when it does something, and that love can be a kind of craft.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Is The Last Leaf as good as its reputation? In large part, yes, but with some honest reservations. The strongest thing about it is its emotional design. The story begins with comedy and a touch of mockery, with its description of crazy Greenwich Village streets and the cold figure of "Mr. Pneumonia" as a rough old gentleman who is not very chivalrous. Then it narrows to a bedroom, a window, a vine, and a dying girl, and the humor drains away just as the tension rises. The counting of the leaves, "Twelve," then "eleven," then "ten," is a simple device that creates real suspense. We are being told a clock is running down, and we know that something must change.

Behrman is the story's most vivid creation. In a few strokes Henry gives him a Michelangelo beard on an imp's body, a smell of juniper berries, and a comic accent, "Vass! Is dere people in de world mit der foolishness to die because leafs dey drop off from a confounded vine?" The roughness hides tenderness, and when he agrees to pose, he shouts, "Gott! dis is not any blace in which one so goot as Miss Yohnsy shall lie sick." We laugh at him, and then we are asked to honor him, which is a difficult trick.

The structure is clever, too. Henry keeps the secret from us and gives a fair set of clues, the cold night, the wind, the unmoving leaf, and then the doctor's remark that an old man downstairs named Behrman has acute pneumonia. The twist does not cheat, which is what a good twist should do. It explains everything that came before. But the story also has weaknesses. The plot depends on some convenient assumptions. Would a painted leaf, from a window twenty feet away, fool a sick woman for two full days, in daylight, as she stared at it? Henry hurries past the problem, and most readers accept it as part of the fairy-tale spell, but a skeptical reader may notice. The idea that a person's will to live can decide the outcome of pneumonia is the doctor's, and it sits uneasily with medical fact.

Some of the narration is also overwritten. Henry's decorative style, with phrases like the "red-fisted, short-breathed old duffer," can feel like a performance. The portrayal of Johnsy is rather thin. She is a passive figure who must be rescued by others, and we learn almost nothing about her except that she wants to paint the Bay of Naples. And the sacrifice of the old man to save the young woman is, frankly, sentimental. Still, the sentiment is earned by how little the story insists on it.

III. The Impact It Had

The Last Leaf appeared in The Trimmed Lamp in 1907, at the height of O. Henry's popularity. Within a few years he was dead, from cirrhosis, in 1910, but by then the story had begun its long life as one of his signature works. Along with The Gift of the Magi, it is the piece that readers most often name when they think of him. Its influence can be seen first in education. For more than a century, it has been a regular in school reading, partly because it is short and partly because it teaches so much so clearly: symbolism in the leaf, foreshadowing in the cold night, irony in the ending. Teachers use it to show how an object can carry a theme, and how a surprise can be prepared in advance. Many students encounter it as their first example of a twist ending that feels earned.

It has also been adapted many times. The 1952 anthology film O. Henry's Full House included a version of the story, with the cast including Anne Baxter and Jean Peters, among others, and it has appeared in television dramas, radio programs, and in stage and animated versions in many countries. The basic image has become so familiar that it has been borrowed, sometimes without credit, in all sorts of fiction about patients, hospitals, and magic tricks of hope. A story about a patient who believes in a sign, and a friend who quietly arranges for the sign to hold, has become a recognizable shape in popular storytelling.

The leaf itself has become a piece of general cultural shorthand. People speak of "the last leaf" as a symbol of a final hope, a last thread, or someone who hangs on when others have fallen. It is also associated with the idea that a small, loving deception can heal. Doctors and nurses sometimes cite it when talking about the power of hope in recovery, though that is a matter of folk wisdom more than science.

In Henry's own career, the story shows that he could do more than amuse. Much of his fame rests on his twist endings, but here the twist serves tenderness rather than a joke. It gave critics and readers a reason to take him seriously as a humane writer, one who understood poverty and failure from inside. Because he had himself known disgrace and hardship, the portrait of Behrman, a man whose life has not gone as planned but who still finds a way to be great, carries a personal weight.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Last Leaf mainly because it moves them. Many people remember the first time they read it, usually in school, and the moment they realized what the last leaf really was. The ending is one of the most celebrated in short fiction because it makes the reader feel two things at once, relief that Johnsy lives and grief that Behrman does not. It is hard to get both so quickly in so few words.

They also love Behrman. Readers are drawn to the old failure who turns out to be a hero. He has the dignity of someone who has never been recognized, and the story gives him recognition at the cost of his life. The idea that a failed artist could, in one cold night, paint what he had been promising for forty years has an almost religious force. For anyone who has doubted their own talent, or watched a parent or teacher go unappreciated, it offers a deep consolation. A life is not wasted if it ends in one great act of love.

The friendship between Sue and Johnsy is another source of affection. Sue's loyalty, shown in her tears over the Japanese napkin and then her noisy whistling of ragtime as she walks into the sickroom, is a model of what it means to care for someone while pretending to be cheerful. Readers who have nursed a sick friend recognize that mixture of fear and false brightness at once. There is also a pleasure in the setting. Henry's Greenwich Village, with its crooked streets and struggling painters, is a charming little world, and the details, the chicory salad, the pewter mugs, the "squatty, three-story brick," make it vivid. Many readers say they would like to have visited that place.

Then there is the language of the ending. Its calm and simplicity affect readers deeply. Sue's last speech is not grand. She just says what happened, in plain words, and invites Johnsy to look out the window. Many writers have praised this restraint, because Henry could easily have ended with a flourish. Instead he lets the quiet fact do the work. Lastly, readers love that the story believes in hope. In a world in which pneumonia killed many, the idea that a person might be saved by seeing something brave is comforting, and that is part of why the story has been retold for so long.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Last Leaf has plenty of admirers, but it has also met reservations over the years, and they deserve a fair hearing. The most frequent criticism is sentimentality. Critics who prefer restraint have found the story too eager to bring tears. The dying heroine, the noble old drunk, the heroic sacrifice, and the miraculous recovery all follow patterns that were well worn by 1907. Some readers feel that Henry is manipulating them, arranging events so that the emotional payoff is guaranteed. A more demanding kind of fiction, they say, would have left Johnsy's recovery uncertain, or would have let death be less tidy and less meaningful. In a real hospital, a painted leaf does not save a life, and the story's comfort comes at the price of truth.

Related to that is the objection to the mechanism of the plot. As with much of Henry's work, the story is built around a surprise, and some critics say that the surprise matters more than the people. The idea that a woman could be fooled for two days by a painted leaf is difficult to believe, and the medical idea that a patient's despair decides the outcome of pneumonia is at best exaggerated. Henry rushes past these problems because he knows the reader wants the ending to work.

There are also some uncomfortable details of characterization. Behrman is a comic German immigrant, with a thick accent that Henry spells out phonetically, and the accent was a stock comic device of the time that can read as mockery. His drinking, his bluster, and his failure are all handled in a familiar way. And the story gives its female characters little to do beyond suffering and nursing, while the one man performs the great act. A modern reader may notice that the women are objects of rescue and that the man's sacrifice is what the story honors most.

Some readers have also pointed out a moral ambiguity that the story does not look at. Behrman is a man past sixty with, presumably, a weak constitution, and he climbs a ladder in a freezing storm to paint a leaf. The story praises him, but it does not ask whether he had other choices, such as getting Sue to help, or telling Johnsy the truth. It treats his death as noble and necessary, which is a romantic choice. Finally, there is the stylistic criticism that Henry's heavy ornament, the jokes in the narration and the literary asides, sometimes gets in the way of the emotion. Critics who prefer plainness find some of the early pages too busy. None of these objections have damaged the story's reputation, but they explain why it is admired more by general readers than by academic critics.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Last Leaf belongs to the tradition of local-color and urban sketches that flourished in American magazines around 1900, when writers discovered that ordinary city life, with its small tragedies and comedies, made good fiction. Henry's New York stories, collected in books like The Four Million, The Trimmed Lamp, and The Voice of the City, were the most popular of the kind. They gave voice to the shop clerks and out-of-work artists who filled the city but not, until then, its literature.

At a deeper level, the story descends from the Victorian tradition of the dying young person, which includes figures like Little Nell in Dickens's The Old Curiosity Shop, and the whole era's fascination with consumption and fading. Nature, in such stories, mirrors the heroine's fate. Henry takes that pattern and turns it upside down in a characteristic way. Instead of letting the heroine fade with the leaves, he lets a human hand intervene, which is both a rejection of fatalism and a tribute to the power of kindness. In this he is closer to the Dickens of A Christmas Carol than to the Dickens of Nell.

It also fits a larger theme in literature about the relationship between art and life. A painter who has failed all his life makes one image that is more alive than any he has ever made. There is an echo of the old legend of Pygmalion, the sculptor whose statue comes to life, and of countless stories about artists whose best work is a sacrifice. In this version, the work does not become alive. It makes someone else alive, which is a gentler twist on the idea.

Compared with other works about illness, such as Katherine Anne Porter's Pale Horse, Pale Rider, which treats the influenza epidemic with a far darker and more ambiguous eye, The Last Leaf is a story of consolation. It offers the hope that Porter withholds. Readers who want both kinds of truth will find that they complement each other. Among Henry's own stories, it pairs naturally with The Gift of the Magi, which also turns on sacrifice and a small object, and with The Cop and the Anthem, which has the same gentle eye for the poor of New York. All three show his belief that dignity can be found in unlikely places. Why read it now? Because its central question remains alive: what gives a person the will to go on? The story answers, with great simplicity, that sometimes it is a sign of courage from someone who loves you, and that such a gesture can matter more than a masterpiece in a museum. It takes only a few minutes to read, and it tends to stay with people for years.
