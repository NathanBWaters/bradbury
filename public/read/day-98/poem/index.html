Requiem

Robert Louis Stevenson (1887) • 3 min read

Under the wide and starry sky, dig the grave and let me lie: eight luminous lines of gratitude engraved upon Stevenson’s mountain tomb in Samoa.

Key Context & Why It Matters

First drafted in January 1880 in San Francisco when twenty-nine-year-old Robert Louis Stevenson was near death from tuberculosis and poverty (living at 608 Bush Street before his marriage to Fanny Vandegrift Osbourne), revised in Hyères in 1884, published in Underwoods (1887), and carved in bronze upon his tomb atop Mount Vaea in Samoa after his death in 1894. Belonging to the Late Victorian / Scottish Romanticism tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'Requiem' (1887) unfolds across 8 verbatim lines in 2 stanzas. Translated into Samoan as the Song of Grief by the islanders who cut the 'Road of the Loving Heart' up Mount Vaea to carry Stevenson’s coffin on their shoulders, and quoted by A. E. Housman and Jorge Luis Borges, 'Requiem' is the most beloved self-epitaph in English.

Under the wide and starry sky,

Dig the grave and let me lie.

Glad did I live and gladly die,

And I laid me down with a will.

This be the verse you grave for me:

Here he lies where he longed to be;

Home is the sailor, home from sea,

And the hunter home from the hill.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

First drafted in January 1880 in San Francisco when twenty-nine-year-old Robert Louis Stevenson was near death from tuberculosis and poverty (living at 608 Bush Street before his marriage to Fanny Vandegrift Osbourne), revised in Hyères in 1884, published in Underwoods (1887), and carved in bronze upon his tomb atop Mount Vaea in Samoa after his death in 1894. Within the broader trajectory of Late Victorian / Scottish Romanticism, Robert Louis Stevenson composed 'Requiem' (1887) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Under the wide and starry sky,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Robert Louis Stevenson grounded these 8 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Requiem' lies joyful, uncomplaining gratitude for the gift of mortal life and peaceful acceptance of its close: declaring 'Glad did I live and gladly die, / And I laid me down with a will,' the lifelong invalid who crossed oceans and continents asks that his epitaph record simply that 'Home is the sailor, home from sea, / And the hunter home from the hill'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"This be the verse you grave for me:"—Robert Louis Stevenson develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"And the hunter home from the hill."—the central metaphor.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Robert Louis Stevenson constructs 'Requiem' using two four-line monorhymed/coupled quatrains (AAAB CCCB) in resonant iambic tetrameter and trimeter, using thirty-six simple Anglo-Saxon words (thirty-one of them monosyllables) and chiasmic parallelism ('Glad did I live and gladly die,' 'sailor... from sea / hunter... from the hill') to achieve the permanence of Roman stone. Spanning 8 lines across 2 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Robert Louis Stevenson balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Under the wide and starry sky," and "And the hunter home from the hill." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Translated into Samoan as the Song of Grief by the islanders who cut the 'Road of the Loving Heart' up Mount Vaea to carry Stevenson’s coffin on their shoulders, and quoted by A. E. Housman and Jorge Luis Borges, 'Requiem' is the most beloved self-epitaph in English. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Requiem' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Robert Louis Stevenson’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
