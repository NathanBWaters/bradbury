Nightly Essay • Travel & Personal Essay

Davos in Winter

Robert Louis Stevenson (1881) • 6 min read

High in the snow-locked Alpine valley, only the silent procession of sun and stars moves across the white amphitheater.

Key Context & Why It Matters

In the early 1880s Robert Louis Stevenson, then in his thirties and already struggling with a serious lung illness, spent two winters at Davos, a Swiss mountain village that had become a refuge for people with tuberculosis. He was newly married to Fanny Osbourne and anxious about money as well as health. This short essay, which appeared in 1881, is his answer to a question travellers rarely ask: what is it actually like to live in a famous beautiful place when you cannot leave and cannot walk far? His answer is surprising. The valley is a pretty prison. The snow is monotonous, the walks are crowded, and the mountains close you in. Yet he finds odd moments of grandeur in the storms and the dawns. The piece is brief and funny, but it is really about confinement, boredom, and what the invalid's eye can still learn to see.

A mountain valley has, at the best, a certain prison-like effect on the imagination, but a mountain valley, an Alpine winter, and an invalid’s weakness make up among them a prison of the most effective kind. The roads indeed are cleared, and at least one footpath dodging up the hill; but to these the health-seeker is rigidly confined. There are for him no crosscuts over the field, no following of streams, no unguided rambles in the wood. His walks are cut and dry. In five or six different directions he can push as far, and no farther, than his strength permits; never deviating from the line laid down for him and beholding at each repetition the same field of wood and snow from the same corner of the road. This, of itself, would be a little trying to the patience in the course of months; but to this is added, by the heaped mantle of the snow, an almost utter absence of detail and an almost unbroken identity of colour. Snow, it is true, is not merely white. The sun touches it with roseate and golden lights. Its own crushed infinity of crystals, its own richness of tiny sculpture, fills it, when regarded near at hand, with wonderful depths of coloured shadow, and, though wintrily transformed, it is still water, and has watery tones of blue. But, when all is said, these fields of white and blots of crude black forest are but a trite and staring substitute for the infinite variety and pleasantness of the earth’s face. Even a boulder, whose front is too precipitous to have retained the snow, seems, if you come upon it in your walk, a perfect gem of colour, reminds you almost painfully of other places, and brings into your head the delights of more Arcadian days⁠—the path across the meadow, the hazel dell, the lilies on the stream, and the scents, the colours, and the whisper of the woods. And scents here are as rare as colours. Unless you get a gust of kitchen in passing some hotel, you shall smell nothing all day long but the faint and choking odour of frost. Sounds, too, are absent: not a bird pipes, not a bough waves, in the dead, windless atmosphere. If a sleigh goes by, the sleigh-bells ring, and that is all; you work all winter through to no other accompaniment but the crunching of your steps upon the frozen snow.

It is the curse of the Alpine valleys to be each one village from one end to the other. Go where you please, houses will still be in sight, before and behind you, and to the right and left. Climb as high as an invalid is able, and it is only to spy new habitations nested in the wood. Nor is that all; for about the health resort the walks are besieged by single people walking rapidly with plaids about their shoulders, by sudden troops of German boys trying to learn to jödel, and by German couples silently and, as you venture to fancy, not quite happily, pursuing love’s young dream. You may perhaps be an invalid who likes to make bad verses as he walks about. Alas! no muse will suffer this imminence of interruption⁠—and at the second stampede of jödellers you find your modest inspiration fled. Or you may only have a taste for solitude; it may try your nerves to have someone always in front whom you are visibly overtaking, and someone always behind who is audibly overtaking you, to say nothing of a score or so who brush past you in an opposite direction. It may annoy you to take your walks and seats in public view. Alas! there is no help for it among the Alps. There are no recesses, as in Gorbio Valley by the oil-mill; no sacred solitude of olive gardens on the Roccabruna-road; no nook upon Saint Martin’s Cape, haunted by the voice of breakers, and fragrant with the threefold sweetness of the rosemary and the sea-pines and the sea.

For this publicity there is no cure, and no alleviation; but the storms of which you will complain so bitterly while they endure, chequer and by their contrast brighten the sameness of the fair-weather scenes. When sun and storm contend together⁠—when the thick clouds are broken up and pierced by arrows of golden daylight⁠—there will be startling rearrangements and transfigurations of the mountain summits. A sun-dazzling spire of alp hangs suspended in mid-sky among awful glooms and blackness; or perhaps the edge of some great mountain shoulder will be designed in living gold, and appear for the duration of a glance bright like a constellation, and alone “in the unapparent.” You may think you know the figure of these hills; but when they are thus revealed, they belong no longer to the things of earth⁠—meteors we should rather call them, appearances of sun and air that endure but for a moment and return no more. Other variations are more lasting, as when, for instance, heavy and wet snow has fallen through some windless hours, and the thin, spiry, mountain pine trees stand each stock-still and loaded with a shining burden. You may drive through a forest so disguised, the tongue-tied torrent struggling silently in the cleft of the ravine, and all still except the jingle of the sleigh bells, and you shall fancy yourself in some untrodden northern territory⁠—Lapland, Labrador, or Alaska.

Or, possibly, you arise very early in the morning; totter downstairs in a state of somnambulism; take the simulacrum of a meal by the glimmer of one lamp in the deserted coffee-room; and find yourself by seven o’clock outside in a belated moonlight and a freezing chill. The mail sleigh takes you up and carries you on, and you reach the top of the ascent in the first hour of the day. To trace the fires of the sunrise as they pass from peak to peak, to see the unlit treetops stand out soberly against the lighted sky, to be for twenty minutes in a wonderland of clear, fading shadows, disappearing vapours, solemn blooms of dawn, hills half glorified already with the day and still half confounded with the greyness of the western heaven⁠—these will seem to repay you for the discomforts of that early start; but as the hour proceeds, and these enchantments vanish, you will find yourself upon the farther side in yet another Alpine valley, snow white and coal black, with such another long-drawn congeries of hamlets and such another senseless watercourse bickering along the foot. You have had your moment; but you have not changed the scene. The mountains are about you like a trap; you cannot foot it up a hillside and behold the sea as a great plain, but live in holes and corners, and can change only one for another.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The essay begins with a startling move. Instead of praising the Alps, as nearly every Victorian traveller did, Stevenson calls the mountain valley in winter a prison. He builds the case slowly and with great precision. The roads are cleared, but only a few of them, and the invalid is rigidly confined to them. He cannot cut across a field, follow a stream, or wander into a wood. His walks are cut and dry. He is allowed to go as far as his strength permits and no farther, and he sees the same view from the same corner of the same road again and again. The key word is invalid. The writer is not a tourist but a patient, and every complaint in the essay is filtered through the fact that he cannot walk, climb, or go where he likes.

Then he turns to the senses. Snow, he says, is not merely white, and he praises it generously, noting the roseate and golden lights, the tiny sculpture of the crystals, even the watery blue. But once the praise is out of the way, he reminds us that it is a trite and staring substitute for the variety of the earth's face. What he misses is detail: colour, scent, and sound. The little passage about a boulder is the heart of this section. A rock too steep to hold snow looks like a jewel, because it reminds him almost painfully of other places, the path across the meadow, the hazel dell, the lilies in the stream. The pain in that word is real. Deprivation sharpens memory, and what he misses is not grandeur but ordinary country life.

The second half of the essay moves from nature to people. Stevenson complains that the Alpine valley is each one village from end to end, and that the walks are besieged by walkers in plaids, by parties of German boys learning to yodel, and by German couples silently pursuing love's young dream. The comedy has a serious point. He is a writer who makes bad verses as he walks, and he cannot compose with someone always in front whom you are visibly overtaking and someone always behind who is audibly overtaking you. He wants solitude, and the Alps will not give it. By contrast he recalls the quiet olive gardens and the sea-nooks of the Riviera, that he had known in earlier years.

Finally, the essay turns, as many of Stevenson's do, toward grace. Storms break up the sameness, and he describes mountain peaks transfigured by sun and cloud, a forest of snow-laden pines like Lapland, and the unearthly dawn from a mail sleigh. But the last note is not triumphant. You have had your moment, he says, but you have not changed the scene. The mountains are about you like a trap. The final sentence is a quiet, rueful sentence about living in holes and corners, and its meaning is that beauty does not cancel confinement.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a small piece, and it should be judged as one. Its virtues are precision, comic timing, and an unusually honest tone. Stevenson's central trick is to take a received idea, that the Alps are glorious, and examine it from the point of view of someone who must live there. He does this without bitterness. The humour is the humour of a man who knows he is complaining and enjoys the complaint, and who understands that the invalid has a right to be cross. Nothing in the essay is a pose of suffering. The sentence about the second stampede of yodellers that chases away a modest inspiration is funny precisely because it is so small.

The best writing is in the sensory passages. His account of how little there is to smell, nothing but a gust of kitchen from a hotel or the faint and choking odour of frost, is exact and slightly shocking. The silence is made audible: not a bird pipes, not a bough waves, and you work all winter to no accompaniment but the crunching of your steps on frozen snow. Few writers describe absence so well. He also knows how to build a long sentence, using lists and repeated clauses so that the reader feels the sameness of the days. The prose enacts the monotony it describes, yet never becomes dull.

The storm and dawn passages are a deliberate counterweight. They offer the splendour that the first paragraphs withhold, and they show how well Stevenson can paint with words, in phrases like a spire of alp hanging suspended in mid-sky among awful glooms, or a mountain shoulder designed in living gold. These are the most conventional moments in the essay, and a little more purple than the plain sentences around them. To a modern ear the language of transfigured peaks and solemn blooms of dawn can feel stock, and the essay is stronger on boredom than on beauty.

There are other weaknesses. The structure is slight, and the piece reads more like a sketch than a finished essay. It ends on a note of mild rue and does not develop its ideas into anything larger. The second person address, the you who totters downstairs at dawn, sometimes tips from charm into mannerism. And the gentle sniping at the German visitors, while good-humoured, rests on a national stereotype that does not wear well.

None of this destroys the piece. It is a bit of fine, dry observation, and its honesty about the limits of beauty is its chief gift. It reminds us that even the world's best views can wear thin, a truth that few travel writers dare to say.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be false to claim that this short essay changed the course of literature, and it is better to say plainly that its influence has been small and indirect. It was a journalistic piece, written for a newspaper, at a time when Stevenson was better known as a travel writer and essayist than as a novelist. It was never a famous work in its own right, and it is seldom taught on its own. What it did do was add a little to a larger picture.

The first thing to say is that it records an important chapter in Stevenson's life. He went to Davos in the winter of 1880 and returned for a second stay in 1881 and 1882, and those winters were deeply tied up with his work. During the second stay he finished Treasure Island, the book that made his name. The famous novel of pirates and buried gold was thus completed in a snowbound valley by a sick man, and this essay gives an unusually direct sense of what that setting felt like. It is a small window on the conditions in which a classic adventure story was written, and the contrast between the cramped, colourless life of the invalid and the bright imagined world of the island is striking.

Second, the essay belongs to the wider literature of Davos as a place. The little town became a famous health resort for people with tuberculosis, and its reputation as a place of confinement, boredom and strange beauty gave rise to a whole body of writing. The most famous example is Thomas Mann's novel The Magic Mountain, published in 1924, in which a young man visits a sanatorium at Davos and stays for seven years. Mann's book is a very different work, a vast novel of ideas, but it shares with Stevenson's essay the sense of a mountain world cut off from ordinary time, where sickness and stillness change the mind. Anyone who has read Mann will find Stevenson's few paragraphs a fascinating early glimpse of the same atmosphere.

Third, the essay anticipates later complaints about mass tourism and crowded beauty spots. His irritation at being besieged by walkers on a supposedly remote mountain path is a recognisable ancestor of modern grumbling about popular destinations.

Finally, the essay helped to build the legend of Stevenson as a writer who made literature out of illness. He lived with severe lung trouble throughout his adult life, and he treated it with courage and wit. Pieces like this one, which turn confinement into prose, are part of what made him an exemplar for later writers who worked under physical limits. It is a minor contribution, but a genuine one.

IV. What Readers Loved

The great pleasure of this essay is its honesty. Most writing about the Alps before and after Stevenson praises them without reservation, and there is something refreshing in a writer who stops to say that after months in the same valley the view is a trap. Readers often respond to that candour with gratitude. Many have felt the same fatigue in a lovely place, on a long holiday, in a sickroom with a view, and they enjoy seeing it put so exactly.

They also love the comedy. The little portrait of the health resort, with the single people walking rapidly with plaids about their shoulders, the sudden troops of German boys trying to learn to yodel, and the German couples pursuing love's young dream silently and not quite happily, is like a small comic painting. It is affectionate rather than cruel, and it gives the essay a lightness that stops the complaint from becoming heavy. Stevenson's habit of speaking to you directly, in a tone of friendly irony, makes the reader feel taken into his confidence.

Another strong appeal is his handling of the senses. There is a particular pleasure in a writer who lists what is missing, no scent, no colour, no birdsong, and by doing so makes you feel the world's richness more strongly. The boulder that seems a perfect gem of colour is one of the loveliest touches in the piece, and many readers remember it. The idea that deprivation makes the eye hungry for small things is both true and moving.

Readers also admire the shift to the transfigured mountains. After the long stretch of complaint, the description of a spire of alp hanging in the sky among glooms, or an edge of mountain designed in living gold for the length of a glance, is exhilarating. It is the more powerful because it has been earned. The image of the pines each loaded with a shining burden of snow, through which you drive in silence as if in Lapland or Alaska, has the dreamlike quality that Stevenson often achieved. And the passage on the dawn, with fires of sunrise passing from peak to peak, is one that anthologists have liked.

Finally, people love the humane note beneath the humour. The essay does not pretend that the writer is brave or the place is wonderful. It simply reports, in clear and graceful prose, what it is like to be stuck. That plainness, offered by a man in real physical trouble, makes the piece quietly affecting.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the essay is short and rarely discussed, there is no great history of criticism attached to it. The objections below are the sort that readers and critics have raised about Stevenson's travel essays in general, and that a thoughtful modern reader is likely to feel when meeting this one.

The first is that it is slight. Compared with his longer works, it is a sketch, and it makes no large argument. A reader who wants ideas will find mainly mood. The essay observes, jokes, and then stops, and it never really explains why monotony weighs so heavily on the invalid or what it teaches him. For critics who prize the essay as a vehicle for thought, this makes it a lesser piece, charming but not weighty.

The second is that it is a complaint from a position of privilege. Davos in the 1880s was a place for those who could afford to travel, rent rooms in a hotel, and spend months recovering. The essay is about the boredom of the comfortable. Stevenson's money worries were real, but the people who cleaned the hotels and drove the sleighs do not appear. Some readers might feel that his grumbling about German couples and yodelling boys is pettish when set against the plight of many invalids who had no such refuge.

The third is the national stereotyping. The German visitors are treated as comic types, rather like figures in a cartoon, and the joke depends on the idea of a typical German tourist. It is gentle by the standards of the age, but it sits on a stock of assumptions that have worn thin.

Another line of criticism is about the ornate prose. The passages describing the mountains at sunrise or in storm use the vocabulary of high Victorian description, and some critics, especially in the twentieth century, have found such writing overdone. Phrases like solemn blooms of dawn can seem stagey, and the sentence about meteors and appearances of sun and air that return no more reaches for effect.

Finally, the ending is slightly abrupt. The essay makes its point that the mountains are a trap and then closes, and some readers feel that the piece ends not with a conclusion but with a shrug. Defenders would say that this is exactly the right tone, since the whole point is that nothing changes. Still, the lack of development is a fair complaint, and it is the main reason the essay is regarded as a pleasant minor work.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Stevenson's essay belongs to several overlapping traditions. The first is the Victorian travel essay, which had been shaped by writers like John Ruskin, who wrote with passion about the Alps, and by the mountaineers who made the peaks a fashionable subject. The great mountain literature of the time celebrated the sublime, the moral lessons of the heights, and the thrill of ascent. Stevenson's piece is a deliberate reversal of that tradition. He writes from the valley, not the summit, from weakness, not strength. In that sense it is an anti-sublime, an essay that looks at the Alps from the viewpoint of someone who cannot climb them.

The second tradition is the literature of illness. Writers have always written about sickness, but the nineteenth century, with its fascination with tuberculosis, produced a particular body of work in which the sickroom becomes a place of heightened perception. John Keats, who died of tuberculosis, is the great Romantic example, and later novelists like Thomas Mann and Katherine Mansfield, who also suffered from the disease, explored the same ground. Stevenson's contribution is a lightness of tone. He never dwells on his symptoms. Instead he turns the experience into comedy and observation, and this gives the essay a modern sound.

Third, it belongs to the family of essays about a place seen through a particular mood. In this sense it is related to the personal essays of Charles Lamb and Hazlitt, and later to the travel writing of D. H. Lawrence, Robert Byron and others who refused to praise places by the book. The tradition of the honest traveller who admits boredom, irritation and disappointment runs from Stevenson through to modern writers who complain about tourist crowds, hotel food and cancelled trains.

What descends from it is harder to say, since the essay is minor and not widely read. But the idea that a famous beauty spot can become a prison is one of the lasting insights of modern travel writing, and the claim that you cannot change the scene, only change one hole for another, anticipates a good deal of later reflection on tourism and restlessness.

Why does it matter today? Partly because it is short and pleasing, a quick example of how Stevenson turns a limitation into a style. Partly because it is a useful antidote to the cult of scenery. Most of all, it reminds us that what we see depends on who we are and how we are, and that being confined can sharpen the eye as well as dull it. In that sense, the small essay says something large about attention, and it is worth reading for that alone.
