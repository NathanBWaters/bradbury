The Three Hermits

Leo Tolstoy (1886) • 13 min read

“An Old Legend Current in the Volga District”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Leo Tolstoy published The Three Hermits in 1886, and he labeled it an old legend from the Volga district, meaning a tale he had picked up from ordinary Russian storytellers rather than invented himself. By then he was one of the most famous writers alive, but he had broken with the Russian Orthodox Church and was writing stories meant for peasants, printed cheaply and sold by the hundreds of thousands. The tale opens with a line from Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew, a warning against praying with empty repetition, as if God had to be talked into listening. A bishop is sailing to a famous monastery when he learns of three old hermits living on a tiny island, and he decides to teach them to pray properly. Listen for who ends up teaching whom, and for what the story suggests about religion, humility, and the gap between rules and sincerity.

An Old Legend Current in the Volga District

“And in praying use not vain repetitions, as the Gentiles do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking. Be not therefore like unto them: for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask Him.”

A Bishop was sailing from Archangel to the Solovétsk Monastery; and on the same vessel were a number of pilgrims on their way to visit the shrines at that place. The voyage was a smooth one. The wind favourable, and the weather fair. The pilgrims lay on deck, eating, or sat in groups talking to one another. The Bishop, too, came on deck, and as he was pacing up and down, he noticed a group of men standing near the prow and listening to a fisherman, who was pointing to the sea and telling them something. The Bishop stopped, and looked in the direction in which the man was pointing. He could see nothing, however, but the sea glistening in the sunshine. He drew nearer to listen, but when the man saw him, he took off his cap and was silent. The rest of the people also took off their caps, and bowed.

“Do not let me disturb you, friends,” said the Bishop. “I came to hear what this good man was saying.”

“The fisherman was telling us about the hermits,” replied one, a tradesman, rather bolder than the rest.

“What hermits?” asked the Bishop, going to the side of the vessel and seating himself on a box. “Tell me about them. I should like to hear. What were you pointing at?”

“Why, that little island you can just see over there,” answered the man, pointing to a spot ahead and a little to the right. “That is the island where the hermits live for the salvation of their souls.”

“Where is the island?” asked the Bishop. “I see nothing.”

“There, in the distance, if you will please look along my hand. Do you see that little cloud? Below it, and a bit to the left, there is just a faint streak. That is the island.”

The Bishop looked carefully, but his unaccustomed eyes could make out nothing but the water shimmering in the sun.

“I cannot see it,” he said. “But who are the hermits that live there?”

“They are holy men,” answered the fisherman. “I had long heard tell of them, but never chanced to see them myself till the year before last.”

And the fisherman related how once, when he was out fishing, he had been stranded at night upon that island, not knowing where he was. In the morning, as he wandered about the island, he came across an earth hut, and met an old man standing near it. Presently two others came out, and after having fed him, and dried his things, they helped him mend his boat.

“And what are they like?” asked the Bishop.

“One is a small man and his back is bent. He wears a priest’s cassock and is very old; he must be more than a hundred, I should say. He is so old that the white of his beard is taking a greenish tinge, but he is always smiling, and his face is as bright as an angel’s from heaven. The second is taller, but he also is very old. He wears a tattered, peasant coat. His beard is broad, and of a yellowish grey colour. He is a strong man. Before I had time to help him, he turned my boat over as if it were only a pail. He too, is kindly and cheerful. The third is tall, and has a beard as white as snow and reaching to his knees. He is stern, with overhanging eyebrows; and he wears nothing but a mat tied round his waist.”

“And did they speak to you?” asked the Bishop.

“For the most part they did everything in silence, and spoke but little even to one another. One of them would just give a glance, and the others would understand him. I asked the tallest whether they had lived there long. He frowned, and muttered something as if he were angry; but the oldest one took his hand and smiled, and then the tall one was quiet. The oldest one only said: ‘Have mercy upon us,’ and smiled.”

While the fisherman was talking, the ship had drawn nearer to the island.

“There, now you can see it plainly, if your Grace will please to look,” said the tradesman, pointing with his hand.

The Bishop looked, and now he really saw a dark streak⁠—which was the island. Having looked at it awhile, he left the prow of the vessel, and going to the stern, asked the helmsman:

“What island is that?”

“That one,” replied the man, “has no name. There are many such in this sea.”

“Is it true that there are hermits who live there for the salvation of their souls?”

“So it is said, your Grace, but I don’t know if it’s true. Fishermen say they have seen them; but of course they may only be spinning yarns.”

“I should like to land on the island and see these men,” said the Bishop. “How could I manage it?”

“The ship cannot get close to the island,” replied the helmsman, “but you might be rowed there in a boat. You had better speak to the captain.”

The captain was sent for and came.

“I should like to see these hermits,” said the Bishop. “Could I not be rowed ashore?”

The captain tried to dissuade him.

“Of course it could be done,” said he, “but we should lose much time. And if I might venture to say so to your Grace, the old men are not worth your pains. I have heard say that they are foolish old fellows, who understand nothing, and never speak a word, any more than the fish in the sea.”

“I wish to see them,” said the Bishop, “and I will pay you for your trouble and loss of time. Please let me have a boat.”

There was no help for it; so the order was given. The sailors trimmed the sails, the steersman put up the helm, and the ship’s course was set for the island. A chair was placed at the prow for the Bishop, and he sat there, looking ahead. The passengers all collected at the prow, and gazed at the island. Those who had the sharpest eyes could presently make out the rocks on it, and then a mud hut was seen. At last one man saw the hermits themselves. The captain brought a telescope and, after looking through it, handed it to the Bishop.

“It’s right enough. There are three men standing on the shore. There, a little to the right of that big rock.”

The Bishop took the telescope, got it into position, and he saw the three men: a tall one, a shorter one, and one very small and bent, standing on the shore and holding each other by the hand.

The captain turned to the Bishop.

“The vessel can get no nearer in than this, your Grace. If you wish to go ashore, we must ask you to go in the boat, while we anchor here.”

The cable was quickly let out, the anchor cast, and the sails furled. There was a jerk, and the vessel shook. Then a boat having been lowered, the oarsmen jumped in, and the Bishop descended the ladder and took his seat. The men pulled at their oars, and the boat moved rapidly towards the island. When they came within a stone’s throw, they saw three old men: a tall one with only a mat tied round his waist: a shorter one in a tattered peasant coat, and a very old one bent with age and wearing an old cassock⁠—all three standing hand in hand.

The oarsmen pulled in to the shore, and held on with the boathook while the Bishop got out.

The old men bowed to him, and he gave them his benediction, at which they bowed still lower. Then the Bishop began to speak to them.

“I have heard,” he said, “that you, godly men, live here saving your own souls, and praying to our Lord Christ for your fellow men. I, an unworthy servant of Christ, am called, by God’s mercy, to keep and teach His flock. I wished to see you, servants of God, and to do what I can to teach you, also.”

The old men looked at each other smiling, but remained silent.

“Tell me,” said the Bishop, “what you are doing to save your souls, and how you serve God on this island.”

The second hermit sighed, and looked at the oldest, the very ancient one. The latter smiled, and said:

“We do not know how to serve God. We only serve and support ourselves, servant of God.”

“But how do you pray to God?” asked the Bishop.

“We pray in this way,” replied the hermit. “Three are ye, three are we, have mercy upon us.”

And when the old man said this, all three raised their eyes to heaven, and repeated:

“Three are ye, three are we, have mercy upon us!”

The Bishop smiled.

“You have evidently heard something about the Holy Trinity,” said he. “But you do not pray aright. You have won my affection, godly men. I see you wish to please the Lord, but you do not know how to serve Him. That is not the way to pray; but listen to me, and I will teach you. I will teach you, not a way of my own, but the way in which God in the Holy Scriptures has commanded all men to pray to Him.”

And the Bishop began explaining to the hermits how God had revealed Himself to men; telling them of God the Father, and God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost.

“God the Son came down on earth,” said he, “to save men, and this is how He taught us all to pray. Listen, and repeat after me: ‘Our Father.’ ”

And the first old man repeated after him, “Our Father,” and the second said, “Our Father,” and the third said, “Our Father.”

“Which art in heaven,” continued the Bishop.

The first hermit repeated, “Which art in heaven,” but the second blundered over the words, and the tall hermit could not say them properly. His hair had grown over his mouth so that he could not speak plainly. The very old hermit, having no teeth, also mumbled indistinctly.

The Bishop repeated the words again, and the old men repeated them after him. The Bishop sat down on a stone, and the old men stood before him, watching his mouth, and repeating the words as he uttered them. And all day long the Bishop laboured, saying a word twenty, thirty, a hundred times over, and the old men repeated it after him. They blundered, and he corrected them, and made them begin again.

The Bishop did not leave off till he had taught them the whole of the Lord’s prayer so that they could not only repeat it after him, but could say it by themselves. The middle one was the first to know it, and to repeat the whole of it alone. The Bishop made him say it again and again, and at last the others could say it too.

It was getting dark, and the moon was appearing over the water, before the Bishop rose to return to the vessel. When he took leave of the old men, they all bowed down to the ground before him. He raised them, and kissed each of them, telling them to pray as he had taught them. Then he got into the boat and returned to the ship.

And as he sat in the boat and was rowed to the ship he could hear the three voices of the hermits loudly repeating the Lord’s prayer. As the boat drew near the vessel their voices could no longer be heard, but they could still be seen in the moonlight, standing as he had left them on the shore, the shortest in the middle, the tallest on the right, the middle one on the left. As soon as the Bishop had reached the vessel and got on board, the anchor was weighed and the sails unfurled. The wind filled them, and the ship sailed away, and the Bishop took a seat in the stern and watched the island they had left. For a time he could still see the hermits, but presently they disappeared from sight, though the island was still visible. At last it too vanished, and only the sea was to be seen, rippling in the moonlight.

The pilgrims lay down to sleep, and all was quiet on deck. The Bishop did not wish to sleep, but sat alone at the stern, gazing at the sea where the island was no longer visible, and thinking of the good old men. He thought how pleased they had been to learn the Lord’s prayer; and he thanked God for having sent him to teach and help such godly men.

So the Bishop sat, thinking, and gazing at the sea where the island had disappeared. And the moonlight flickered before his eyes, sparkling, now here, now there, upon the waves. Suddenly he saw something white and shining, on the bright path which the moon cast across the sea. Was it a seagull, or the little gleaming sail of some small boat? The Bishop fixed his eyes on it, wondering.

“It must be a boat sailing after us,” thought he, “but it is overtaking us very rapidly. It was far, far away a minute ago, but now it is much nearer. It cannot be a boat, for I can see no sail; but whatever it may be, it is following us, and catching us up.”

And he could not make out what it was. Not a boat, nor a bird, nor a fish! It was too large for a man, and besides a man could not be out there in the midst of the sea. The Bishop rose, and said to the helmsman:

“Look there, what is that, my friend? What is it?” the Bishop repeated, though he could now see plainly what it was⁠—the three hermits running upon the water, all gleaming white, their grey beards shining, and approaching the ship as quickly as though it were not moving.

The steersman looked and let go the helm in terror.

“Oh Lord! The hermits are running after us on the water as though it were dry land!”

The passengers hearing him, jumped up, and crowded to the stern. They saw the hermits coming along hand in hand, and the two outer ones beckoning the ship to stop. All three were gliding along upon the water without moving their feet. Before the ship could be stopped, the hermits had reached it, and raising their heads, all three as with one voice, began to say:

“We have forgotten your teaching, servant of God. As long as we kept repeating it we remembered, but when we stopped saying it for a time, a word dropped out, and now it has all gone to pieces. We can remember nothing of it. Teach us again.”

The Bishop crossed himself, and leaning over the ship’s side, said:

“Your own prayer will reach the Lord, men of God. It is not for me to teach you. Pray for us sinners.”

And the Bishop bowed low before the old men; and they turned and went back across the sea. And a light shone until daybreak on the spot where they were lost to sight.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The tale begins with a bishop on a calm sea, traveling with pilgrims to the Solovetsky Monastery, a famous holy place on islands in the far north. Out on deck he overhears a fisherman describing a small island where three hermits live, old men who are said to have saved their souls. The bishop is curious and a little condescending. He asks to be taken there and when the helmsman reluctantly agrees he is rowed ashore in a small boat.

The three hermits are touching. One is small and bent, one tall, and one very old, wearing nothing but a mat, and they greet him with simple smiles. When he asks how they serve God, they say they do not know how, they only serve and feed themselves. He asks how they pray, and they answer with their entire prayer, the words that Three are ye, three are we, have mercy upon us. The bishop is shocked at how crude this is, and sets out to teach them the Lord's Prayer. It is slow work. The hermits are old, toothless, and forgetful, and they keep stumbling, and he spends the whole day repeating each phrase until they can say it, then leaves them practicing.

That night, as his ship sails away, the bishop sits on deck and notes a glowing patch of moonlight on the water. Something is following the ship, moving fast, and it is not a boat or a bird. It is the three hermits, running over the water as if it were dry land, hand in hand, shining white. They call out that they have forgotten his teaching, that when they stopped repeating it a word dropped out and the whole thing fell apart, and they beg him to teach them again. The bishop crosses himself and bows low. He says that their own prayer will reach the Lord, and that it is not for him to teach them. They turn and go back across the sea, and a light shines at the spot until daybreak.

The meaning is in the reversal. The bishop is educated, powerful, and sure he knows the proper way to pray, and the hermits are ignorant of every formula. Yet the hermits can walk on water, an image that echoes Jesus and the apostle Peter in the Gospels, and the bishop cannot. The miracle is the story's way of saying which prayer God actually hears. The bishop's lesson was about words. The hermits' prayer, though clumsy, came from sincere hearts, and the bishop realizes that he has been trying to improve something that was not broken.

There are details worth noticing. The epigraph from Matthew warns against vain repetitions, yet the Lord's Prayer is itself repeated by the hermits until it goes to pieces, and in the end they rely on their own crude phrase. The story seems to put the weight on the heart behind the words, not on the words. Notice also that the bishop's humility comes at once. He does not argue, and he does not insist on his rank. That quick turning is what makes him, at the end, one of Tolstoy's rare attractive clergymen.

One last detail concerns the title word Old Legend. Tolstoy presents the story as something handed down, which gives it a certain authority and also places it at a distance. The bishop is no one in particular, the hermits have no names, and the island is simply an island. That anonymity is part of the meaning. Anyone could be the bishop, the one who is sure of the proper way, and anyone could be among the hermits, the ones whose own plain words may be enough.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of craft this is nearly flawless, and the reason is that it uses every element it has. The setting, a calm ship with idle pilgrims, gives the bishop an audience, and so makes the visit to the island feel like an act of vanity as well as charity. The island scene is quick and visual, three figures as simple as children's drawings. The day of teaching is told with a light, affectionate humor, with the old men trying and failing, and the bishop's patience turning gradually into a kind of pride in his work.

The ending is the best part. Tolstoy builds the suspense with a single, clear image. A shape on the water, too large to be a bird, drawing near faster than a ship, and the narrator's refusal to name it until the bishop sees for himself. The helmsman lets go of the wheel in terror. The hermits' request, so humble and so anxious, makes the miracle feel intimate rather than grand. And the last image, the light staying until dawn, is quiet and exact.

There are weaknesses. The hermits are almost too perfect. They are childlike, always smiling, with no flaws and no inner life, and the story depends on a miracle to settle an argument that is really about the nature of faith. A skeptic may feel the case is rigged. The bishop is also a mild target. He is a decent man, only mistaken, so the story's critique of the clergy is less sharp than Tolstoy's other writing on the church, which could be harsh.

There is a subtler problem, and the story does not quite face it. Jesus, in the verses right after the one quoted in the epigraph, gives his followers the Lord's Prayer as a model, which is exactly what the bishop is teaching. The point against empty repetition is fair, but the story makes the prayer itself look like the problem when it is really the attitude that matters. And the idea that formal knowledge counts for nothing could easily be used to dismiss all teaching. Tolstoy leans hard into a particular view of simple faith, and a reader who disagrees may find the tale a bit one-sided.

Even so, its brevity helps it. In a few pages it gives us a complete arc, vanity, correction, humility, and does not stay a moment past the point where it has said what it wants.

III. The Impact It Had

The story came out in 1886, in the same stretch of years as The Death of Ivan Ilyich and How Much Land Does a Man Need?, and it belongs to the cheap-edition series that Tolstoy and a publisher friend ran to put good stories in the hands of ordinary readers. These little books sold in huge numbers for a few pennies, and for many people in Russia they were the first writing of Tolstoy's they ever saw. It helped establish the idea that the author of great novels might also be a teller of tales for the poor.

It also fed into his conflict with the church. Tolstoy believed that institutional religion, with its rituals, its hierarchy, and its doctrines, had buried the simple teaching of Jesus. A story in which a bishop is taught by uneducated hermits reads like an illustration of that belief. The Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church excommunicated him in 1901, and while that had many causes, the attitude behind this story was part of it. I would be careful not to say that this one story caused anything, but it is a small, clear expression of the attitude that did.

Beyond Russia it has had a long life as a teaching story. It is retold in sermons, in collections of moral tales, and in books for children, and it has been included in many anthologies of Tolstoy's short work. Its central image, holy men running on water, has been borrowed and referred to in many places, and the idea that sincerity matters more than polish is one that many traditions have returned to. Its direct influence on other major writers is harder to trace, but its influence on how Tolstoy was read by general audiences is clear.

Because the story is so short and so easily retold, it is hard to separate its influence from the broader folk tradition it came from. Tolstoy was borrowing a legend rather than inventing one, and after he gave it a polished written form it re-entered oral circulation, passed along by preachers and teachers who may never have known where the version they told came from. That is arguably the best kind of influence a folk story can have, and it fits what Tolstoy wanted, which was for these stories to belong to everyone.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved the story first for its images. The hermits running over the water, gleaming white with beards shining, is among the most memorable pictures in the whole of Tolstoy's short fiction, and it seems to stay with people who read the story only once. It is a miracle told without fuss, and that makes it feel truer.

They love the humor and warmth. The scene of the old men repeating the Lord's Prayer is funny, since we can see them struggling, and yet it never mocks them. The reader is on the hermits' side from the start, and gradually on the bishop's too, because he proves capable of changing his mind.

Many also admire the story's gentleness about a hard point. Tolstoy could be fierce in his attacks on the church, but this is not a fierce story. The bishop is neither a hypocrite nor a villain. He is a good man who must learn something, and the lesson is given to him without humiliation. Readers often say that this is what makes the story persuasive, since it invites us to see ourselves in the bishop rather than to feel superior to him. Anyone who has tried to teach, to correct, or to improve someone else can recognize the temptation.

And many just love its simplicity. It can be read in ten minutes, and it reads like a legend, with the rhythm of a story told aloud. That oral quality makes it ideal for listening, which is how many people have met it.

Teachers love how well it holds up in a classroom. A group can talk for an hour about what the hermits' prayer means, whether the bishop was right to teach them, and what the miracle is really saying. Because the story takes no cheap shots at the bishop, believers of many kinds, and nonbelievers too, can find something in it. People from very different traditions have said that it gave voice to something they already felt, that a heartfelt, imperfect prayer counts for more than a perfect one said without attention.

There is also a pleasure in how the story is built like a legend, with its triplets. Three hermits, three saying their prayer, three times they say they forgot, the number repeating throughout, and that almost musical structure helps it feel old and true. Listeners tend to remember it long after the details of other stories have gone, which is an achievement for something so short.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The chief complaint from the religious side is that the story undervalues learning and tradition. Orthodox and other Christian critics have pointed out that liturgy and set prayers are not necessarily empty, and that the Lord's Prayer was given by Jesus himself. To them the story makes a false opposition between sincere feeling and shared forms of worship, and slights the church's role in preserving faith across generations.

From the literary side, critics have found the tale overly simple, a parable with a tidy message that leaves little room for the ambiguities of his great novels. The hermits are sometimes called sentimental figures, Tolstoy's idealized peasants in saintly form. This is a recurring complaint about his late work, that he romanticized the simple man while living in a manor house, and that the stories can feel like sermons wearing a costume.

There is also the doubt about the miracle. Tolstoy rejected miracles in his own theology, and some readers have wondered why a writer who thought the Gospels' supernatural events were later additions should end this story on a miracle. One answer is that the tale was a legend and he kept its form. Another is that the miracle works as a symbol rather than a claim. Either way, the question reveals a slight tension between Tolstoy's rational Christianity and his love of traditional storytelling.

Finally, there is the sheer ease of the lesson. Some find that the bishop gives up too easily, since a real cleric might have argued, and that the story's resolution comes before any real conflict has taken place.

A related objection concerns the portrait of the bishop's motives. The story suggests that the bishop is moved to visit the island partly out of curiosity and a sense of his own importance, but it never lets him be a real antagonist, and that mildness can feel like a dodge. A sharper story might have shown a bishop who truly resists the lesson, or a hermit who truly needs the instruction. As written, the conflict is settled by the hermits' miracle, and some readers want more of a struggle.

Others raise a social point, that the story flatters a particular picture of the poor as simple, holy, and content. In Tolstoy's Russia, many peasants were illiterate and desperately poor, and some later critics have worried about gentry writers who found spiritual perfection in other people's hardship. The hermits are not exactly peasants, but they belong to that tradition of the pure, uneducated saint, and the criticism applies. It is a fair question to ask what the hermits know that a bishop, with a lifetime of study and responsibility, cannot know.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Three Hermits belongs to an old family of stories about humble people who turn out to be holier than the learned. You find it in the Gospels, where Jesus says that the truth is hidden from the wise and revealed to little children, and in the lives of the saints, and in desert fathers' tales in which a famous teacher is corrected by an uneducated monk. Russian culture also has the figure of the holy fool, the person who seems foolish or simple but carries real spiritual insight.

Within Tolstoy's work it is part of the late short tales, with Where Love Is, God Is, and How Much Land Does a Man Need?, in which he used the folk tale to express his own version of Christianity. Its closest cousin in other literature may be the Hasidic tales told in the Jewish tradition, in which a simple man's honest prayer reaches heaven where a scholar's does not. There is also a resemblance to the story of the widow's mite in the Gospels. As to descendants, the idea of the learned person humbled by the simple one has become a staple of fable, children's stories, and films.

A small historical irony adds to its interest. The Solovetsky Monastery that the bishop is sailing to became, after the Russian Revolution, one of the first camps in the Soviet prison system, a place where many people suffered and died. A modern reader cannot help noticing that the holy island of the story would later become a place of terror. That has nothing to do with Tolstoy's intent, but it gives the opening an unexpected shadow.

It is worth reading now because its question remains alive. How much of religion, or any practice, is the form and how much is the sincerity behind it? The story does not settle that, but it poses it more gracefully than most arguments do, and in barely ten minutes. For listeners especially, its steady, almost chanted rhythm makes it a good companion for a quiet evening.
