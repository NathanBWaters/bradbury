Nightly Poem • Harlem Renaissance

The Creation

James Weldon Johnson (1920) • 6 min read

And God stepped out on space, and He looked around and said, 'I’m lonely—I’ll make me a world': Genesis reimagined through the thunderous cadence of the Black folk preacher.

Key Context & Why It Matters

James Weldon Johnson's "The Creation" retells the first chapter of Genesis as if a Black country preacher were telling it from the pulpit, and it has become one of the most loved American poems of the twentieth century. Johnson was a remarkable figure: a lawyer, a diplomat, a leader of the NAACP, and with his brother, the writer of the song "Lift Every Voice and Sing." During the 1910s and early 1920s he was part of the great flowering of Black arts we now call the Harlem Renaissance. He had grown weary of the old stage dialect that white audiences expected from Black poets, and he wanted a form that honored the real music of Black preaching without caricature. He found it in the oral sermon. This poem opened his 1927 collection God's Trombones. Its God is not a distant king but a lonely, hands-on creator who rolls up the sun like a ball of dough and kneels in the dust like a parent. Listen for the rhythm. It was made to be spoken aloud.

And God stepped out on space,

And he looked around and said:

I’m lonely—

I’ll make me a world.

And far as the eye of God could see

Darkness covered everything,

Blacker than a hundred midnights

Down in a cypress swamp.

Then God smiled,

And the light broke,

And the darkness rolled up on one side,

And the light stood shining on the other,

And God said: That’s good!

Then God reached out and took the light in his hands,

And God rolled the light around in his hands

Until he made the sun;

And he set that sun a-blazing in the heavens.

And the light that was left from making the sun

God gathered it up in a shining ball

And flung it against the darkness,

Spangling the night with the moon and stars.

Then down between

The darkness and the light

He hurled the world;

And God said: That’s good!

Then God himself stepped down—

And the sun was on his right hand,

And the moon was on his left;

The stars were clustered about his head,

And the earth was under his feet.

And God walked, and where he trod

His footsteps hollowed the valleys out

And bulged the mountains up.

Then he stopped and looked and saw

That the earth was hot and barren.

So God stepped over to the edge of the world

And he spat out the seven seas—

He batted his eyes, and the lightnings flashed—

He clapped his hands, and the thunders rolled—

And the waters above the earth came down,

The cooling waters came down.

Then the green grass sprouted,

And the little red flowers blossomed,

The pine tree pointed his finger to the sky,

And the oak spread out his arms,

The lakes cuddled down in the hollows of the ground,

And the rivers ran down to the sea;

And God smiled again,

And the rainbow appeared,

And curled itself around his shoulder.

Then God raised his arm and he waved his hand

Over the sea and over the land,

And He said: Bring forth! Bring forth!

And quicker than God could drop his hand,

Fishes and fowls

And beasts and birds

Swam the rivers and the seas,

Roamed the forests and the woods,

And split the air with their wings.

And God said, That’s good!

Then God walked around,

And God looked around

On all that he had made.

He looked at his sun,

And he looked at his moon,

And he looked at his little stars;

He looked on his world

With all its living things,

And God said: I’m lonely still.

Then God sat down—

On the side of a hill where he could think;

By a deep, wide river he sat down;

With his head in his hands,

God thought and thought,

Till he thought: I’ll make me a man!

Up from the bed of the river

God scooped the clay;

And by the bank of the river

He kneeled him down;

And there the great God Almighty

Who lit the sun and fixed it in the sky,

Who flung the stars to the most far corner of the night,

Who rounded the earth in the middle of his hand;

This Great God,

Like a mammy bending over her baby,

Kneeled down in the dust

Toiling over a lump of clay

Till he shaped it in his own image;

Then into it he blew the breath of life,

And man became a living soul.

Amen. Amen.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, "The Creation" is simple. God is lonely, so he makes a world. He makes light, the sun, the moon and stars, the land, the seas, the plants and animals, and finally, because he is still lonely, he makes a man out of river clay. That is the order of Genesis, the first book of the Bible, which many of Johnson's first listeners knew by heart. What changes is the telling. Johnson takes a text that usually sounds distant and majestic and turns God into someone you could almost sit beside.

The most important idea in the poem is the very first one: "I'm lonely— / I'll make me a world." In the Bible, God never gives a reason for creating. Johnson gives one, and it is a human reason. Creation is not an act of power but of need, the way a person might plant a garden or start a family. Notice that God says he is lonely twice. After he has made the sun, the moon, the stars, the waters and all the animals, he looks over everything and says, "I'm lonely still." The whole of nature, however beautiful, is not enough. Only something that can answer him back will do. That makes the making of man the real goal of the poem, and it explains why the poem slows down when it reaches him.

Look at how the pace changes. For most of the poem God works with sweeping, almost cartoonish gestures. He hurls the world, spits out the seven seas, bats his eyes so the lightning flashes, claps his hands so the thunder rolls. Then he sits down by a river with his head in his hands and thinks. When he finally makes man, the poem gets quiet and tender. God kneels in the dust "Like a mammy bending over her baby, / Kneeled down in the dust / Toiling over a lump of clay." The mightiest figure imaginable is suddenly doing the humblest, most patient kind of work, shaping something small by hand.

There are layers underneath. For Black Americans who had lived through slavery and its aftermath, a creator who gets down in the dirt and labors lovingly over his creation carried a pointed message. The people the world treated as disposable were made by hand, in God's own image, with God's own breath. The poem never says this outright. It does not need to. When you hear the last lines, "Then into it he blew the breath of life, / And man became a living soul," followed by the preacher's "Amen. Amen," you are meant to feel both the ancient story and the dignity of the people telling it.

A smaller thing worth catching is how earthy the heaven is. The pine tree "pointed his finger to the sky," the oak "spread out his arms," the lakes "cuddled down" like children. Nature is alive and a little playful. Johnson is quietly saying that this is a world made by a God with a body and a sense of humor, and a love of what he has made.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of craft, "The Creation" is close to a masterpiece, and the reason is that Johnson solved a hard problem. He wanted to write poems in the voice of Black preachers without using the broken, comic spelling that earlier dialect poets had leaned on. In his preface to God's Trombones he explained that this style had become trapped in two moods, humor and pathos, and was no longer a good tool for serious feeling. So he kept the rhythm, the images and the spoken swing of the sermon, but let the words stand on the page in ordinary spelling. The result sounds like speech and reads like poetry.

The poem has no regular rhyme and no fixed meter. Instead it moves by the breath. Lines are short when the action is quick and long when God is expansive. Repetition does much of the work: "And God said: That's good!" comes back like a refrain, and phrases are stacked up in the way a preacher builds momentum, "Who lit the sun and fixed it in the sky, / Who flung the stars to the most far corner of the night, / Who rounded the earth in the middle of his hand." Each line is bigger than the last, and then the poem drops to "This Great God, / Like a mammy bending over her baby." That drop is the best stroke in the poem. It works like a turn in a sermon, where the preacher builds toward thunder and then goes soft.

The images are wonderful. "Blacker than a hundred midnights / Down in a cypress swamp" gives you darkness through a specific, local, almost frightening place. The sun is made by rolling light in God's hands, the stars are what is left over, like scraps of dough, and the rainbow curls around God's shoulder like a pet. These are the images of someone who thinks with his hands, and they keep a very old story fresh.

Now the weaknesses, because there are a few. The comparison of God to "a mammy" is the line modern readers stumble over most. In 1920 it would have been heard as warm, a picture of tenderness. Today the word "mammy" carries the weight of a plantation stereotype, the loyal enslaved nurse of white fantasy, and some listeners feel it jars against the dignity the rest of the poem builds. It is worth reading it as Johnson meant it, as a plain image of a caregiver bent over a child, but the word has a history he could not fully control.

The poem can also feel a little neat. God's emotional arc is easy to follow, and the sentiment is large and warm without much shadow. There is no hint here of the trouble that follows creation, no fall, no pain. That is partly the point, since Johnson wanted a hymn, not a debate. But it means the poem asks to be loved more than it asks to be argued with. And on the page, away from a speaking voice, it loses some of its power. It was written for the ear, and it is best heard.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's first big impact was on Johnson himself. He had already published verse, and his novel The Autobiography of an Ex-Colored Man had appeared anonymously in 1912, but "The Creation" and the sermons gathered around it became his best-known poetic achievement. When God's Trombones came out in 1927 it was a landmark of the Harlem Renaissance, with striking illustrations by the painter Aaron Douglas. The collection is made of seven sermons in verse, and "The Creation" is the one that opens the book and sets its tone.

Johnson's real contribution was to show that the Black church had produced a living art form worth taking seriously on the page. For generations, the sermons of Black preachers had been treated, by many white observers, as quaint or comic. Johnson, who had himself heard such preachers and been moved by one in particular, treated them as folk artists working in a great oral tradition, and he asked other writers to see them the same way. He made the same argument as an editor. In 1922 he put together The Book of American Negro Poetry, and its long preface is one of the key documents of the era, insisting that Black poets could draw on their own speech and music without being stuck in imitation.

The poem's influence reaches well beyond literature. It is performed constantly. Actors and preachers have recited it in churches, schools and concert halls, and it has been set to music and read at ceremonies. Many readers first meet it in school, where its refrains and vivid images make it unusually easy to read aloud. It has also been adapted for the stage and for recordings, and part of its appeal to performers is that it comes with a built-in script of pauses and crescendos.

Among writers, the example of God's Trombones helped open a path for poets who wanted to build verse from Black speech and church rhythm without caricature. You can hear its legacy in later poets who borrow the cadence of the pulpit, and in the larger idea, so important to Black American writing, that vernacular speech is a real literary resource rather than a lowly one. Johnson sits as a bridge figure here, between earlier dialect poets like Paul Laurence Dunbar, whom he admired but wanted to move beyond, and the generation of the Harlem Renaissance and after.

The poem also still shapes how religious art is imagined. It is a famous example of retelling scripture in a particular community's voice, and it reminds readers that the Bible has always been told differently by different peoples. Teachers use it to talk about oral tradition, the sermon as a literary form, and the Harlem Renaissance itself. For many Americans it is the poem that made the Book of Genesis feel like it was happening in someone's backyard, which is a pretty remarkable thing for a poem about the beginning of the universe to do.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing readers love is the voice. You do not need to know anything about poetry to be caught by "And God stepped out on space, / And he looked around and said: / I'm lonely." The opening is plain, strange and funny all at once. It immediately makes God feel like a character in a story rather than a statue in a church, and that sense of warmth carries through the whole poem.

People also love how physical it is. God does not simply command things into being. He rolls the light between his palms, flings a shining ball into the dark, hurls the world, tramps across the earth so that his footsteps leave valleys and mountains. This is creation as handiwork and as play. For a child, it is delightful. For an adult, it has a deeper pull, because it suggests that making things, working with your hands and thinking hard about what to make, is godlike in itself. The scene where God sits by the river "With his head in his hands" and "thought and thought" until he decides to make a man is one many readers remember as the heart of the poem. It is a picture of the loneliness behind all invention.

Another reason for its popularity is that it works for almost everyone. Believers love it because it is reverent underneath the playfulness. Nonbelievers often love it because it can be read as a poem about the human need for company and meaning. Teachers love it because it can be performed. And many Black readers have treasured it because it takes a voice they know from church, from family, from home, and treats it as the material of serious art. That recognition matters a great deal. For them it is not just a clever retelling but a statement of pride.

Writers and performers have long admired the control behind its apparent ease. Johnson makes a free-flowing, speech-like poem that never wanders. It has a clear shape: a lonely beginning, a long middle of making, a second loneliness, and a final, tender act. Because the structure is so clean, an audience can follow it by ear, which is exactly what a sermon needs.

And then there is the ending. After all the cosmic noise, the poem closes on a quiet moment, one lump of clay and one breath. "Amen. Amen." is both a church response and a quiet sign that the speaker believes what he has said. Many readers report that the last few lines make their throats tighten even on a tenth hearing. That is a rare quality in a poem, and it is probably the strongest sign that the poem works. It is not admired from a distance. It is felt, and then it is passed along, by voice, to the next listener.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

It is worth saying first that "The Creation" has been far more loved than attacked. It has never been the target of a famous hostile review. The criticisms that exist are mostly questions about particular choices, and some come from readers who respect the poem deeply.

The most frequent concern is the "mammy" simile. Many modern readers find it troubling to compare the Almighty to a figure so tied up with plantation nostalgia and racial stereotype. Defenders point out that Johnson was a proud, accomplished Black man writing for Black audiences as well as white ones, and that the image in context is about patient tenderness, not servility. Critics reply that intentions are one thing and associations another, and that the word carries baggage the poem has to bear. Both views are reasonable, and the debate is a good illustration of how language changes over a century.

A second criticism concerns the idea of an authentic folk voice. Johnson was not himself a country preacher. He was a highly educated, cosmopolitan writer who studied at Atlanta University and later lived in New York and abroad as a diplomat. Some have wondered whether turning the oral sermon into polished written verse involves a kind of translation that smooths out its roughness. Johnson was candid about this. He said he was not trying to copy a dialect but to capture the spirit and cadence of the old-time preacher. Still, there is a real question about how much is gained and lost when a living spoken art is tidied up for the printed page.

There is also a broader debate about the poem's politics. During the Harlem Renaissance and afterward, some Black writers and critics argued about how much art should serve the cause of racial uplift and how much it should simply be art. Younger, more rebellious writers sometimes found the older generation of Renaissance figures too polite or too eager to please. Johnson, a civil rights leader as well as a poet, could look cautious to them. A poem as warm and consoling as this one can seem, to a sharper reader, to hold back its anger. It offers dignity but little protest.

Finally, some readers simply find the poem too sweet. Its God is so friendly and its world so cozy that it can read as a nursery version of Genesis. It does not wrestle with suffering, doubt or injustice the way some of Johnson's other sermons in the book do, such as the one about the Judgment Day. These are fair points. They are worth keeping in mind, but they rarely stop anyone from being moved when the poem is read aloud by a good voice.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"The Creation" belongs to several traditions at once, and that is part of why it feels so rich. It is a Harlem Renaissance poem, part of the burst of Black artistic energy in the 1920s that also produced Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, Countee Cullen and Jean Toomer. It is also a descendant of the African American sermon and the spiritual, those oral forms that carried a people's history and hope through slavery and beyond. And it is, in a looser sense, part of the long Western habit of retelling the Bible, running from the medieval mystery plays through Milton's Paradise Lost.

Set beside Milton, the contrast is instructive. Milton's God is vast, solemn and remote, and his creation is told in grand, learned Latinate verse. Johnson's God is close, funny and lonely, and his verse sounds like a person talking. Both are trying to explain why anything exists, but one does it from the heights and the other from a hillside by a river. The Bible itself is closer to Johnson in spirit than many people realize, since the second creation story in Genesis has God shaping man from dust with his hands.

Closer in time, you can compare it with other poets who worked from oral and folk traditions. Langston Hughes drew on blues and jazz. Sterling Brown used the speech and songs of Southern Black life. Walt Whitman, much earlier, had tried to put American speech into free verse. Johnson's special achievement was to find a voice for the pulpit that was neither a joke nor a copy of English poetry. After him, it became easier for writers to believe that spoken Black language could carry ambition.

What descends from it? Obviously, a whole tradition of poems that sound like sermons, and of sermons that are treated as poems. You can hear echoes in civil rights oratory, whose rhythms of repetition and rising emotion are of the same family, and in later spoken-word and performance poetry. The poem is also a staple of dramatic recitation, which means it keeps being rediscovered by new voices.

Why read it now? Partly because it is short, vivid and satisfying, and takes only a few minutes aloud. Partly because it shows how one of the oldest stories we have can be made new simply by changing who tells it. And partly because of its central insight, which has not aged. The poem proposes that behind the whole universe is not an indifferent force but a being who felt alone and wanted company, who knelt in the dust and made someone out of mud and breath. You do not have to be religious to find that an unforgettable picture of why we make things, and why we need one another.
