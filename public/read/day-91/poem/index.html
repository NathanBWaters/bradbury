The Creation

James Weldon Johnson (1920) • 6 min read

And God stepped out on space, and He looked around and said, 'I’m lonely—I’ll make me a world': Genesis reimagined through the thunderous cadence of the Black folk preacher.

Key Context & Why It Matters

First published in The Freeman in December 1920 and later made the opening masterpiece of Johnson’s celebrated 1927 collection God’s Trombones: Seven Negro Sermons in Verse, 'The Creation' was inspired by a tour-de-force sermon Johnson heard an African American preacher deliver in a Kansas City church in 1918. Belonging to the Harlem Renaissance tradition and categorized under Harlem Renaissance, 'The Creation' (1920) unfolds across 91 verbatim lines in 12 stanzas. Performed by Paul Robeson, Cicely Tyson, Morgan Freeman, and church choirs across the globe, 'The Creation' is one of the supreme dramatic performance poems in American literature. Through ninety-one lines of dynamic, James Weldon Johnson fuses formal precision with emotional immediacy, making the poem an enduring cornerstone of the poetic canon.

And God stepped out on space,

And he looked around and said:

I’m lonely—

I’ll make me a world.

And far as the eye of God could see

Darkness covered everything,

Blacker than a hundred midnights

Down in a cypress swamp.

Then God smiled,

And the light broke,

And the darkness rolled up on one side,

And the light stood shining on the other,

And God said: That’s good!

Then God reached out and took the light in his hands,

And God rolled the light around in his hands

Until he made the sun;

And he set that sun a-blazing in the heavens.

And the light that was left from making the sun

God gathered it up in a shining ball

And flung it against the darkness,

Spangling the night with the moon and stars.

Then down between

The darkness and the light

He hurled the world;

And God said: That’s good!

Then God himself stepped down—

And the sun was on his right hand,

And the moon was on his left;

The stars were clustered about his head,

And the earth was under his feet.

And God walked, and where he trod

His footsteps hollowed the valleys out

And bulged the mountains up.

Then he stopped and looked and saw

That the earth was hot and barren.

So God stepped over to the edge of the world

And he spat out the seven seas—

He batted his eyes, and the lightnings flashed—

He clapped his hands, and the thunders rolled—

And the waters above the earth came down,

The cooling waters came down.

Then the green grass sprouted,

And the little red flowers blossomed,

The pine tree pointed his finger to the sky,

And the oak spread out his arms,

The lakes cuddled down in the hollows of the ground,

And the rivers ran down to the sea;

And God smiled again,

And the rainbow appeared,

And curled itself around his shoulder.

Then God raised his arm and he waved his hand

Over the sea and over the land,

And He said: Bring forth! Bring forth!

And quicker than God could drop his hand,

Fishes and fowls

And beasts and birds

Swam the rivers and the seas,

Roamed the forests and the woods,

And split the air with their wings.

And God said, That’s good!

Then God walked around,

And God looked around

On all that he had made.

He looked at his sun,

And he looked at his moon,

And he looked at his little stars;

He looked on his world

With all its living things,

And God said: I’m lonely still.

Then God sat down—

On the side of a hill where he could think;

By a deep, wide river he sat down;

With his head in his hands,

God thought and thought,

Till he thought: I’ll make me a man!

Up from the bed of the river

God scooped the clay;

And by the bank of the river

He kneeled him down;

And there the great God Almighty

Who lit the sun and fixed it in the sky,

Who flung the stars to the most far corner of the night,

Who rounded the earth in the middle of his hand;

This Great God,

Like a mammy bending over her baby,

Kneeled down in the dust

Toiling over a lump of clay

Till he shaped it in his own image;

Then into it he blew the breath of life,

And man became a living soul.

Amen. Amen.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

First published in The Freeman in December 1920 and later made the opening masterpiece of Johnson’s celebrated 1927 collection God’s Trombones: Seven Negro Sermons in Verse, 'The Creation' was inspired by a tour-de-force sermon Johnson heard an African American preacher deliver in a Kansas City church in 1918. Within the broader trajectory of Harlem Renaissance, James Weldon Johnson composed 'The Creation' (1920) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"And God stepped out on space,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, James Weldon Johnson grounded these 91 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'The Creation' lies cosmic creation driven not by cold geometric decree, but by warm relational longing ('I’m lonely— / I’ll make me a world') and maternal tenderness: God rolls the light in His hands to make the sun, spits out the seven seas, bats His eyes to make lightning, walks the green earth leaving lakes in His footprints, and finally kneels down in the clay by a river bed like a 'mammy bending over her baby' to shape man and blow into him the breath of life. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"The lakes cuddled down in the hollows of the ground,"—James Weldon Johnson develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, James Weldon Johnson constructs 'The Creation' using ninety-one lines of dynamic, unrhymed rhythmic free verse across twelve stanzas—deliberately avoiding caricature dialect spelling in favor of the trombone-like brass cadences, dramatic pauses, biblical anaphora, and physical anthropomorphism of the traditional Black pulpit. Spanning 91 lines across 12 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how James Weldon Johnson balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "And God stepped out on space," and "Amen. Amen." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Performed by Paul Robeson, Cicely Tyson, Morgan Freeman, and church choirs across the globe, 'The Creation' is one of the supreme dramatic performance poems in American literature. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'The Creation' has remained a touchstone of the Harlem Renaissance canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to James Weldon Johnson’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form. From nineteenth- and twentieth-century critical debates to contemporary anthologies, James Weldon Johnson’s 'The Creation' retains all of its original freshness and authority.
