Discourses I.1: Of the Things Which Are in Our Power, and Not in Our Power

Epictetus (c. 108 AD) • 7 min read

You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This is the very first chapter of the Discourses, the classroom lectures of Epictetus, a Greek philosopher who lived in the Roman Empire around the year 100. Epictetus began life as a slave, was lame, and later became one of the most respected teachers of Stoicism, an ancient school that taught calm, courage and self-command. He never wrote anything himself. A devoted student named Arrian took notes, and that is why we have these talks, roughly as he delivered them, in blunt and sometimes funny spoken language. The chapter lays down the idea the whole Stoic way of life rests on: some things are up to us, and most things are not. Your judgments and choices belong to you. Your body, your money, your reputation and other people do not. Epictetus argues that nearly all our misery comes from mixing the two up. The idea later shaped Marcus Aurelius, modern therapy, and a military prisoner in Vietnam. As you listen, watch for the stories of men who faced Nero and kept their composure.

Of all the faculties (except that which I shall soon mention), you will find not one which is capable of contemplating itself, and, consequently, not capable either of approving or disapproving. How far does the grammatic art possess the contemplating power? As far as forming a judgment about what is written and spoken. And how far music? As far as judging about melody. Does either of them then contemplate itself? By no means. But when you must write something to your friend, grammar will tell you what words you should write; but whether you should write or not, grammar will not tell you. And so it is with music as to musical sounds; but whether you should sing at the present time and play on the lute, or do neither, music will not tell you. What faculty then will tell you? That which contemplates both itself and all other things. And what is this faculty? The rational faculty; for this is the only faculty that we have received which examines itself, what it is, and what power it has, and what is the value of this gift, and examines all other faculties: for what else is there which tells us that golden things are beautiful, for they do not say so themselves? Evidently it is the faculty which is capable of judging of appearances. What else judges of music, grammar, and the other faculties, proves their uses, and points out the occasions for using them? Nothing else.

As then it was fit to be so, that which is best of all and supreme over all is the only thing which the gods have placed in our power, the right use of appearances; but all other things they have not placed in our power. Was it because they did not choose? I indeed think that, if they had been able, they would have put these other things also in our power, but they certainly could not. For as we exist on the earth, and are bound to such a body and to such companions, how was it possible for us not to be hindered as to these things by externals?

But what says Zeus? “Epictetus, if it were possible, I would have made both your little body and your little property free and not exposed to hindrance. But now be not ignorant of this: this body is not yours, but it is clay finely tempered. And since I was not able to do for you what I have mentioned, I have given you a small portion of us, this faculty of pursuing an object and avoiding it, and the faculty of desire and aversion, and, in a word, the faculty of using the appearances of things; and if you will take care of this faculty and consider it your only possession, you will never be hindered, never meet with impediments; you will not lament, you will not blame, you will not flatter any person.”

Well, do these seem to you small matters? I hope not. Be content with them then and pray to the gods. But now when it is in our power to look after one thing, and to attach ourselves to it, we prefer to look after many things, and to be bound to many things, to the body and to property, and to brother and to friend, and to child and to slave. Since then we are bound to many things, we are depressed by them and dragged down. For this reason, when the weather is not fit for sailing, we sit down and torment ourselves, and continually look out to see what wind is blowing. It is north. What is that to us? When will the west wind blow? When it shall choose, my good man, or when it shall please Aeolus; for God has not made you the manager of the winds, but Aeolus. What then? We must make the best use that we can of the things which are in our power, and use the rest according to their nature. What is their nature then? As God may please.

Must I then alone have my head cut off? What, would you have all men lose their heads that you may be consoled? Will you not stretch out your neck as Lateranus did at Rome when Nero ordered him to be beheaded? For when he had stretched out his neck, and received a feeble blow, which made him draw it in for a moment, he stretched it out again. And a little before, when he was visited by Epaphroditus, Nero’s freedman, who asked him about the cause of offense which he had given, he said, “If I choose to tell anything, I will tell your master.”

What then should a man have in readiness in such circumstances? What else than this? What is mine, and what is not mine; and what is permitted to me, and what is not permitted to me. I must die. Must I then die lamenting? I must be put in chains. Must I then also lament? I must go into exile. Does any man then hinder me from going with smiles and cheerfulness and contentment? Tell me the secret which you possess. I will not, for this is in my power. But I will put you in chains. Man, what are you talking about? Me in chains? You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower. I will throw you into prison. My poor body, you mean. I will cut your head off. When then have I told you that my head alone cannot be cut off? These are the things which philosophers should meditate on, which they should write daily, in which they should exercise themselves.

Thrasea used to say, I would rather be killed today than banished tomorrow. What then did Rufus say to him? If you choose death as the heavier misfortune, how great is the folly of your choice? But if, as the lighter, who has given you the choice? Will you not study to be content with that which has been given to you?

What then did Agrippinus say? He said, “I am not a hindrance to myself.” When it was reported to him that his trial was going on in the Senate, he said, “I hope it may turn out well; but it is the fifth hour of the day”⁠—this was the time when he was used to exercise himself and then take the cold bath⁠—“let us go and take our exercise.” After he had taken his exercise, one comes and tells him, You have been condemned. To banishment, he replies, or to death? To banishment. What about my property? It is not taken from you. Let us go to Aricia then, he said, and dine.

This it is to have studied what a man ought to study; to have made desire, aversion, free from hindrance, and free from all that a man would avoid. I must die. If now, I am ready to die. If, after a short time, I now dine because it is the dinner-hour; after this I will then die. How? Like a man who gives up what belongs to another.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Epictetus opens in an odd way, with a question about how a mind can examine itself. He points out that no skill can judge itself. Grammar can tell you how to spell a word in a letter to a friend, but it cannot tell you whether you should write the letter. Music can tell you how to play the lute, but it cannot tell you whether this is the right moment to play. Something else has to make those calls, and that something is reason, the faculty that looks at everything, including itself. Specifically, it is the faculty that judges "appearances," his word for the way things strike us. A thing shows up in your mind as frightening, or insulting, or desirable, and reason is what decides whether to accept that first impression.

From there he makes the move that defines the whole chapter. Of everything in life, the gods placed only one thing fully in our power: the right use of appearances. Everything else, the body, property, family, friends, reputation, even our own lifespan, is tangled up with the outside world, and the outside world cannot be ordered around. Epictetus has Zeus speak to him directly in a small imaginary scene. Zeus says, in effect, that he would have liked to make the body and property free too, but could not. The body is "clay finely tempered," not truly yours. What you were given instead is a small portion of the divine itself, the ability to choose, to reach for things and turn away from them, and to handle impressions well.

Then comes the diagnosis. We are unhappy because we attach ourselves to many things we do not control, and so we are dragged down by each of them. His example is the sailor tormenting himself over the wind. The winds belong to Aeolus, he says, not to you.

The second half turns the idea into a drill for hard moments, and it is where the chapter gets its force. Epictetus imagines a tyrant threatening him. I will put you in chains. You may fetter my leg, he answers, but my will not even Zeus can overpower. I will throw you into prison. That is my poor body you mean. I will cut off your head. When did I ever say my head was the one part of me that could not be cut off?

The closing story of Agrippinus is the picture of how this looks in practice. Told that his trial is under way in the Senate, he says he hopes it goes well, but it is time for his exercise and cold bath. Told he has been condemned, he asks whether it is to death or exile. Exile. Is his property taken? No. Then, he says, let us go to Aricia and dine.

A first-time reader may miss that this is not a call to passivity. Epictetus still wants you to act, to write the letter, to sail the ship. What he wants removed is the demand that the result obey you. That is the difference between effort and anxiety, and the whole chapter is built to make you feel it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As rhetoric, this chapter is very good, and much of the credit goes to its pacing. It starts with a dry, almost technical point about faculties that cannot examine themselves, which could easily put a listener off. Then it turns it into a theology, then into a theatrical dialogue with Zeus, then into a series of sharp little exchanges with an imaginary tyrant, and finally into a short, funny, human story. Each step makes the abstract idea more concrete.

The tyrant dialogue is the best piece of writing here. It works because it is so quick and so cheeky. Epictetus does not argue that chains are pleasant or that death is nothing. He simply keeps correcting the grammar of the threat. You are threatening my leg, not me. This is a clever trick of redescription, and it is memorable because it can be repeated in your head at three in the morning.

The weaknesses are real, though. The opening argument, that reason alone can judge reason, is a little too neat. It moves from "no skill judges itself" to "reason is the only thing in our power" without much of a bridge, and a skeptical reader could object that our judgments are shaped by upbringing, mood and the people around us, so they are not so cleanly ours. Epictetus half admits this elsewhere by insisting that the ability needs training, but here he states the claim with more confidence than he earns.

There is also a hardness in the examples. He speaks of loved ones in the same breath as property. "Brother and friend, and child" sit in a list with "slave" and the body, all of them things that drag you down. Modern readers often find this chilling, and it is hard to defend it as simple good sense. A parent who is calm at a child's illness because the child is "not in my power" has, many would say, lost something that makes a life worth living. The chapter has no room for grief that is a form of love.

And the stories of Lateranus and Agrippinus, while inspiring, are selected from the survivors of a very specific kind of danger: Roman aristocrats facing the emperor. They sound better than they would feel.

Even so, the sheer practicality of the method keeps this chapter on the list of the best short pieces of moral writing. It is unsentimental, quick, and it asks the reader to do something rather than merely agree. Where it is weakest, it is weak the way a strong, confident teacher is weak: he gives the medicine at full dose and trusts you to adjust it. That makes it sometimes severe, but also very clear.

III. The Impact It Had

The Discourses were never published by Epictetus, so their influence is in a way an accident of loyalty. Arrian, a young Greek who later became a Roman governor and a historian of Alexander the Great, attended Epictetus's school at Nicopolis in northwestern Greece and wrote down what he heard. He later said he had not meant to make a book of it, which is partly why the voice is so alive. Four of the original eight books survive, along with a short handbook of the main ideas called the Enchiridion, which Arrian also compiled. This first chapter, with its split between what is and is not in our power, is the foundation of both.

The first great reader was probably Marcus Aurelius, the emperor, who in his private notebook, now called the Meditations, credits his teacher Rusticus for lending him a copy of Epictetus's lectures. Much of the calm, self-addressed tone of Marcus's writing is Epictetus's training turned inward. Through the late Roman world, Epictetus was widely read, and in the Middle Ages his handbook was adapted by Christian monks. Its ideas of detachment and acceptance of providence fit comfortably into monastic life, and a version of the Enchiridion was reworked for use in religious communities.

The twentieth century gave him two famous second lives. In the 1960s, the American psychologists Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, who founded rational emotive therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy, both said they had drawn on Epictetus. The central idea is the same one that opens the Discourses: it is our judgments about events, not the events themselves, that disturb us. Much of modern cognitive therapy, which teaches people to catch a distressing thought and examine it, is a clinical version of treating appearances with suspicion.

The second life belongs to Admiral James Stockdale, a navy pilot shot down over North Vietnam in 1965 who spent about seven and a half years as a prisoner of war, much of it in torture and isolation. Before his capture he had read Epictetus under the guidance of a philosophy professor. In captivity, Stockdale later wrote and said, the idea that you control your own will but not your body or circumstances was what kept him intact. He wrote about this in essays and lectures, and the story brought Epictetus to a very wide audience, including the military.

More recently, the broad revival of Stoicism has made this chapter common reading. Books and online communities promote what is often called the dichotomy of control, a sentence that sums up the chapter's opening lines. The phrase is modern, but the habit it describes, sorting your worries into "mine" and "not mine," is straight out of this text.

In schools, the chapter is often taught as a primer on Stoic ethics, and it serves historians as a window into the dangers of life under Nero, naming real men who lived and died under tyranny.

IV. What Readers Loved

What people have loved in this chapter is, first of all, its usefulness. Most philosophy asks you to consider an idea. Epictetus asks you to try it out before breakfast. Readers across nearly two thousand years have reported that this single move, asking whether a given worry is within their power, has actually changed how a bad day feels. That is a rare thing for a text that old to claim, and many readers have said so in their own words.

Admirers also love the voice. Epictetus does not sound like a professor handing down conclusions. He sounds like a sharp, slightly sarcastic coach who has heard every excuse. "My good man," he says to the person fretting about the wind, and the little phrase tells you he has seen this before and finds it a bit funny. This humor makes the severity bearable. He lets the reader laugh at their own foolishness, and that laughter is a first step toward changing.

Another loved quality is the dignity he gives to ordinary people. Epictetus was born a slave, lived with a damaged leg, and had no property to speak of. His teaching says that the inner freedom a person needs is available to the poor, the sick and the powerless just as much as to the rich. For a reader in a tight spot, there is something very moving about hearing this from someone who actually lived through the tight spot, and not from a comfortable aristocrat. This is why prisoners, soldiers and people in hospital have turned to him so often.

The Agrippinus story in particular has delighted generations. It is a few sentences long, it has an almost comic timing, and it leaves a picture in the mind: a man receiving his sentence and asking, in effect, where is lunch. Readers have enjoyed it as an image of grace under pressure. The same goes for Lateranus stretching his neck out a second time after the first blow fell weakly, a story that is shocking but also, in a grim way, almost admirable.

Many people also love how compact the chapter is. It lays out a whole philosophy of daily life in about a thousand words.

Finally, there is the consolation. People who love this chapter frequently speak of it as something they return to in a crisis. The reasoning is that if you cannot control the outcome, you are released from the exhausting job of controlling it, and what is left is a clear, small task: act well and accept the rest. For anyone worn out by anxiety, that is a real relief. Epictetus does not promise that things will turn out fine. He promises that you can be fine whether or not they do, and a great many readers have found that promise worth the harshness that comes with it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The best-known objection to Epictetus came from Blaise Pascal, the seventeenth-century French mathematician and religious thinker. In a conversation recorded about his reading, Pascal praised Epictetus for understanding duty but charged him with pride. In Pascal's view, telling people that they can master themselves by willpower alone ignores human weakness and our need for grace. A philosophy that makes you the owner of your own peace, he thought, flatters the reader. The charge has echoed ever since, in various forms.

A second line of criticism is about emotion. Critics have long argued that Stoic detachment asks people to cut off their feelings for others. If you train yourself to treat your children, friends and spouse as things you do not control and should not be too attached to, you may protect yourself from pain but also from love. The philosopher Martha Nussbaum has written in detail about the Stoic effort to remove strong emotion and has asked whether the resulting life is truly better. Many readers, hearing a parent told not to grieve, feel that something has gone wrong.

A third criticism is political. If a person is taught that only their inner attitude matters, they may stop caring about changing unjust conditions. The German philosopher Hegel, in his account of the history of consciousness, described Stoic freedom as a kind of retreat, a freedom that exists only in thought and leaves the real chains in place. A slave who feels free inside is still a slave. Later readers have worried that such teaching can be used to make people accept oppression. Epictetus, to be fair, was no apologist for tyranny, and his examples show men resisting emperors. But the emphasis falls on how to endure, not how to reform.

Nietzsche attacked the Stoics from another side. He thought that telling people to live according to nature was a self-deception, since the Stoics in fact wanted to impose their own values on nature. He also disliked what he saw as an ethic of self-protection rather than greatness.

There are also more ordinary objections. Many things are partly in our power, such as our health and our work, so the line looks sharper than life allows. And the theology, Zeus speaking to his student, will not persuade anyone who does not share the assumption of a caring divine order.

Finally, there is the question of tone. Epictetus is sometimes harsh, and his willingness to call his listeners fools or wretches can feel more like scolding than help. Modern self-help versions of Stoicism, in turn, have been criticized for turning a demanding ancient discipline into a productivity trick, a way to keep calm at work and endure an unfair system. That criticism is aimed at today's popularizers more than at Epictetus, but it has become part of the conversation around him.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter belongs to the school of philosophy called Stoicism, founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium and named after the painted porch, the stoa, where he taught. The early Stoics, above all Chrysippus, built a large system of logic, physics and ethics. By the time of the Roman Empire, the emphasis had narrowed to ethics, and Stoicism had become a practical discipline for living under uncertain and often dangerous rulers. Seneca, a wealthy statesman and Nero's tutor, Musonius Rufus, Epictetus's own teacher, Epictetus himself and the emperor Marcus Aurelius are the main voices of this late phase. Of the four, Epictetus is the most plainspoken and the most severe.

He also descends from Socrates, whom he treats as the model of the good man. Socrates accepted his execution calmly and said, as Plato reports, that his accusers could harm him in body but not in the things that count. Epictetus returns to that idea again and again. The Stoics took it and built a whole system around it, and the cheerful way Epictetus talks about death and exile is a direct echo.

Compared with its neighbors, this chapter has a different flavor than Marcus Aurelius, who writes privately and wrestles with doubt. Epictetus is a teacher in a room, speaking to students with a challenge in his voice. Compared with Seneca, he is less polished and less literary, and he lacks the rich man's perspective. His sentences are the sentences of someone who has had very little and does not envy anyone.

Outside the Stoic tradition, similar ideas appear in other places. The Serenity Prayer, with its request for the wisdom to tell the changeable from the unchangeable, is a close relative of this chapter, though it has a different origin. The Bhagavad Gita counsels acting without clinging to the fruits of action, which sounds very much like Epictetus's advice to do your part and leave the outcome alone.

Among descendants, cognitive behavioral therapy is the clearest, and the modern Stoic revival, with its books, podcasts and daily practices, is the most visible. Philosophers such as Pierre Hadot, who wrote about ancient philosophy as a way of life and not only a set of arguments, have used Epictetus as a central example. Hadot's point was that these lectures were spiritual exercises, to be repeated, memorized and lived.

Why does it still matter to read? Partly because it is short and clear and forceful. Partly because we live in a time of constant alarm, when the news makes us feel responsible for things we cannot touch. And partly because it asks a question that never stops being useful: what, right now, is actually up to me? Even if you decide, with Pascal or Nussbaum, that Epictetus asks too much, you will have to think hard to find out exactly where you disagree. A text that makes you do that has earned its place in the great conversation.
