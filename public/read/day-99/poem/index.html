The Land of Counterpane

Robert Louis Stevenson (1885) • 3 min read

Confined to his sickbed with two pillows at his head, a child transforms the wrinkles of his bedsheets into rolling hills, oceans, and forests for his lead soldiers and ships.

Key Context & Why It Matters

"The Land of Counterpane" comes from A Child's Garden of Verses, which Robert Louis Stevenson published in 1885, and which became one of the most successful books of poetry for children ever written. Stevenson, the Scottish author of Treasure Island, was himself a sickly boy who spent long nights in bed in Edinburgh, nursed by a devoted nurse named Alison Cunningham, whom he called Cummy and to whom he dedicated the book. Most Victorian verse for children was designed to teach manners and morals. Stevenson did something new. He wrote from inside a child's mind, remembering how a small person actually plays and imagines. In this poem, a sick boy turns his bed into a whole world. "Counterpane" simply means bedspread. In a sentence, the poem is about how imagination can transform confinement into kingdom, and make a sick room a place of power.

When I was sick and lay a-bed,

I had two pillows at my head,

And all my toys beside me lay

To keep me happy all the day.

And sometimes for an hour or so

I watched my leaden soldiers go,

With different uniforms and drills,

Among the bed-clothes, through the hills;

And sometimes sent my ships in fleets

All up and down among the sheets;

Or brought my trees and houses out,

And planted cities all about.

I was the giant great and still

That sits upon the pillow-hill,

And sees before him, dale and plain,

The pleasant land of counterpane.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, this is the simplest poem imaginable. A child is sick in bed, and he plays. But it is worth walking through it slowly, because it is arranged with real care, and because the simplicity is the result of skill.

The first stanza sets the scene. "When I was sick and lay a-bed, I had two pillows at my head, and all my toys beside me lay to keep me happy all the day." Notice the tense. It is "when I was", so the speaker is looking back. This is an adult, or at least an older person, remembering. And notice the tone of the second line, the gentle comfort of "two pillows". To a child, an extra pillow is a luxury, a sign that you are being looked after. The toys are there "to keep me happy", and the phrase tells us that other people, probably a nurse or a parent, arranged things so that a sick boy would not be miserable.

Then the play begins, and it escalates. In the second stanza he watches his "leaden soldiers", little toy soldiers made of lead, march through the bedclothes, "with different uniforms and drills, among the bed-clothes, through the hills". The humps and folds of the blanket become the hills. In the third stanza, ships sail "up and down among the sheets", and he plants "trees and houses" and "cities all about". A rumpled bed is now a landscape with armies, seas and towns.

The final stanza is the real point of the poem. "I was the giant great and still, that sits upon the pillow-hill, and sees before him, dale and plain, the pleasant land of counterpane." The boy has changed scale. He is no longer a small, sick child under a blanket. He is a giant, looking down on a country he rules. The word "still" is the key. He is "great and still", and a sick child is of course still, lying in bed with nothing to do but stay put. But Stevenson turns the stillness into majesty. The thing that is a weakness in the real world, being unable to move, becomes in the imagined one the calm of a giant surveying his realm.

A first-time reader might also miss a quiet layer of sadness. The poem is cheerful, but it is a cheerful poem about being ill, and the soldiers and ships are both signs of a child who cannot go outdoors. The adventure is real but also a consolation. It shows how children, and indeed adults, make worlds out of whatever they have. The poem is never self-pitying, and it never mentions pain or fear. That omission is itself a kind of meaning. It shows a mind that, for an hour or so, has found a way to be happy.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The most obvious thing to say is that the poem is beautifully easy to say aloud. It is written in rhyming couplets, pairs of lines that rhyme one after another, in a short, bouncy line of eight syllables. The rhymes are a-bed and head, lay and day, so and go, drills and hills, fleets and sheets, out and about, still and pillow-hill, plain and counterpane. They fall neatly, and yet none of them feels forced. That is craftsmanship. Children's verse can easily sound sing-song and clumsy, and Stevenson's never does.

The structure is the next strength. Each stanza has its own job. The first sets the scene, the second brings in the soldiers, the third widens the play to ships and cities, and the fourth gives the whole thing a new angle by making the boy a giant. The progression is neat and satisfying. It moves from small to large, from objects to world, and finally from the child as a player to the child as a ruler. Because each stanza begins with "And sometimes" or a similar phrase, the play seems to unroll over an afternoon, with the dreamy looseness of real childhood games.

Particular words are chosen well. "Counterpane" is a lovely old word, and it makes the bedspread sound like a country. "Pillow-hill" is a small compound that does a lot of work, turning a soft pillow into a lofty mountain. "Dale and plain" gives the landscape the ring of a real map. Even "leaden", with its metal heaviness, makes the soldiers feel real.

Now the weaknesses. The poem is, frankly, a bit slight. It makes a single point, and once you have seen the giant on the pillow, there is nothing left to discover. Compared with the great poems of childhood, such as Blake's Songs of Innocence, it has less depth and no real shadow. Some readers find the vocabulary a little twee for a modern ear, with "a-bed" as an obvious example. And the voice is slightly less honest than it seems. Though it claims to speak for a child, it is clearly an adult's memory, tidied and softened. Real sick children are often bored, scared and cross. This one is serene.

There is also a technical quibble about the last stanza. "Great and still" is a lovely phrase, but the giant "sits" on the pillow-hill while the boy is lying in bed, so the picture is slightly mixed. A strict reader might say the metaphor wobbles. But most readers happily accept the inconsistency, because the logic of play is not the logic of geometry. The poem's small flaws are those of any charming piece, and its virtues are rarer than they look: clarity, warmth and an uncommon respect for the seriousness of a child's game.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's impact is inseparable from the impact of the book it belongs to. A Child's Garden of Verses, published in 1885, was a quiet hit and then a lasting classic. It has hardly ever been out of print, and it remains one of the best-known collections of poems for children in the English language. "The Land of Counterpane" is among its most reprinted pieces, along with "My Shadow", "The Swing" and "Bed in Summer".

The real change the book made was in point of view. Before Stevenson, much English verse for children was designed to improve them. The famous hymns and moral rhymes of earlier generations, including those of Isaac Watts and Jane and Ann Taylor, warned children against laziness, quarrelling and untruth. Stevenson set out to describe the world as a child sees it. His poems do not scold. They share. That change of angle opened the door for later writers who treated children's imaginative life as worth recording for its own sake.

You can trace the influence forward. A. A. Milne's When We Were Very Young, in 1924, with its small, sharply observed scenes of nursery life, owes a clear debt to Stevenson. So do many later poets for children, who adopted his plain language, his light rhymes and his respect for play. The idea that a children's poem can be both simple and truthful became standard because of books like this one.

The poem has also had a long life through illustration. Successive artists have drawn the boy among his soldiers and ships. Charles Robinson's illustrated edition appeared in the 1890s, and the American artist Jessie Willcox Smith produced a famous edition in 1905 whose pictures became part of how generations imagined the poems. Composers have set the verses to music, and the poem is often recited in classrooms. Some adults can still say it by heart.

It has an important place in the biography of its author too. The poem is rooted in Stevenson's real childhood in Edinburgh, where he suffered from poor health and was looked after through sleepless nights by Cummy, who would tell him stories and read to him. The dedication to her is one of the most moving parts of the book. This link between the poem and Stevenson's life made it a favourite subject in studies of his work, and it helps explain why his adventure stories, with their sense of play and risk, have the feel of games enacted by a boy who spent his youth in bed. In that sense, the poem is a small key to the larger writer.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing readers love about "The Land of Counterpane" is recognition. Almost everyone has made a world out of a bed, a floor or a pile of blankets. The poem puts into words something that most people did as a child and never described. Readers who come to it as adults often feel a small shock of memory, as if they could see the folds of a quilt and the little figures marching across them.

They also love its gentleness. The poem is about illness, but it contains no suffering, only comfort and play. Parents and nurses have valued it for that reason, since it shows a sick child being cared for and finding his own happiness. Some readers say it taught them that a bad day could be turned into a good one by imagination alone. That is a lesson of great practical worth, and the poem delivers it without preaching.

The language, too, has won affection. The poem is easy to memorize. Its rhymes click into place, and the images are so clear that they stay in the mind. Children enjoy the soldiers and ships, and adults enjoy the neat joke of the "pillow-hill". It is one of those poems that serve two audiences at once, the child who takes it literally and the adult who sees what lies behind it.

The final stanza is where many readers feel the poem's heart. The image of the boy as a giant surveying a country is strong enough to survive repetition. People often describe it as a picture of power gained through imagination, a way of being large when you are small and weak. Those who have been ill themselves, or who have cared for someone who was, feel the relief in it. The stillness of the bedridden becomes the stillness of a giant. It is a gentle triumph.

There is also the pleasure of the book's whole spirit. A Child's Garden of Verses has been loved by people as different as writers, teachers and grandparents, and many have testified to having been read these poems at bedtime. The poem has therefore taken on a second meaning for them, as part of a shared memory of being tucked up and read to. That personal association is one of the strongest forms of literary affection. It means that readers do not simply admire the poem. They remember being given it, and that is a form of love that no criticism can easily challenge.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The criticisms of "The Land of Counterpane" are mostly gentle, as befits the poem, but they are real. The most frequent is that the poem, and the whole Garden of Verses, is sentimental. It presents childhood as a sunny, imaginative paradise and leaves out the fear, boredom and cruelty that real children know. Some critics have said that Stevenson's children are a little too good, too content and too easily consoled. A sick boy who is quietly happy for hours on end might strike a modern parent as improbable.

A related complaint is that the voice is not truly a child's. The poem is written by an adult looking back, and it has the polish and neatness of adult memory. Critics have pointed out that the speaker uses ordered, literary language, like "dale and plain" and "pillow-hill", that no real small boy would use. In that sense the book is a nostalgic performance rather than a transcription of childhood. This is a fair point, though it is also true of almost every poem about childhood ever written, including those of Wordsworth and Blake.

Another objection is social. The world of the poem is distinctly middle class. The boy has toys, a nurse, pillows and a comfortable bed. The sick children of Victorian slums, who might have had none of those things, are absent. Some historians of children's literature have observed that Stevenson's gentle nursery is a privileged place, and that it hides the harsher realities of the period. The poem is not meant to be a social document, but it does reflect the narrow slice of life its author knew.

Some critics have also argued that Stevenson's verse is simply minor next to his prose. In his lifetime and afterward, readers were more drawn to the stories than to the poems, and some thought the poems sentimental trifles. The poet's own hesitations did not help. He described the poems with modesty, and he did not claim for them the standing of his fiction. In the twentieth century, as poetry became more difficult, the simple verse of the Garden seemed old-fashioned.

Finally, there is the matter of time. A few words, like "a-bed" and "counterpane", have slipped out of everyday speech, and some modern children need the poem explained. That is a minor drawback, but it explains why the poem is now often met in an anthology of classics rather than discovered on its own. The objections do not, however, outweigh its strengths, and most readers consider them a small price for such a lasting and affectionate piece of work.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To see where this poem belongs, imagine a long conversation among writers about what childhood is. In the eighteenth century, William Blake wrote Songs of Innocence and of Experience, in which children are both holy and vulnerable. A little later, William Wordsworth declared that "the child is father of the man" and made childhood the source of imaginative power. The Romantics, in short, taught the Victorians to think of childhood as precious. Stevenson belongs to this tradition, but he gave it a lighter, more playful form.

Within children's literature, he sits among a golden generation. Lewis Carroll's Alice books appeared in the 1860s, Christina Rossetti's Sing-Song in 1872, and Edward Lear's nonsense verses earlier still. Kate Greenaway's illustrated children were fashionable in the 1880s. Compared with Carroll's wit and nonsense, Stevenson's verse is more gentle and more truthful to everyday experience. It does not invent a fantasy world. It shows the real world transformed.

The poem also reflects something particular to Stevenson. The role of play and make-believe is central to his fiction as well. Treasure Island, which he began as a game of drawing a map with his stepson, grew from the same instinct as the boy on the pillow-hill. The leaden soldiers and ships are ancestors of pirates and hidden treasure. In his essay "Child's Play", he wrote with great insight about how children use their imaginations, and the poem is a small, perfect case of that insight.

Looking forward, the line from Stevenson runs to A. A. Milne, to Walter de la Mare, and to the many modern children's poets who write in plain, musical language. It also touches the picture-book tradition, in which a child's room is turned into a wild world, as in Maurice Sendak's Where the Wild Things Are, where a bedroom becomes a forest. The idea of a child ruling a private kingdom is a long-lived one in literature, and "Counterpane" is one of its earliest and neatest statements.

Why does it still matter? For one thing, it reminds us what imagination is for. It is not a luxury for easy times but a tool for surviving hard ones. A child in bed, with nothing but blankets and a handful of toys, can make an empire. That is a fact about human beings, and it is worth remembering at any age. For another, it is a gentle introduction to poetry. A reader who is nervous of verse can read these sixteen lines and discover that poems can be clear, funny and moving at once. It is a good place to start, and a good place to return to.
