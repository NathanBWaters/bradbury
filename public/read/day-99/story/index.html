The Cop and the Anthem

O. Henry (1904) • 11 min read

“On his bench in Madison Square Soapy moved uneasily.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Cop and the Anthem is a short story by O. Henry, the pen name of William Sydney Porter, first published in 1904 and later collected in The Four Million. That title was a playful answer to a snobbish phrase of the day that said only four hundred people in New York really mattered. Henry insisted the other millions mattered too. Porter had known hard times himself. He had worked in Texas, served a prison sentence for embezzlement, and then become the most popular storyteller in New York, writing about the poor, the odd, and the overlooked. This story follows a homeless man named Soapy as winter approaches. Oddly, all he wants is to get arrested, so he can spend the cold months in jail. It is funny and sharp, with an ending that is both ironic and surprisingly sad. Underneath the jokes is a question about how a society treats those at the bottom.

On his bench in Madison Square Soapy moved uneasily. When wild geese honk high of nights, and when women without sealskin coats grow kind to their husbands, and when Soapy moves uneasily on his bench in the park, you may know that winter is near at hand.

A dead leaf fell in Soapy’s lap. That was Jack Frost’s card. Jack is kind to the regular denizens of Madison Square, and gives fair warning of his annual call. At the corners of four streets he hands his pasteboard to the North Wind, footman of the mansion of All Outdoors, so that the inhabitants thereof may make ready.

Soapy’s mind became cognisant of the fact that the time had come for him to resolve himself into a singular Committee of Ways and Means to provide against the coming rigour. And therefore he moved uneasily on his bench.

The hibernatorial ambitions of Soapy were not of the highest. In them there were no considerations of Mediterranean cruises, of soporific Southern skies drifting in the Vesuvian Bay. Three months on the Island was what his soul craved. Three months of assured board and bed and congenial company, safe from Boreas and bluecoats, seemed to Soapy the essence of things desirable.

For years the hospitable Blackwell’s had been his winter quarters. Just as his more fortunate fellow New Yorkers had bought their tickets to Palm Beach and the Riviera each winter, so Soapy had made his humble arrangements for his annual hegira to the Island. And now the time was come. On the previous night three Sabbath newspapers, distributed beneath his coat, about his ankles and over his lap, had failed to repulse the cold as he slept on his bench near the spurting fountain in the ancient square. So the Island loomed big and timely in Soapy’s mind. He scorned the provisions made in the name of charity for the city’s dependents. In Soapy’s opinion the Law was more benign than Philanthropy. There was an endless round of institutions, municipal and eleemosynary, on which he might set out and receive lodging and food accordant with the simple life. But to one of Soapy’s proud spirit the gifts of charity are encumbered. If not in coin you must pay in humiliation of spirit for every benefit received at the hands of philanthropy. As Caesar had his Brutus, every bed of charity must have its toll of a bath, every loaf of bread its compensation of a private and personal inquisition. Wherefore it is better to be a guest of the law, which though conducted by rules, does not meddle unduly with a gentleman’s private affairs.

Soapy, having decided to go to the Island, at once set about accomplishing his desire. There were many easy ways of doing this. The pleasantest was to dine luxuriously at some expensive restaurant; and then, after declaring insolvency, be handed over quietly and without uproar to a policeman. An accommodating magistrate would do the rest.

Soapy left his bench and strolled out of the square and across the level sea of asphalt, where Broadway and Fifth Avenue flow together. Up Broadway he turned, and halted at a glittering café, where are gathered together nightly the choicest products of the grape, the silkworm and the protoplasm.

Soapy had confidence in himself from the lowest button of his vest upward. He was shaven, and his coat was decent and his neat black, ready-tied four-in-hand had been presented to him by a lady missionary on Thanksgiving Day. If he could reach a table in the restaurant unsuspected success would be his. The portion of him that would show above the table would raise no doubt in the waiter’s mind. A roasted mallard duck, thought Soapy, would be about the thing⁠—with a bottle of Chablis, and then Camembert, a demitasse and a cigar. One dollar for the cigar would be enough. The total would not be so high as to call forth any supreme manifestation of revenge from the café management; and yet the meat would leave him filled and happy for the journey to his winter refuge.

But as Soapy set foot inside the restaurant door the head waiter’s eye fell upon his frayed trousers and decadent shoes. Strong and ready hands turned him about and conveyed him in silence and haste to the sidewalk and averted the ignoble fate of the menaced mallard.

Soapy turned off Broadway. It seemed that his route to the coveted island was not to be an epicurean one. Some other way of entering limbo must be thought of.

At a corner of Sixth Avenue electric lights and cunningly displayed wares behind plate-glass made a shop window conspicuous. Soapy took a cobblestone and dashed it through the glass. People came running around the corner, a policeman in the lead. Soapy stood still, with his hands in his pockets, and smiled at the sight of brass buttons.

“Where’s the man that done that?” inquired the officer excitedly.

“Don’t you figure out that I might have had something to do with it?” said Soapy, not without sarcasm, but friendly, as one greets good fortune.

The policeman’s mind refused to accept Soapy even as a clue. Men who smash windows do not remain to parley with the law’s minions. They take to their heels. The policeman saw a man halfway down the block running to catch a car. With drawn club he joined in the pursuit. Soapy, with disgust in his heart, loafed along, twice unsuccessful.

On the opposite side of the street was a restaurant of no great pretensions. It catered to large appetites and modest purses. Its crockery and atmosphere were thick; its soup and napery thin. Into this place Soapy took his accusive shoes and telltale trousers without challenge. At a table he sat and consumed beefsteak, flapjacks, doughnuts and pie. And then to the waiter be betrayed the fact that the minutest coin and himself were strangers.

“Now, get busy and call a cop,” said Soapy. “And don’t keep a gentleman waiting.”

“No cop for youse,” said the waiter, with a voice like butter cakes and an eye like the cherry in a Manhattan cocktail. “Hey, Con!”

Neatly upon his left ear on the callous pavement two waiters pitched Soapy. He arose, joint by joint, as a carpenter’s rule opens, and beat the dust from his clothes. Arrest seemed but a rosy dream. The Island seemed very far away. A policeman who stood before a drug store two doors away laughed and walked down the street.

Five blocks Soapy travelled before his courage permitted him to woo capture again. This time the opportunity presented what he fatuously termed to himself a “cinch.” A young woman of a modest and pleasing guise was standing before a show window gazing with sprightly interest at its display of shaving mugs and inkstands, and two yards from the window a large policeman of severe demeanour leaned against a water plug.

It was Soapy’s design to assume the role of the despicable and execrated “masher.” The refined and elegant appearance of his victim and the contiguity of the conscientious cop encouraged him to believe that he would soon feel the pleasant official clutch upon his arm that would insure his winter quarters on the right little, tight little isle.

Soapy straightened the lady missionary’s ready-made tie, dragged his shrinking cuffs into the open, set his hat at a killing cant and sidled toward the young woman. He made eyes at her, was taken with sudden coughs and “hems,” smiled, smirked and went brazenly through the impudent and contemptible litany of the “masher.” With half an eye Soapy saw that the policeman was watching him fixedly. The young woman moved away a few steps, and again bestowed her absorbed attention upon the shaving mugs. Soapy followed, boldly stepping to her side, raised his hat and said:

“Ah there, Bedelia! Don’t you want to come and play in my yard?”

The policeman was still looking. The persecuted young woman had but to beckon a finger and Soapy would be practically en route for his insular haven. Already he imagined he could feel the cozy warmth of the station-house. The young woman faced him and, stretching out a hand, caught Soapy’s coat sleeve.

“Sure, Mike,” she said joyfully, “if you’ll blow me to a pail of suds. I’d have spoke to you sooner, but the cop was watching.”

With the young woman playing the clinging ivy to his oak Soapy walked past the policeman overcome with gloom. He seemed doomed to liberty.

At the next corner he shook off his companion and ran. He halted in the district where by night are found the lightest streets, hearts, vows and librettos. Women in furs and men in greatcoats moved gaily in the wintry air. A sudden fear seized Soapy that some dreadful enchantment had rendered him immune to arrest. The thought brought a little of panic upon it, and when he came upon another policeman lounging grandly in front of a transplendent theatre he caught at the immediate straw of “disorderly conduct.”

On the sidewalk Soapy began to yell drunken gibberish at the top of his harsh voice. He danced, howled, raved and otherwise disturbed the welkin.

The policeman twirled his club, turned his back to Soapy and remarked to a citizen.

“ ’Tis one of them Yale lads celebratin’ the goose egg they give to the Hartford College. Noisy; but no harm. We’ve instructions to lave them be.”

Disconsolate, Soapy ceased his unavailing racket. Would never a policeman lay hands on him? In his fancy the Island seemed an unattainable Arcadia. He buttoned his thin coat against the chilling wind.

In a cigar store he saw a well-dressed man lighting a cigar at a swinging light. His silk umbrella he had set by the door on entering. Soapy stepped inside, secured the umbrella and sauntered off with it slowly. The man at the cigar light followed hastily.

“My umbrella,” he said, sternly.

“Oh, is it?” sneered Soapy, adding insult to petit larceny. “Well, why don’t you call a policeman? I took it. Your umbrella! Why don’t you call a cop? There stands one on the corner.”

The umbrella owner slowed his steps. Soapy did likewise, with a presentiment that luck would again run against him. The policeman looked at the two curiously.

“Of course,” said the umbrella man⁠—“that is⁠—well, you know how these mistakes occur⁠—I⁠—if it’s your umbrella I hope you’ll excuse me⁠—I picked it up this morning in a restaurant⁠—If you recognise it as yours, why⁠—I hope you’ll⁠—”

“Of course it’s mine,” said Soapy, viciously.

The ex-umbrella man retreated. The policeman hurried to assist a tall blonde in an opera cloak across the street in front of a street car that was approaching two blocks away.

Soapy walked eastward through a street damaged by improvements. He hurled the umbrella wrathfully into an excavation. He muttered against the men who wear helmets and carry clubs. Because he wanted to fall into their clutches, they seemed to regard him as a king who could do no wrong.

At length Soapy reached one of the avenues to the east where the glitter and turmoil was but faint. He set his face down this toward Madison Square, for the homing instinct survives even when the home is a park bench.

But on an unusually quiet corner Soapy came to a standstill. Here was an old church, quaint and rambling and gabled. Through one violet-stained window a soft light glowed, where, no doubt, the organist loitered over the keys, making sure of his mastery of the coming Sabbath anthem. For there drifted out to Soapy’s ears sweet music that caught and held him transfixed against the convolutions of the iron fence.

The moon was above, lustrous and serene; vehicles and pedestrians were few; sparrows twittered sleepily in the eaves⁠—for a little while the scene might have been a country churchyard. And the anthem that the organist played cemented Soapy to the iron fence, for he had known it well in the days when his life contained such things as mothers and roses and ambitions and friends and immaculate thoughts and collars.

The conjunction of Soapy’s receptive state of mind and the influences about the old church wrought a sudden and wonderful change in his soul. He viewed with swift horror the pit into which he had tumbled, the degraded days, unworthy desires, dead hopes, wrecked faculties and base motives that made up his existence.

And also in a moment his heart responded thrillingly to this novel mood. An instantaneous and strong impulse moved him to battle with his desperate fate. He would pull himself out of the mire; he would make a man of himself again; he would conquer the evil that had taken possession of him. There was time; he was comparatively young yet; he would resurrect his old eager ambitions and pursue them without faltering. Those solemn but sweet organ notes had set up a revolution in him. Tomorrow he would go into the roaring downtown district and find work. A fur importer had once offered him a place as driver. He would find him tomorrow and ask for the position. He would be somebody in the world. He would⁠—

Soapy felt a hand laid on his arm. He looked quickly around into the broad face of a policeman.

“What are you doin’ here?” asked the officer.

“Nothin’,” said Soapy.

“Then come along,” said the policeman.

“Three months on the Island,” said the Magistrate in the Police Court the next morning.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, The Cop and the Anthem is a comic story about a man who cannot get himself arrested. Underneath, it is a story about dignity, about how hard it is to climb out of poverty, and about a cruel irony in how the world works. Soapy is a homeless man who spends his days on a bench in Madison Square. When a dead leaf falls in his lap, which the narrator jokes is "Jack Frost's card," he knows winter is coming. Where wealthier New Yorkers buy tickets to Palm Beach or the Riviera, Soapy has a simpler plan. He wants "three months on the Island," meaning the jail on Blackwell's Island, with its guaranteed bed, meals, and company. The story's first joke is that jail is a kind of vacation, and the narrator treats it with a perfectly straight face.

Soapy refuses the charity that is on offer, and the reason is worth noticing. He says that "the gifts of charity are encumbered," and that if you do not pay in coin you must "pay in humiliation of spirit for every benefit received at the hands of philanthropy." The beds of charity come with a mandatory bath, and the loaf of bread with "a private and personal inquisition." He would rather be a guest of the law, which "does not meddle unduly with a gentleman's private affairs." This is both a joke and a real social observation. Help that comes with strings can feel worse than punishment, and a man with nothing may still cling to pride.

Then the comedy of failure begins. Soapy tries to be arrested over and over. He attempts to eat a fine meal he cannot pay for, but the head waiter throws him out before he sits down. He smashes a shop window, and the policeman simply assumes that no one who stayed behind could be the culprit. He eats a cheap dinner and cannot pay, and the waiters throw him onto the pavement. He pesters a young woman, and she cheerfully invites him to buy her a drink. He yells like a drunk, and the officer decides he is a harmless college boy. He steals an umbrella, and the owner apologizes. The world refuses to punish him exactly when he wants it.

Then comes the turn. Outside an old church, he hears an organ playing an anthem he once knew, "in the days when his life contained such things as mothers and roses and ambitions and friends and immaculate thoughts and collars." A sudden wave of shame and hope comes over him. He decides to find work the next day and make something of himself. And at that very moment a policeman puts a hand on his arm. "What are you doin' here?" "Nothin'." "Then come along." The next morning, the magistrate gives him three months on the Island. The meaning is the cruelty of timing. The law that ignored his crimes arrests him for doing nothing, at the instant he decides to be good.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, The Cop and the Anthem is among the most skilled of O. Henry's stories, though it is not without flaws. The best thing about it is the structure. The story is built on a pattern of repetition and variation, in which Soapy tries one method after another and fails in a different way each time. This is a classic comic form, and Henry handles it well. Each failure is shorter than the last, and each has its own small surprise, such as the young woman's "Sure, Mike, if you'll blow me to a pail of suds," or the policeman's remark that Soapy must be one of "them Yale lads celebratin'" after a game. The accumulation makes us laugh, and also makes us feel an odd pity, because we sense that Soapy is being refused not just jail but recognition. The world does not see him at all.

That is where the story gets its power. The final reversal works because everything before it has trained us to expect the opposite. We have watched a man try and fail to be arrested. When his heart changes and the policeman at last seizes him, the effect is of a joke that suddenly turns bitter. Henry gives us the hint with the single word "Tomorrow," and then the hand on the arm cuts it off in midsentence, with Soapy's thoughts left unfinished: "He would be somebody in the world. He would——" It is a beautiful touch, a sentence broken off, in the same way his hope is.

The voice is also a strength. Henry's narrator mixes high and low language in a way that mocks the pompous and cheers the humble. Describing a cheap restaurant whose "crockery and atmosphere were thick" while its "soup and napery thin," or calling the police "the law's minions," makes a little theater of every scene. The ornamental phrases keep a story about homelessness from becoming grim, yet the sadness is never completely forgotten.

There are weaknesses, however. The conversion in the church scene is a little too convenient. A man who has been joking and scheming for pages suddenly experiences a moral awakening because of a hymn, and the narrator describes it in a rush of conventional language, "the pit into which he had tumbled, the degraded days, unworthy desires, dead hopes." This is the most sentimental passage, and it can feel like a stage effect more than a real change of heart. Also, Henry's habit of coincidence is on full display. The timing of the arrest is so neat that it seems arranged by the author rather than by life. Still, the neatness is partly the point. The cruelty of the ending seems to be arranged by a cold universe, and the cold universe is the real target. The balance of jokes and sorrow is difficult, and Henry brings it off in a very short space.

III. The Impact It Had

The Cop and the Anthem first appeared in 1904, and was gathered in The Four Million in 1906, the book that made O. Henry a national figure. The book's title was a deliberate answer to a society phrase of the time, the "Four Hundred," which referred to the elite of New York. Henry argued that the real city was the millions of ordinary people, and this story is a clear example of what he meant. Its first impact was on Henry's own career. The story helped establish the type of tale for which he became known, the New York sketch that mixes comedy, social observation, and an ironic final twist. Its success contributed to his reputation as a chronicler of the city's poor. He went on to publish several books and write for newspapers until his death in 1910, and this story remained one of his most reprinted.

It has had a long afterlife in schools. Because it is short, funny, and rich in irony, it is a standard for teaching how a story can turn on a twist. Teachers also use it to discuss social class, poverty, and the justice system, since the story raises serious questions beneath its humor. Students often respond to Soapy's predicament with surprising feeling, and many remember the broken sentence at the end.

The story has also been adapted. The 1952 film O. Henry's Full House included a version, with the great actor Charles Laughton as Soapy and a young Marilyn Monroe in a small part, and it has been performed on radio and stage and in other languages. Laughton's performance, in particular, is often remembered by classic film fans. The story's plot has also been borrowed in sitcom episodes and cartoons, wherever a character tries desperately to be arrested or evicted and the world refuses to cooperate.

Beyond adaptations, the story has become part of the way people talk about irony. The notion of a man being punished at the moment he turns good is a recognizable image, and writers on law and social policy sometimes mention it when discussing how institutions treat the poor. Also, the question the story raises, about whether charity humiliates and whether jail is better, has not disappeared. In a world where homelessness remains a serious problem, a story that treats a homeless man as a person, not a statistic, has not lost its relevance. In short, the story's impact is partly literary and partly moral. It helped fix a style, and it kept alive a point of view, that of the ordinary person, that popular fiction sometimes forgets.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Cop and the Anthem for several connected reasons. The first is plain enjoyment. The story is funny, and it stays funny. The parade of failed attempts at arrest is a delight, and many readers recall particular moments, the waiter with "an eye like the cherry in a Manhattan cocktail," the policeman who waves off the shouting as college high spirits, the woman who answers Soapy's pickup line by cheerfully accepting. The jokes are gentle, and they never make fun of Soapy himself in a cruel way. We laugh with a kind of fondness, as we would at a friend.

Readers also love the humanity of the main character. Soapy is a bum, a petty thief, and a bit of a faker, and yet he has a certain pride and wit. His philosophy of charity, that it costs a man his self-respect, strikes many readers as quietly true. His gloomy comedy gives him a style of his own. When the hymn reaches him, readers feel the force of his shame and hope, because Henry has taken the trouble to make him real. Many say that they suddenly stop laughing at the church scene, which is a sign of how well the story shifts its mood.

Another thing people admire is the economy and surprise of the ending. Many readers say that the ending hit them like a small shock, and they remember it for years. The broken sentence, "He would——," seems to many readers like one of the best uses of an interrupted thought in short fiction. It leaves a feeling of loss, and also a sense of the world's indifference.

There is also admiration for how the story treats poverty. Henry was writing at a time when many popular stories treated the poor as comic types or moral warnings. Here the poor man is the center, with his own reasons and his own dreams, and the powerful are shown as blind, not wicked. Readers who care about social justice find this surprisingly modern. Finally, readers love the music. The anthem, never named, is a clever choice, since it lets each reader imagine the tune that would stop them in their tracks. The scene's images, the moon, the sleepy sparrows, the iron fence, create a short, quiet pause in a noisy story. It is a moment of grace, even though the story cannot give it a happy outcome.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a favorite story draws objections, and The Cop and the Anthem has attracted some. The most common is the charge of sentimentality, which is made against Henry in general. The church scene in particular has been criticized as too sudden and too conventional. Soapy's conversion, brought about by an organ hymn, relies on a Victorian belief that a good memory or a good melody can awaken the soul. Critics who prefer the realism that came after Henry's day find this old-fashioned. They say it is more a stage device than a believable change.

Another criticism is that the story is more about its twist than about its people. Like much of Henry's work, it is built to arrive at one ironic moment, and some readers feel the characters are made to fit it. Soapy is vivid, but we learn nothing about how he came to be homeless, what he did before, or why he cannot find work. The story is not curious about causes, and a more serious social writer might have been. It is a comedy of surfaces, and some readers say that its sympathy for the poor is real but shallow.

There is also a point about politics. The story's picture of the poor is warm, but it does not call for change. The law is presented as an indifferent machine, charity as an insult, and the only escape is private will. Critics on the left have sometimes argued that this is a comfortable kind of compassion, one that invites pity but not action. Readers who are sensitive about such matters may notice that the story's irony holds its audience at a safe distance from the problem. Other complaints are about coincidence. It is rather convenient that the policeman should appear at the exact moment of Soapy's conversion. A skeptical reader might say that Henry has arranged life so that his joke can land. Defenders reply that this is the nature of ironic fiction, and that the coincidence is the story's point.

Finally, a few readers have noticed unattractive details in the way minor figures are drawn, such as the broad ethnic accent in the policeman's voice, or the story's casual use of the idea of a "masher," a man who bothers women on the street. These reflect the manners of 1904, and they can jar. Some also find that Soapy's treatment of the young woman, whom he pesters in the hope of being arrested, is not much examined by the narrator. None of this has hurt the story's standing, but it shows where the story is open to challenge.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Cop and the Anthem belongs to the tradition of the urban sketch, a form that grew in the late nineteenth century alongside the newspapers and magazines of big American cities. Writers like Stephen Crane, in Maggie, and Jacob Riis, in How the Other Half Lives, had shown the world of the poor in New York, one with stark realism and the other with documentary photographs. Henry's approach was softer, a mixture of humor and sympathy. He did not argue, but invited the reader to like the people he described.

Its other ancestors are the picaresque tradition, which goes back centuries and follows a lovable rogue through a series of scrapes, and the comic literature of poverty, including Dickens, who often placed warmth and wit among the destitute. Soapy is a distant cousin of Dickens's Mr. Micawber, a man who gets by on language and hope. There is also a link to the French tradition of the short story with a final twist, perfected by Guy de Maupassant, though Henry's version is warmer.

Looking forward, the story is part of a long line of tales about the cruelty of timing and the irony of institutions. One can see its kinship with stories in which a man tries to do wrong and is thwarted, or does right and is punished, from Kafka's absurd trials to modern comedies of bureaucracy. In a gentler way, it anticipates the point of many later social comedies, that the system does not respond to people on the basis of their needs. It should also be read alongside Henry's own life. He knew prison from the inside, and he knew how a man's past could follow him. The idea of a man who wants to be in jail, then is jailed for the wrong reason, carries the shadow of a writer who had learned that the law is blind to a person's inner life. That personal weight, never stated, gives the story a depth that its jokes might suggest it lacks.

Among Henry's own work, it sits close to The Gift of the Magi and The Last Leaf, with the same tenderness for the poor, and close to The Ransom of Red Chief, with its comic exaggeration. It mixes both modes, and may be the best single example of his range. Why read it today? Because homelessness, charity, and the ways society handles its poorest members are still pressing questions. The story's central insight, that dignity matters as much as shelter, is one that policymakers and ordinary people still struggle to learn. And because, in a few pages, it makes the reader laugh, and then leaves a small silence behind, which is a rare and valuable thing.
