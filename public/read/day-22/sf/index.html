The Transamerica Pyramid's Seismic Truss and the Ghosts of the 1853 Montgomery Block

San Francisco — How Henry Halleck's 'Monkey Block' sheltered Mark Twain, Bret Harte, and Sun Yat-sen before William Pereira raised an 853-foot quartz-clad pyramid and a coastal redwood grove in its place (Gold Rush Bohemia (1853–1959) to Modernist Skyline (1969–1972)) • 8 min read

“The ideal skyscraper form is the pyramid: it lets the sunlight and fresh air down into the street where human beings walk.” — William L. Pereira (1969)

Key Context & Why It Matters

The city block at 600 Montgomery Street between Washington and Clay streets holds the richest double biography in downtown San Francisco. For 106 years (1853–1959), it was the site of Henry Wager Halleck's four-story brick Montgomery Block—the city's first fireproof building, floated atop a gigantic cross-timbered redwood raft in Yerba Buena mud—which survived the 1906 earthquake and served as the storied bohemian haunt ('The Monkey Block') of Mark Twain, Bret Harte, Ambrose Bierce, Frida Kahlo, and Dr. Sun Yat-sen. After its tragic 1959 demolition for a parking lot, architect William L. Pereira redeemed the corner in 1969–1972 by erecting the 853-foot white-quartz Transamerica Pyramid alongside a half-acre urban forest of Santa Cruz coast redwoods.

Before you crane your neck up at the 853-foot white quartz spire of the Transamerica Pyramid at 600 Montgomery Street, walk to the bronze historical plaque set into the base of the building and picture the remarkable four-story brick fortress that occupied this exact footprint from December 1853 to May 1959: the Montgomery Block. After six catastrophic fires incinerated Gold Rush San Francisco between 1849 and 1851, thirty-eight-year-old West Point military engineer and lawyer Henry Wager Halleck—who later served as Abraham Lincoln's General-in-Chief during the Civil War—resolved in 1853 to build a structure at Montgomery and Washington streets that neither earthquake nor conflagration could destroy.

Because the corner of Montgomery and Washington streets sat directly at the muddy water's edge of old Yerba Buena Cove, Halleck and architect Gordon P. Cummings excavated a vast trench and laid down a subterranean 'floating raft' composed of two tiers of giant twenty-four-inch-square coast redwood tree trunks bolted together in a cross-hatched grillage beneath two-foot-thick brick walls and iron window shutters. When the $500,000 Montgomery Block opened in December 1853 as the largest and safest office building in the American West, its ground floor housed the famous Bank Exchange Saloon—where bartender 'Pisco John' (Duncan Nicol) mixed the legendary Pisco Punch out of Peruvian pisco brandy, fresh pineapple, lime juice, and a secret distilled water recipe, limiting patrons to two glasses lest they try to climb the gasoliers.

On the afternoon of October 8, 1865, while twenty-nine-year-old newspaperman Samuel Clemens sat drinking in the Bank Exchange Saloon with his friend Bret Harte, a magnitude 6.5 earthquake rocked Montgomery Street; that experience, combined with the yarns he traded in the saloon and upstairs corridors, propelled Clemens toward the global fame of 'Mark Twain.' Over the next half-century, as downtown banks migrated south toward Market Street, room rents inside the four hundred offices of the Montgomery Block dropped to a few dollars a month, transforming the building—affectionately nicknamed 'The Monkey Block' by North Beach artists—into the undisputed headquarters of West Coast literary and political bohemia.

In 1906, when the April 18 earthquake and three-day firestorm leveled every surrounding block of downtown and Chinatown, Halleck's floating redwood-raft foundation and iron-shuttered brick walls held the Montgomery Block completely intact—allowing the U.S. Army to use it as relief headquarters while poet George Sterling, Jack London, Ambrose Bierce, and Maynard Dixon returned to its studios. Four years later, inside a second-floor office of the Montgomery Block in 1910–1911, exiled Chinese revolutionary physician Dr. Sun Yat-sen drafted the constitutional blueprints for the Republic of China and edited the Young China (Shaonian Zhongguo) newspaper that helped overthrow the Qing Dynasty in the 1911 Xinhai Revolution.

During the 1930s Great Depression, the Monkey Block sheltered a dazzling roster of painters and writers—including Coit Tower muralists Victor Arnautoff and Bernard Zakheim, sculptor Beniamino Bufano, Kenneth Rexroth, and visiting Mexican painters Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo—until developers tragically demolished the 106-year-old landmark in 1959 to build a surface parking lot. For a decade, the asphalt parking lot at 600 Montgomery Street stood as an open wound in the city's cultural memory until Transamerica Corporation CEO John R. Beckett acquired the site in 1968 and commissioned futuristic Los Angeles architect William L. Pereira to design a corporate headquarters that would NOT cast a dark rectangular shadow wall across North Beach and Chinatown.

Pereira returned with a bold geometric solution: whereas a conventional forty-eight-story rectangular box tower blocks sunlight and creates violent downdraft wind vortices at street level, a slender tapered four-sided pyramid reduces upper-floor bulk by seventy percent, allowing morning and afternoon sunlight to slant unimpeded into the surrounding narrow Gold Rush streets. When Pereira unveiled the 853-foot Pyramid design in January 1969, architectural critics and anti-highrise activists initially mocked it as 'Pereira's Dunce Cap' and marched outside City Hall wearing pointed cardboard hats; yet structural engineers at Chin & Hensolt and digital computer analysts quickly proved that the pyramid geometry was a masterpiece of earthquake engineering.

Between 1969 and 1972, workers excavated fifty-two feet below street level—removing the preserved 1853 redwood raft timbers of Halleck's Montgomery Block—and poured a continuous nine-foot-thick, 17,000-cubic-yard reinforced concrete mat foundation before erecting the exterior steel space-frame K-truss around the building's base. Look closely at the angled diagonal steel struts framing the ground-floor lobby of the Transamerica Pyramid: that perimeter K-truss transfers both vertical gravity loads and horizontal earthquake shear forces down into the fifty-two-foot deep concrete mat, allowing the upper building to flex safely while two external 'wings' rising from the 29th floor house elevator shafts and stairwells without wasting narrow upper floor plates.

Clad in 3,678 precast panels embedded with sparkling white crushed quartz from the Sierra Nevada and topped by a 212-foot translucent aluminum-and-glass spire illuminated from within on holidays, the forty-eight-story Transamerica Pyramid became upon its 1972 completion the beloved visual signature of the San Francisco skyline—and survived the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake without a single structural failure despite swaying over a foot at its peak. Most fittingly, on the half-acre lot immediately east of the tower along Washington and Sansome streets, landscape architect Anthony Guzzardo planted Transamerica Redwood Park in 1974 with eighty mature coast redwoods (Sequoia sempervirens) transplanted from the Santa Cruz Mountains, creating a cool, fog-dappled cathedral of living redwoods on the exact ground where Halleck's redwood foundation logs once held up the Monkey Block.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From 1853 Redwood Raft to 1972 Seismic K-Truss Space Frame

The site at 600 Montgomery Street showcases two revolutions in San Francisco seismic engineering separated by 119 years. In 1853, Henry Halleck floated the four-story brick Montgomery Block on bolted tiers of whole redwood logs buried in anaerobic bay mud, allowing the building to ride out the 1865, 1868, and 1906 earthquakes intact. In 1972, William Pereira and engineers Chin & Hensolt achieved the modern equivalent: a 52-foot-deep, nine-foot-thick concrete mat supporting an external triangulated steel K-truss that absorbs horizontal seismic shear.

2. Solar Access, Wind Downdrafts, and the Logic of the Tapered Pyramid

Why did William Pereira choose a pyramid over the boxy International Style glass slabs rising along Market and California streets in the late 1960s? A tapered tower has a low center of gravity, breaks up the high-altitude wind shear that otherwise plunges down flat glass facades onto pedestrians, and preserves sky exposure and solar corridors for the historic low-rise brick blocks of Jackson Square and Chinatown immediately to the north.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this corner with three essential details: first, the Montgomery Block ('The Monkey Block,' 1853–1959), where Mark Twain met Bret Harte, Duncan Nicol mixed Pisco Punch at the Bank Exchange, and Sun Yat-sen planned the 1911 Chinese Revolution; second, the 29th-floor East and West Elevator Wings on the Pyramid's facade, which keep elevator cores out of the narrowing upper floors; and third, the bronze frog fountain inside Redwood Park honoring Mark Twain's 'Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County.'

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Stand at the corner of Montgomery and Washington streets to read the historical bronze plaques commemorating the 1853 Montgomery Block and examine the massive white diagonal steel K-trusses supporting the Transamerica Pyramid. Walk east through the newly restored Transamerica Redwood Park beneath the towering coast redwoods, find the bronze jumping-frog fountain, and exit onto Sansome Street facing the brick warehouses of Jackson Square.
