The Transamerica Pyramid's Seismic Truss and the Ghosts of the 1853 Montgomery Block

San Francisco — How Henry Halleck's 'Monkey Block' sheltered Mark Twain, Bret Harte, and Sun Yat-sen before William Pereira raised an 853-foot quartz-clad pyramid and a coastal redwood grove in its place (Gold Rush Bohemia (1853–1959) to Modernist Skyline (1969–1972)) • 20 min read

“The ideal skyscraper form is the pyramid: it lets the sunlight and fresh air down into the street where human beings walk.” — William L. Pereira (1969)

Key Context & Why It Matters

The city block at 600 Montgomery Street between Washington and Clay streets (37.7952° N, 122.4028° W) holds the richest double biography in downtown San Francisco. For 106 years (1853–1959), it was the site of Henry Wager Halleck's four-story brick Montgomery Block—the city's first fireproof building, floated atop a gigantic cross-timbered redwood raft in Yerba Buena mud—which survived the 1906 earthquake and served as the storied bohemian haunt ('The Monkey Block') of Mark Twain, Bret Harte, Ambrose Bierce, Frida Kahlo, and Dr. Sun Yat-sen. After its tragic 1959 demolition for a parking lot, architect William L. Pereira redeemed the corner in 1969–1972 by erecting the 853-foot white-quartz Transamerica Pyramid alongside a half-acre urban forest of Santa Cruz coast redwoods.

Standing today at 600 Montgomery Street between Clay and Washington streets at the border of the Financial District and Jackson Square in the heart of the Northern Financial District / Jackson Square edge (37.7952° N, 122.4028° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Gold Rush Bohemia (1853–1959) to Modernist Skyline (1969–1972). Before you crane your neck up at the 853-foot white quartz spire of the Transamerica Pyramid at 600 Montgomery Street, walk to the bronze historical plaque set into the base of the building and picture the remarkable four-story brick fortress that occupied this exact footprint from December 1853 to May 1959: the Montgomery Block. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how General Henry Wager Halleck (builder of the 1853 Montgomery Block) and modernist architect William L. Pereira and a generation of San Franciscans confronted floating an indestructible brick literary citadel on Gold Rush bay mud in 1853 and later engineering an 853-foot seismic steel pyramid on the exact same footprint, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along 600 Montgomery Street between Clay and Washington streets at the border of the Financial District and Jackson Square.
