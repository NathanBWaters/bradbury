Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

To Waken an Old Lady

William Carlos Williams (1921) • 3 min read

Old age is a flight of small cheeping birds skimming over bare trees above a snow glaze—buffeted by dark wind, yet singing above a flock of shrill piping.

Key Context & Why It Matters

To Waken an Old Lady is an eighteen-line poem by William Carlos Williams, published in his 1921 collection Sour Grapes. Williams was a physician in Rutherford, New Jersey, who spent his days with the old and the sick and his evenings writing poems in a plain American voice. He was also part of the Imagist and modernist circle around Ezra Pound, which believed a poem should show a thing clearly rather than talk about it. The poem has often been linked to Williams's mother, who came from Puerto Rico and lived most of her life in the same New Jersey town, though the poem never says so. Instead of describing old age as a sunset or a dying fire, which is what poets usually did, Williams offers a picture of a flock of small winter birds fighting the wind. In a few lines it asks what it really feels like to be old, and answers with courage and a surprise.

Old age is

a flight of small

cheeping birds

skimming

bare trees

above a snow glaze.

Gaining and failing

they are buffetted

by a dark wind—

But what?

On harsh weedstalks

the flock has rested,

the snow

is covered with broken

seedhusks

and the wind tempered

by a shrill

piping of plenty.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem begins with a definition: "Old age is a flight of small cheeping birds skimming bare trees above a snow glaze." That is the whole claim, and everything else unfolds from it. Old age is not a person, a face or a feeling. It is a flock. The birds are small, noisy, thin and hungry. The trees are bare, the ground is crusted with a hard, shiny layer of snow, and the landscape offers them nothing.

Then comes the struggle. "Gaining and failing they are buffetted by a dark wind." This is the experience of aging in motion. The birds gain a little ground and lose it, push ahead and are blown back. Anyone who has watched an older person with a bad hip climb stairs, or seen a grandparent's strength come and go from week to week, will recognize the rhythm of gaining and failing. The dark wind is time, illness, loss, the cold forces that do not care how small and determined you are.

The pivot is the shortest line in the poem: "But what?" Two words, a question. The speaker stops the poem to ask what happens next. It is an interruption, almost a challenge to the reader who expects the usual gloomy ending. What follows is the poem's gift. "On harsh weedstalks the flock has rested, the snow is covered with broken seedhusks and the wind tempered by a shrill piping of plenty."

Here the picture shifts. The birds have landed on dried weeds, which look like nothing to a human eye, and have found food. The seed husks scattered across the snow are evidence of a feast. Notice that the weeds are called harsh. The comfort is not soft or pretty. It comes from rough, ordinary material. And the wind is "tempered," meaning softened, not stopped. It is still there. The poem does not pretend old age ends the hardship. It says there is a kind of plenty even in a hard season, and that the flock can fill the air with sound about it.

The final phrase, "piping of plenty," is a small bit of music. The birds are no longer just cheeping, a weak sound. They are piping, loudly and happily. The reader is meant to hear that sound, and to understand that the old lady is being woken, not to sadness but to a sense of abundance. The title makes this gentler: the poem is a kind of morning greeting to someone, saying that her age has its own songs.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged as craft, To Waken an Old Lady is one of Williams's most accomplished short poems, and the main reason is control. In eighteen lines of two to five words each, he builds a single moving picture and a small narrative arc: the flight, the battering, the pause, the rest, the music. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is explained. The comparison, old age as a flock of birds, does not announce itself as a metaphor with any fuss. It is simply stated, and trusted.

The line breaks do real work. Words like "skimming" and "bare trees" sit on lines of their own, so the eye drops down the page the way birds drop over a field. "Gaining and failing" has a rocking motion in its sound that copies the thing it describes. The question "But what?" is isolated, so you feel the hush before the answer. Williams's sense of timing, of when to stop and let silence do the work, is excellent here.

The sound is also well managed. The poem is full of short, sharp, hard consonants, cheeping, skimming, snow glaze, weedstalks, seedhusks, and these give way in the last lines to the long, open "shrill piping of plenty," where the s and p sounds trill. The change in texture mirrors the emotional change, from hardness to release.

There are weaknesses, or at least limits. The first is that the poem is quite compressed, and a reader who wants emotional depth may feel it is more picture than feeling. It tells us old age is brave and resilient, but it does not show a particular old person, a particular loss or a particular joy. The old lady of the title is almost absent. Some readers feel that the poem sidesteps the harder truths of aging, memory loss, loneliness, pain, and ends with a slightly tidy consolation. The word "plenty" is a little too reassuring for those who see old age as a decline without a happy ending.

There is also a question of address. The title says the poem is meant to waken an old lady, but the poem never speaks to her directly, and it is unclear what waking means. Is she waking from sleep, from despair, from a long habit of seeing herself as finished? That ambiguity can be seen as richness, but it can also be seen as vagueness. And the spelling "buffetted," with its extra t, looks like a slip rather than a design, a small blemish in a poem that depends on precision.

Overall, though, the poem's strengths clearly outweigh its limits. It achieves a rare thing, a consoling poem about old age that never sounds sentimental, because it keeps its eyes on birds and snow.

III. The Impact It Had

To Waken an Old Lady was not a sensation when it appeared in Sour Grapes in 1921, a book that was received quietly. Williams was then known to a small circle of poets and editors rather than to a wide public. But the poem was one of the pieces that made later readers take Sour Grapes seriously, and over time it became one of his most reprinted short poems.

Its main impact was to show how much a modern poem could do with almost nothing. In 1921 many people still thought of poetry as rhyme, grand language and elevated subjects. Here was a poem with no rhyme, no big words, no capital letters at the start of the lines, and a subject, old age, that poets had always handled in a solemn tone. Williams treats it with a picture of sparrows, or something like sparrows, and the effect is just as powerful. Poets who read it learned that a single exact image can carry a whole idea.

For Williams's own career, this poem belongs to the stretch of work in which he developed his famous method, saying things through objects, not through commentary. Soon after came Spring and All in 1923, with The Red Wheelbarrow, and the whole line of his work that insisted on the local, the plain and the seen. To Waken an Old Lady is an early demonstration of that approach, with its snow, weeds and seeds.

The poem has also had a quiet life in teaching. It is a staple of introductory poetry classes because it is short, vivid and easy to discuss. Teachers use it to explain metaphor, line breaks and the idea of the image. Students can easily see what a poem looks like when it has nothing extra in it. It is also used in discussions about aging and in programs that bring poetry to older people, because its message is hopeful without being false.

Among later poets, its influence is hard to measure precisely, but the broader tradition it belongs to, short, image-driven American free verse written in plain speech, shaped writers from the Objectivists in the 1930s to the Black Mountain poets and the many poets of the later twentieth century who followed Williams's lead. The poem is one small brick in a very large wall.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this poem first for its image. It is easy to see the birds. Even people who have never read a line of modern poetry can picture a flock lifting over a snow-covered field, and the comparison to old age feels both surprising and right. It takes something that is usually described as slow, heavy and dim and turns it into something quick, light and alive.

They also love its honesty about difficulty combined with its refusal to give in. The dark wind is real. The birds are small. Nothing here denies that old age is hard. But the poem argues that hardship is not the whole story, and that the old can still find food, rest and song. For many readers who have watched parents or grandparents age, this is a deeply comforting thought, and it rings true because it does not pretend the wind is gone.

The turn at "But what?" is often singled out as the moment readers enjoy most. It is such a small thing, two words, and yet it changes the entire direction of the poem. It feels like a person catching their breath, deciding to look again, and finding something better than they had expected. Readers remember that moment long after they have forgotten the rest of the lines.

Many also admire the quiet affection in the poem. Because the title is addressed to an old lady, and because Williams's mother is often connected with it, readers sense that the poem is an act of love, a gift. It does not flatter. It does not tell her she is young. It tells her she is part of something bright and busy, and that her age is a season of its own with its own music.

Finally, other poets admire its craftsmanship. Writers learning how to make line breaks, how to use a single question as a hinge, and how to end on a sound rather than an idea often point to poems like this one. The last phrase, "piping of plenty," is cherished for the way it gives the ear a final treat. It is the kind of ending that people repeat to themselves.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

To Waken an Old Lady has been widely admired, so the pushback it has received is mild and mostly a matter of taste, though real points can be made. This specific poem drew no famous hostile reviews, so the criticisms below describe the kinds of objections readers have raised about it and about Williams's early style.

The most common concern is sentimentality, or at least a too-easy consolation. The poem ends with plenty, rest and song, and some critics and readers feel that this is a lovely but slightly comfortable answer to the hardest facts of aging. People who have cared for someone with dementia, or watched a long decline, may feel the poem moves too quickly from the dark wind to the happy piping. It answers a very big question, what is old age like, in eighteen short lines, and some find that too neat.

Another objection is about vagueness. The poem leaves open who the old lady is and what it means to waken her. If the reader does not know the biographical connection to Williams's mother, which is not stated in the poem, the dedication-like title can seem somewhat distant. A poem so dependent on a single image may also feel thin to readers who prefer more argument, more voice and more personality. Williams's critics have sometimes said that his minimalism could shade into mere picture-making, and that his short lyrics are snapshots rather than fully developed thought.

There is also an old debate about how successful the central metaphor is. Birds as old people is not a wildly original idea, and some readers feel the poem hovers between a metaphor and a nature description, never fully committing to being either. The poem says old age "is" a flight of birds, but it does not explain how, leaving the reader to do the work. Fans think this is a strength, and skeptics think it is a lack of rigor.

Finally, a more political reading has occasionally questioned the way Williams, a professional man, wrote about the elderly and about hardship with a certain distance, observing birds from a window rather than sharing in the cold. This is a stretch, perhaps, but it reflects a general criticism that Williams's gaze can be that of the observer rather than the participant. Again, such objections are mild, and the poem remains broadly loved.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To Waken an Old Lady belongs to American modernism and, more narrowly, to Imagism, the movement that Ezra Pound promoted in the early 1910s. Imagist poets wanted clear, concrete images, economical language and a free, speech-like rhythm. The most famous Imagist poem is Pound's In a Station of the Metro, which compares faces in a crowd to petals on a wet, black bough. Williams's poem works in a similar way, comparing something abstract, old age, to a precise natural picture. But Williams adds movement and a story, with a turning point, which makes the poem feel more alive than a single still image.

Its ancestors include the Romantics and their poems about birds, such as Shelley's skylark and Keats's nightingale, in which a bird stands for an emotion or an idea. Williams borrows that habit of using a bird as a symbol and strips away the lofty language, giving us tiny, cheeping, ordinary creatures. It also has something in common with Japanese haiku, which Imagist poets admired, in the way a season and a few images carry feeling without comment.

Its descendants are the many American poems that rely on a simple image and a quiet turn. Poets such as Robert Creeley, Denise Levertov and Gary Snyder worked in a lineage shaped by Williams's example. In contemporary poetry, short poems about aging and mortality that favor image over statement owe something to this kind of writing.

A useful comparison is with Yeats's Sailing to Byzantium, written a few years later, which also deals with old age but in a very different way. Yeats's old man is a scarecrow, a tattered coat on a stick, who longs to escape into a golden, eternal art. He wants to leave the body behind. Williams's old age stays entirely in the natural world and finds its answer there, in seeds and snow and the sound of living things. Yeats offers a grand escape, Williams a humble return. The two poems are like opposite answers to the same problem.

Why read it today? Because it is a rare thing: a poem about growing old that can comfort without lying. It takes about thirty seconds to read, it is easy to memorize, and it leaves you with an image of birds on a winter field that you may find coming back at unexpected moments. In a culture that often hides or fears aging, a poem that treats it as a flight with music at the end is worth having.
