Mount Davidson (928 Feet): George Davidson's Survey, Fog-Drip Forests, and the 103-Foot Cross

San Francisco — How San Francisco's highest natural summit evolved from Adolph Sutro's cloud forest and Madge Clairmont's conservation battle to the Armenian Genocide Memorial Cross (Sutro Forestry Era (1880s), Conservation Save (1926–1934) & 1997 Civic Resolution) • 8 min read

“Inside the eucalyptus and myrtle woods of Mount Davidson on a summer afternoon, the Pacific fog condenses on the high leaves and falls like steady rain while downtown lies in sunshine.” — Harold Gilliam, Weather of the San Francisco Bay Region (1962)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rising 928 feet above sea level in the geographic center of southwest San Francisco—twenty-four feet higher than Twin Peaks—stands Mount Davidson, the highest natural point in the City and County of San Francisco. Named in 1911 for pioneering U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey astronomer and geographer Professor George Davidson (1825–1911), wrapped in a lush, moss-hung cloud forest of Tasmanian blue-gum eucalyptus planted in the 1880s by Comstock Lode tunnel engineer and Mayor Adolph Sutro, and saved from real-estate bulldozers in the 1920s by women's club activist Madge Clairmont, the summit is crowned by a 103-foot poured-concrete cross lit via telegraph by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1934 and preserved in 1997 by San Francisco voters as the Armenian Genocide Memorial.

Ask ten visitors or even long-time residents to name the highest natural peak in San Francisco, and nine will guess Twin Peaks (922 feet) or Mount Sutro (908 feet)—yet thirty-six blocks southwest of Market Street rises Mount Davidson, crowning the peninsula's central Franciscan chert ridge at 928 feet above the tides of the Golden Gate. Known in the Mexican Rancho era as Cerro de las Cruces and in the late nineteenth century as Blue Mountain, the bare wind-scoured grassland peak was purchased in 1880 as part of the vast Rancho San Miguel (spanning one-tenth of San Francisco's total land area) by Prussian-born mining engineer, bibliophile, and future San Francisco Mayor Adolph Sutro (1830–1898).

Fresh from engineering the four-mile Sutro Tunnel that drained the boiling subterranean waters of Nevada's Comstock silver mines, Sutro looked at the treeless, windblown hills of western San Francisco and launched the most ambitious private urban afforestation campaign in nineteenth-century America: planting hundreds of thousands of fast-growing Tasmanian blue-gum eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus), Monterey cypress, and Monterey pine saplings across Mount Sutro, Twin Peaks, and Blue Mountain. Within thirty years, Sutro's saplings on Blue Mountain grew into a towering 120-foot canopy that created its own remarkable microclimate: coastal fog-drip (occult precipitation). During San Francisco's rainless summer months from June through September, when forty-knot banks of cold marine advection fog pour eastward from the Pacific Ocean over the 928-foot ridge, the feathery needles and long sickle-shaped leaves of the trees comb moisture directly out of the moving cloud.

Inside the Mount Davidson forest on a foggy August afternoon, up to ten to fifteen inches of конденsed cloud water drips steadily from the upper branches onto the forest floor every summer—sustaining a lush understory of native California blackberry, sword ferns, huckleberry, elderberry, and fragrant Toyon and Wax Myrtle while open streets three blocks away remain bone-dry. In 1911, following the death of eighty-six-year-old Professor George Davidson—the legendary head of the Pacific Coast U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey who had charted every headland from Mexico to Alaska, built the first astronomical observatory on the West Coast in Lafayette Park, and served as President of the California Academy of Sciences and the Sierra Club colleague of John Muir—explorers and the Sierra Club officially renamed Blue Mountain Mount Davidson in his honor.

After Adolph Sutro's estate was partitioned following prolonged probate battles in the early 1920s, speculative real-estate developers Baldwin & Howell began carving curving streetcar suburbs up the lower flanks of Mount Davidson and announced plans to bulldoze the forested summit itself into luxury house lots. To rescue the 928-foot summit and its wild rocky eastern cliffs for the public, civic activist Madge Clairmont and the City Federation of Women's Clubs launched a tireless six-year grassroots campaign—marching school children and neighbors up the mountain at dawn, lobbying the Board of Supervisors, and persuading the city between 1926 and 1929 to purchase forty acres of the peak as permanent Mount Davidson Park.

Meanwhile, beginning on Easter morning in 1923, San Francisco merchant and Boy Scout commissioner James Decatur had organized an annual Easter Sunrise Service on the summit of Mount Davidson around a series of temporary wooden crosses—four of which were successively built and burned down by vandals or accidental brushfires between 1923 and 1931. In 1933, at the depth of the Great Depression, San Francisco architectural firm George W. Kelham (designer of the Russ Building and the old Main Library) designed a permanent, fireproof 103-foot-tall steel-reinforced poured-concrete cross anchored thirty feet deep into the Franciscan chert bedrock of the summit.

On Saturday evening, March 24, 1934—the night before the twelfth annual Easter Sunrise Service, which drew 50,000 worshippers up the foggy trails and was broadcast nationwide over NBC and CBS radio—President Franklin Delano Roosevelt sat in the White House in Washington, D.C., and pressed a gold telegraph key that switched on two giant floodlights illuminating the new 103-foot Mount Davidson Cross against the San Francisco sky. Thirty-seven years later, in 1971, Hollywood director Don Siegel filmed one of cinema's iconic nocturnal suspense scenes at the base of the floodlit Mount Davidson Cross in Dirty Harry, where Inspector Harry Callahan confronts the 'Scorpio' killer on the foggy summit steps.

In 1990, however, a complex constitutional conflict arose: nine San Francisco residents of diverse religious backgrounds, represented by the ACLU and Americans United for Separation of Church and State, filed a federal lawsuit arguing that the City and County of San Francisco's ownership and maintenance of a 103-foot religious cross on public parkland violated the No Preference Clause of the California Constitution. After the U.S. Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that the city could not legally own a giant cross on municipal land—leaving the 1934 Kelham landmark facing court-ordered demolition—a creative civic solution emerged in 1997 that honored both constitutional law and deep historical memory.

The Council of Armenian American Organizations of Northern California offered to purchase the 0.38-acre summit parcel immediately beneath the cross at auction for $26,000—subject to a permanent public-access easement guaranteeing that the summit remain forever open to all hikers without gates or fences—and in November 1997, sixty-eight percent of San Francisco voters approved Proposition F ratifying the sale. Dedicated in April 1998 with a bronze plaque at the base of the 103-foot monument honoring the 1.5 million victims of the 1915 Armenian Genocide—many of whose orphaned survivors found refuge in California in the 1920s—Mount Davidson today welcomes quiet morning walkers through its dripping eucalyptus cloud forest up to a sweeping 360-degree panorama from the Farallones to Mount Diablo.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Microclimate Physics: How 'Occult Precipitation' Sustains a Summer Cloud Forest

Why is the soil inside Mount Davidson's forest damp and lush in mid-August when zero rain has fallen on San Francisco since May? Hydrologists call this phenomenon fog-drip or occult precipitation. Microscopic water droplets in summer marine advection fog are too light to fall as rain on bare grass, but when twenty-knot winds blow the fog bank through a 120-foot canopy of eucalyptus and cypress needles at 900 feet elevation, droplets coalesce on the leaf surfaces and patter to the ground—contributing up to ten inches of summer moisture that supports native ferns and coastal scrub.

2. Who Was Professor George Davidson (1825–1911)?

The man for whom San Francisco's highest peak was named in 1911 was the towering scientific figure of nineteenth-century California. Arriving in San Francisco in 1850 for the U.S. Coast Survey, George Davidson triangulated the exact latitude and longitude of every major promontory on the Pacific Coast, wrote the monumental Coast Pilot of California, Oregon, and Washington that guided thousands of ships safely through the fog, sited the Lick Observatory, and taught geography at UC Berkeley for decades.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three historical anchors on this 928-foot peak: first, Adolph Sutro's 1880s Rancho San Miguel afforestation, which turned bare 'Blue Mountain' into a cloud forest; second, Madge Clairmont and the City Federation of Women's Clubs, who saved the forty-acre park from subdivision in the 1920s; and third, George Kelham's 1934 103-foot concrete cross, preserved by voters in 1997 as the Armenian Genocide Memorial.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take the 36-Teresita Muni bus or walk up from West Portal to the trailhead at Dalewood Way and Myra Way. Follow the shaded dirt switchbacks through the dripping eucalyptus and huckleberry ravine to the 928-foot summit of Mount Davidson, inspect the bronze Armenian Genocide Memorial plaque at the base of the 103-foot cross, and walk a hundred yards east to the rocky clearing for an unobstructed panorama of downtown San Francisco and the bay.
