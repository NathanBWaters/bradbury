San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • Cybernetics, Counterculture & Personal Computing Revolution

Stewart Brand's 1968 Whole Earth Catalog, Engelbart's 'Mother of All Demos' at Civic Auditorium, and Community Memory

San Francisco — How San Francisco bridged sixties counterculture and personal computing—from 99 Grove Street's 1968 mouse-and-hypertext demo to 1380 Howard Street's public terminal and the Homebrew Computer Club (Counterculture-to-Cybernetics Bridge (1966–1975)) • 9 min read

“We are as gods and might as well get good at it. Access to tools.” — Stewart Brand, opening lines of the Whole Earth Catalog (Autumn 1968)

Key Context & Why It Matters

If you want to understand how the computer transformed between 1966 and 1975 from a room-sized Cold War corporate mainframe into a personal tool of individual liberation, walk between two blocks in San Francisco: Civic Auditorium at 99 Grove Street and the Project One warehouse at 1380 Howard Street (at 10th Street in SoMa). In autumn 1968, Stanford biologist and Merry Prankster Stewart Brand published the first Whole Earth Catalog ('Access to Tools'); weeks later, on December 9, 1968, inside Civic Auditorium at 99 Grove Street (with Brand running a camera pickup), Douglas Engelbart staged 'The Mother of All Demos,' unveiling the computer mouse, windows, hypertext, and live video collaboration; and in 1972–1973 inside 1380 Howard Street, counterculture engineers led by Lee Felsenstein and Efrem Lipkin built Resource One and Community Memory—the world's first public computerized bulletin board system—before Felsenstein moderated the Homebrew Computer Club.

In 1960, across the entire world, the word 'computer' meant one thing to the public: a giant, million-dollar IBM mainframe locked behind glass inside a Pentagon bunker, a bank basement, or a university punch-card bureau—so synonymous with conformist bureaucracy that UC Berkeley Free Speech Movement students in 1964 wore signs around their necks reading: 'I am a human being: Do not fold, spindle or mutilate.' How, within a single decade between 1966 and 1976 in the San Francisco Bay Area, did that hated symbol of technocratic control transform into a bicycle for the human mind—a personal tool built by long-haired hobbyists in jeans? Historian Fred Turner traced the bridge to a specific cultural collision in San Francisco between the 1960s counterculture and Norbert Wiener's cybernetics.

The catalyst of that collision was Stewart Brand: a Stanford-trained evolutionary biologist, former U.S. Army paratrooper and photographer, friend of Ken Kesey's Merry Pranksters, and co-organizer of the landmark January 1966 Trips Festival at Longshoremen's Hall near Fisherman's Wharf (where 10,000 people experienced the Grateful Dead and Bill Ham's liquid light shows in one multimedia feedback loop). In February 1966, sitting on the gravel roof of his North Beach apartment in San Francisco watching the tall downtown buildings tilt against the horizon, twenty-seven-year-old Stewart Brand printed hundreds of pinback buttons asking a simple question—'Why haven't we seen a photograph of the whole Earth yet?'—and sold them on college campuses and mailed them to NASA officials, convinced that seeing Earth from space as a single fragile blue island would awaken ecological consciousness.

Two years later, in the autumn of 1968, as thousands of young counterculturalists left the cities to build geodesic domes and back-to-the-land communes, Brand and his wife Lois Jennings used an IBM Selectric Composer typewriter and a Polaroid MP-3 camera to self-publish the first issue of the oversized, newsprint Whole Earth Catalog under the subtitle Access to Tools. Open the pages of the 1968 Whole Earth Catalog—which sold 2.5 million copies, won the 1972 National Book Award, and which Steve Jobs later described at Stanford in 2005 as 'Google in paperback form, thirty-five years before Google came along'—and you find Buckminster Fuller's geodesic geometry, organic gardening guides, and deer-skinning knives reviewed right alongside Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics and Hewlett-Packard programmable desktop calculators.

Weeks after the first Whole Earth Catalog hit the streets, on the afternoon of Monday, December 9, 1968, two thousand computer engineers packed into the arena of the San Francisco Civic Auditorium at 99 Grove Street for the Fall Joint Computer Conference to witness what technology historians universally call 'The Mother of All Demos.' Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of Bill Graham Civic Auditorium (99 Grove Street) and the historic Project One Warehouse (1380 Howard Street) toward private speculation—forcing Whole Earth Catalog founder Stewart Brand, computer pioneer Douglas Engelbart (1925–2013), and Community Memory / Homebrew engineer Lee Felsenstein and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

Sitting alone on the stage of 99 Grove Street before a twenty-two-foot-high video projection screen transmitted via microwave link from his Augmentation Research Center at Stanford Research Institute (SRI)—with Stewart Brand assisting the video camera crew—forty-three-year-old former Navy radar technician Douglas Engelbart wore a headset microphone and rested his right hand on a small wooden block rolling on two perpendicular metal wheels: the world's first computer mouse. Over the next ninety minutes at 99 Grove Street—in 1968, when almost every computer on earth still communicated via paper punch cards or clattering teletypes without screens—Engelbart calmly demonstrated live interactive windows, resizable outline text editing, clickable hypertext links jumping instantly between documents, vector graphics, and live split-screen video teleconferencing with a colleague thirty miles south fixing a shared grocery list and city map in real time.

When Engelbart finished at Civic Auditorium, the 2,000 engineers in the hall rose in a stunned standing ovation; yet most East Coast corporate computer executives flew home thinking Engelbart's vision of 'augmenting human intellect' was decades too expensive—leaving a small circle of Bay Area counterculture technologists and Xerox PARC researchers to carry the torch forward. Two years later, in 1971, right inside a vast four-story reinforced-concrete candy-factory warehouse at 1380 Howard Street (at the corner of 10th Street in San Francisco's South of Market), eighty architects, artists, and anti-war technologists founded an urban warehouse commune called Project One.

On the fourth floor of 1380 Howard Street, computer architect Pam Hardt-English and Stewart Brand helped arrange the loan of a massive $120,000 surplus Scientific Data Systems XDS-940 time-sharing mainframe computer from Transamerica Corporation to a collective of radical young engineers—including Efrem Lipkin, Lee Felsenstein, and Jude Milhon (later famous as the cyberpunk writer 'St. Jude,' who coined the aphorism 'Girls need modems'). Calling their collective Resource One, Felsenstein, Lipkin, and Milhon asked how a mainframe computer inside a South of Market commune could empower regular neighborhood residents who had never touched a computer—and in August 1973, they launched Community Memory: the world's first public computerized bulletin board system (BBS) and social network.

Dragging a noisy Teletype Model 33 ASR terminal connected via a 110-baud acoustic-coupler telephone modem out of 1380 Howard Street, they bolted it inside a cardboard-and-plywood sound-reducing box right next to the manual cardboard bulletin board at Leopold's Records (2518 Durant Avenue in Berkeley) and later at the Mission Public Library and Whole Earth Truck Store. For the first time in human history, any teenager, musician, poet, or neighbor walking into a record store or public library in 1973 could walk up to a free public computer terminal without a password or fee, type ADD followed by a message and any keywords they invented (RIDE SHARE, BAGEL FINDER, SYNTHESIZER, POETRY), and type FIND to search the shared community memory bank.

When Lee Felsenstein looked at how people spontaneously used Community Memory to organize bands, trade tools, and publish street poetry—and realized that maintaining a giant XDS-940 mainframe at 1380 Howard Street was too expensive—he designed the rugged, open-architecture Sol-20 personal computer and in March 1975 picked up a wooden gavel to moderate the legendary meetings of the Homebrew Computer Club, where Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs unveiled the Apple I and launched the personal computing age.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. December 9, 1968 at 99 Grove Street: Inside 'The Mother of All Demos'

What exactly did Douglas Engelbart and his SRI Augmentation Research Center team (including Bill English, who built the first prototype wooden mouse, and Jeff Rulifson) demonstrate on stage at the San Francisco Civic Auditorium on December 9, 1968? Operating their custom oN-Line System (NLS) over a leased telephone line and two microwave video links, Engelbart showed for the first time in a single integrated system: the X-Y mouse cursor, WYSIWYG text editing, hypertext linking, hierarchical file outlining, shared-screen collaborative editing with remote audio/video, and the five-key chord keyset.

2. 1380 Howard Street: How 'Community Memory' Invented Social Media in 1973

Why is the 1973 Community Memory project—hosted on the XDS-940 mainframe inside the Project One warehouse at 1380 Howard Street in SoMa—a foundational milestone of the modern internet? Long before the World Wide Web or commercial online services, Efrem Lipkin, Lee Felsenstein, Jude Milhon, and Mark Szpakowski placed public computer terminals in record shops and libraries where ordinary non-technical citizens invented user-generated folksonomy hashtags (keywords), peer-to-peer classifieds, and pseudonymous literary forums.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this story with three historical bridges: first, Stewart Brand's 1968 Whole Earth Catalog ('Access to Tools'), which fused ecological systems theory with personal technology; second, Civic Auditorium at 99 Grove Street, site of Douglas Engelbart's December 9, 1968 'Mother of All Demos'; and third, 1380 Howard Street in SoMa, home of Project One, Resource One, and the 1973 Community Memory mainframe.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Start at Civic Center Plaza outside the Bill Graham Civic Auditorium at 99 Grove Street, where both the Mechanics' Pavilion once stood in 1906 and Douglas Engelbart demonstrated the mouse and hypertext on December 9, 1968. Then walk five blocks south across Market Street to the corner of 10th and Howard streets to see the historic warehouse where Resource One and Community Memory wired the first public social network.
