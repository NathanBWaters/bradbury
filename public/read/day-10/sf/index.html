Bradbury 1000 • Night 10 • San Francisco Deep-Dive Story

Karl the Fog, the Golden Gate Wind Tunnel, and San Francisco's 17 Microclimates

San Francisco — Why temperature can swing 25°F in three miles across a seven-by-seven-mile peninsula—and how summer marine stratus keeps the coastal redwoods alive (Geological Time to Present) • 4 min read

“The coldest winter I ever spent was a summer in San Francisco.” — Apocryphal quip attributed to Mark Twain

Every July and August, an amusing meteorological drama plays out on the sidewalks of Fisherman’s Wharf and Powell Street: tourists who packed shorts and thin cotton t-shirts for their 'sunny California summer vacation' stand shivering in forty-mile-per-hour gusts and 54°F (12°C) gray mist, rushing into souvenir shops to buy fleece sweatshirts emblazoned with *SAN FRANCISCO*. Yet if those same freezing visitors hopped on a Muni bus or BART train and rode just two-and-a-half miles southeast over the spine of the hills into the Mission District or Potrero Hill, they would step out into 76°F (24°C) blue skies and warm sunshine.

How can a compact, seven-by-seven-mile peninsula at the exact same latitude as Athens, Greece, and Palermo, Sicily (37.7° N), contain at least seventeen distinct microclimates where adjacent neighborhoods experience different seasons at the exact same hour? And why is San Francisco coldest in July and warmest in late September and October? The answer lies in a grand atmospheric engine spanning three hundred miles from the Sierra Nevada foothills to the deep submarine upwelling trenches of the Pacific Ocean—producing the famous advection fog affectionately christened **'Karl the Fog'** by locals in 2010 (named after the misunderstood giant in Tim Burton’s film *Big Fish*).

Here is the three-part recipe that manufactures Karl the Fog every summer afternoon. First, ten to thirty miles offshore along the California coast, prevailing northwesterly winds blowing down the Pacific coastline push surface ocean water away from the continent due to the Earth’s rotation (*Ekman transport*). To replace that displaced surface water, frigid deep-ocean water from hundreds of feet down wells up to the surface, chilling the coastal Pacific right outside the Golden Gate to an icy 50°F to 54°F (10°C–12°C) all summer long.

Second, eighty miles inland across the Coast Ranges, the summer sun bakes the 450-mile-long Central Valley of California—from Redding and Sacramento down to Bakersfield—into a 100°F+ (38°C+) thermal furnace. As that superheated inland air rises rapidly toward the stratosphere, it creates a massive low-pressure thermal vacuum that sucks cool, dense, high-pressure marine air violently inward from the Pacific Ocean.

Third, when warm, moisture-laden air farther out over the Pacific gets pulled across that icy 52°F coastal upwelling belt just offshore, its temperature drops below the dew point and water vapor condenses into trillions of microscopic liquid droplets suspended in a 1,500-foot-thick marine stratus deck. Blocked along most of the California coast by the 2,000-foot wall of the Coast Ranges, that entire river of cold ocean fog searches for the only sea-level gap in six hundred miles of mountains: the one-mile-wide **Golden Gate Strait**.

Like water shooting through the nozzle of a garden hose, the fog accelerates through the Golden Gate and collides directly with the forty-three hills of San Francisco. Where the terrain is low—through the Golden Gate Park panhandle, the Sunset District, and the Alemany Gap along I-280—rivers of white fog pour inland at twenty-five knots. Where 900-foot ridges stand in its path—Twin Peaks (922 feet), Mount Sutro (908 feet), and Mount Davidson (928 feet)—the fog banks up like a breaking ocean wave, spilling halfway over the crest in a ghostly waterfall before evaporating into thin air on the leeward eastern slope! Consequently, neighborhoods sheltered behind those mountain shields—the Mission, Castro/Eureka Valley, Noe Valley, Potrero Hill, and Dogpatch—bask in a 'rain and fog shadow' twenty degrees warmer than Ocean Beach.

Far from being a mere nuisance to summer picnickers, San Francisco’s summer advection fog is the lifeblood of Northern California’s coastal ecology, especially the towering coast redwoods (*Sequoia sempervirens*) of Muir Woods and the Santa Cruz Mountains. Because California experiences nearly zero rainfall between May and October, university plant-physiology studies by Dr. Todd Dawson at UC Berkeley proved that coast redwoods obtain **30 to 40 percent of their entire annual water intake directly from summer fog drip**—absorbing moisture straight through the stomata of their high canopy needles and dripping gallons of condensed fog water onto their shallow roots below.

Stand atop the north peak of Twin Peaks (*Eureka Peak*) at 5:30 PM on an August evening as the golden western sun strikes the billowing white tide of Karl the Fog pouring through the International Orange towers of the Golden Gate Bridge and curling around Sutro Tower like an airborne glacier, and you realize why San Francisco is a city of poets, dreamers, and nocturnal readers: the fog wraps the hills in a cool, pearlescent blanket every evening, inviting you indoors to light a lamp and open a book.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Advection Fog vs. Radiation Fog: The Physics of the Marine Layer

Meteorologists distinguish between two primary types of fog in California: 'radiation fog' (such as the winter Tule fog of the Central Valley, which forms on calm, clear winter nights when the ground radiates heat into space) and San Francisco's 'advection fog,' which forms horizontally when warm, moist air moves ('advects') across a cold surface. Capped overhead by the Pacific Subtropical High's warm 'inversion layer' at roughly 1,200 to 2,000 feet elevation, San Francisco's marine layer cannot rise vertically—so it squeezes sideways through the Golden Gate, the San Bruno Gap (right across SFO airport), and the Estero Americano. 2. Why October Is San Francisco's True Summer

If you want 78°F (26°C) sunshine at Ocean Beach and Crissy Field, visit San Francisco in late September or October. Why? By autumn, the days shorten and the interior Central Valley cools down from 100°F to the 70s, shutting off the thermal vacuum pump that sucks the marine layer through the Golden Gate. At the same time, coastal Pacific water temperatures reach their annual warm peak around 59°F–60°F, and occasional offshore 'Diablo Winds' blow warm, dry air from the east toward the ocean, sweeping the fog miles out to sea.
