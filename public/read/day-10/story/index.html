The Open Window

Saki (H. H. Munro) (1914) • 6 min read

“Romance at short notice was her speciality.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Saki was the pen name of Hector Hugh Munro, a British writer who published The Open Window in 1914 in his collection Beasts and Super-Beasts. He was born in Burma in 1870 and raised in Devon, in England, by strict aunts, a childhood that left him with a lasting talent for portraying formidable women and mischievous children. As an adult he worked as a journalist and became a master of the very short, very witty story, in which polite Edwardian society, with its tea tables and good manners, is quietly turned inside out. It is one of the most taught short stories in English, and it takes only a few minutes to read. A nervous visitor is left alone with a self-possessed young girl, and she tells him a remarkable tale. Saki would die in the First World War only two years later, shot by a sniper in France. Listen for the timing of the jokes, and for how easily a confident voice can make people believe almost anything.

“My aunt will be down presently, Mr. Nuttel,” said a very self-possessed young lady of fifteen; “in the meantime you must try and put up with me.”

Framton Nuttel endeavoured to say the correct something which should duly flatter the niece of the moment without unduly discounting the aunt that was to come. Privately he doubted more than ever whether these formal visits on a succession of total strangers would do much towards helping the nerve cure which he was supposed to be undergoing.

“I know how it will be,” his sister had said when he was preparing to migrate to this rural retreat; “you will bury yourself down there and not speak to a living soul, and your nerves will be worse than ever from moping. I shall just give you letters of introduction to all the people I know there. Some of them, as far as I can remember, were quite nice.”

Framton wondered whether Mrs. Sappleton, the lady to whom he was presenting one of the letters of introduction, came into the nice division. “Do you know many of the people round here?” asked the niece, when she judged that they had had sufficient silent communion.

“Hardly a soul,” said Framton. “My sister was staying here, at the rectory, you know, some four years ago, and she gave me letters of introduction to some of the people here.” He made the last statement in a tone of distinct regret.

“Then you know practically nothing about my aunt?” pursued the self-possessed young lady. “Only her name and address,” admitted the caller. He was wondering whether Mrs. Sappleton was in the married or widowed state. An undefinable something about the room seemed to suggest masculine habitation.

“Her great tragedy happened just three years ago,” said the child; “that would be since your sister’s time.” “Her tragedy?” asked Framton; somehow in this restful country spot tragedies seemed out of place.

“You may wonder why we keep that window wide open on an October afternoon,” said the niece, indicating a large French window that opened on to a lawn. “It is quite warm for the time of the year,” said Framton; “but has that window got anything to do with the tragedy?”

“Out through that window, three years ago to a day, her husband and her two young brothers went off for their day’s shooting. They never came back. In crossing the moor to their favourite snipe-shooting ground they were all three engulfed in a treacherous piece of bog. It had been that dreadful wet summer, you know, and places that were safe in other years gave way suddenly without warning. Their bodies were never recovered. That was the dreadful part of it.” Here the child’s voice lost its self-possessed note and became falteringly human. “Poor aunt always thinks that they will come back someday, they and the little brown spaniel that was lost with them, and walk in at that window just as they used to do. That is why the window is kept open every evening till it is quite dusk. Poor dear aunt, she has often told me how they went out, her husband with his white waterproof coat over his arm, and Ronnie, her youngest brother, singing ‘Bertie, why do you bound?’ as he always did to tease her, because she said it got on her nerves. Do you know, sometimes on still, quiet evenings like this, I almost get a creepy feeling that they will all walk in through that window—”

She broke off with a little shudder. It was a relief to Framton when the aunt bustled into the room with a whirl of apologies for being late in making her appearance. “I hope Vera has been amusing you?” she said. “She has been very interesting,” said Framton. “I hope you don’t mind the open window,” said Mrs. Sappleton briskly; “my husband and brothers will be home directly from shooting, and they always come in this way. They’ve been out for snipe in the marshes today, so they’ll make a fine mess over my poor carpets. So like you menfolk, isn’t it?”

She rattled on cheerfully about the shooting and the scarcity of birds, and the prospects for duck in the winter. To Framton it was all purely horrible. He made a desperate but only partially successful effort to turn the talk on to a less ghastly topic; he was conscious that his hostess was giving him only a fragment of her attention, and her eyes were constantly straying past him to the open window and the lawn beyond. It was certainly an unfortunate coincidence that he should have paid his visit on this tragic anniversary.

“The doctors agree in ordering me complete rest, an absence of mental excitement, and avoidance of anything in the nature of violent physical exercise,” announced Framton, who laboured under the tolerably wide-spread delusion that total strangers and chance acquaintances are hungry for the least detail of one’s ailments and infirmities, their cause and cure. “On the matter of diet they are not so much in agreement,” he continued. “No?” said Mrs. Sappleton, in a voice which only replaced a yawn at the last moment. Then she suddenly brightened into alert attention—but not to what Framton was saying. “Here they are at last!” she cried. “Just in time for tea, and don’t they look as if they were muddy up to the eyes!”

Framton shivered slightly and turned towards the niece with a look intended to convey sympathetic comprehension. The child was staring out through the open window with a dazed horror in her eyes. In a chill shock of nameless fear Framton swung round in his seat and looked in the same direction. In the deepening twilight three figures were walking across the lawn towards the window; they all carried guns under their arms, and one of them was additionally burdened with a white coat hung over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel kept close at their heels. Noiselessly they neared the house, and then a hoarse young voice chanted out of the dusk: “I said, Bertie, why do you bound?”

Framton grabbed wildly at his stick and hat; the hall door, the gravel drive, and the front gate were dimly noted stages in his headlong retreat. A cyclist coming along the road had to run into the hedge to avoid imminent collision. “Here we are, my dear,” said the bearer of the white mackintosh, coming in through the window, “fairly muddy, but most of it’s dry. Who was that who bolted out as we came up?” “A most extraordinary man, a Mr. Nuttel,” said Mrs. Sappleton; “could only talk about his illnesses, and dashed off without a word of goodbye or apology when you arrived. One would think he had seen a ghost.” “I expect it was the spaniel,” said the niece calmly; “he told me he had a horror of dogs. He was once hunted into a cemetery somewhere on the banks of the Ganges by a pack of pariah dogs, and had to spend the night in a newly dug grave with the creatures snarling and grinning and foaming just above him. Enough to make anyone lose their nerve.” Romance at short notice was her speciality.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, The Open Window is a comic anecdote with a twist, and the plot can be told in a minute. A nervous young man named Framton Nuttel arrives at a country house in England carrying a letter of introduction from his sister. He has been sent to the countryside to rest his nerves, and he finds himself waiting in the drawing room for the lady of the house, a Mrs. Sappleton. Her fifteen-year-old niece, Vera, entertains him. Vera has a story. Three years ago, she says, her aunt's husband and her two young brothers went out through that very window to shoot, and they were swallowed by a bog and never seen again. Her aunt has kept the window open every evening since, in the belief that they will walk back in. When the aunt appears and says cheerfully that her husband and brothers will soon be home from shooting, Framton is horrified. The three men come striding across the lawn, one of them singing, and Framton flees in terror. Vera calmly explains to the family that he must have been frightened by the dog. The last line is the famous one: romance at short notice was her speciality.

What is the story really about? On one level, it is about the power of a good storyteller and the credulity of an audience. Vera does not lie clumsily. She builds her tale from small, convincing details: the white waterproof coat over the husband's arm, the youngest brother singing a teasing song to annoy his sister, the particular terrible summer when the bogs were treacherous. She even makes her voice falter at the right moment. Framton, who is already fragile, is helpless before such artistry.

The story is also about nerves, and about the way a mind prepared to find horror will find it. Framton has been sent away to cure his anxiety, and he talks only about his illnesses. He is a man who expects the worst, and Vera feeds that expectation. When the supposed ghosts appear, it is his own fear, as much as her story, that sends him running.

There is a third layer, subtler and more mischievous. The story shows how thin the surface of polite society is. In a quiet drawing room, over a pause in a conversation, a child can invent a tragedy and a ghost story, and nobody, including the narrator, objects. Saki delights in revealing the wildness beneath the good manners.

Finally, the closing joke does more than amuse. Having seen Vera deceive Framton, we watch her immediately deceive her aunt with a second fabrication, about the Ganges and the pariah dogs. The ending shows that for Vera, storytelling is a reflex, a pleasure in itself, and not a means to anything else.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Open Window is a nearly flawless miniature, and it is worth being specific about why, before looking at the few places where it is less impressive.

The first thing to admire is economy. In well under two thousand words, Saki establishes a setting, three characters, a mystery, a horror, and a reversal. Nothing is wasted. The opening line, in which the self-possessed young lady announces that her aunt will be down presently, instantly sets up the mismatch between Framton's anxiety and Vera's composure. Every detail does at least double duty: the open window is a stage prop for the false tragedy and then the doorway through which the apparent ghosts arrive.

The second is comic timing. The story depends on pace and on a kind of dramatic irony. We are not told right away that Vera is lying, but Saki drops tiny hints, such as the smoothness of her details, and a sharp reader may sense something. Then the aunt begins to chatter about her husband's shooting, and the dreadful logic of the situation unfolds with the neatness of a stage farce. The final reveal, with the second lie, is a masterstroke, because it recasts everything we just read as proof of her genius.

The third is the prose. Saki's sentences are urbane and dry. His descriptions of Framton, who endeavours to say the correct something that should flatter the niece without discounting the aunt, are packed with gentle mockery. The narration never raises its voice, and the contrast between the elegant tone and the absurd content gives the story its charm.

Now the limitations. The story is slight. It is a joke, beautifully built, but a joke all the same, and some readers feel that it has little emotional weight. Characters are types: the nervous invalid, the brisk hostess, the precocious girl. Framton is more a target than a person, and the reader is invited to laugh at him without much sympathy.

There is also the question of cruelty. The story's humor depends on a young girl terrorizing a fragile man for her own amusement. Saki keeps the tone light, but if you think about it for more than a moment, the prank is quite nasty, and no one is held accountable. Whether you find this delicious or distasteful depends on your temperament.

Finally, the twist, though skillful, can feel predictable to a modern reader who has met a hundred similar stories. It is perhaps unfair to hold that against it, since it helped create the template. In the end, the story is a gem, small and sharp, and what it lacks in depth it makes up for in precision.

III. The Impact It Had

The Open Window did a great deal for Saki's reputation, though that reputation was cut short. He published it in 1914, and within two years he was dead. When the First World War began, Saki, who was in his early forties and could have avoided service, chose to enlist as a private. He was killed by a sniper in France in November 1916. According to a story widely repeated, his last words were an order to a fellow soldier to put out a cigarette whose glow might draw fire. Whether or not the words are exact, they have the dry spirit of his writing. His sister, Ethel, later destroyed many of his papers, so the record of his life is thin, which makes his stories all the more central to how he is remembered.

Within his career, this story became the best known of a large body of short fiction. He had earlier published collections such as Reginald and The Chronicles of Clovis, filled with brilliant, malicious sketches of Edwardian society. Beasts and Super-Beasts, in which this appeared, was among his last books. The Open Window has since been reprinted more often than almost any of them.

Its influence on other writers is hard to measure precisely, but Saki is regularly named as a model for later humorists of the English tradition. P. G. Wodehouse, Evelyn Waugh and later writers of comic fiction, including Roald Dahl in his dark tales for adults, are often mentioned as heirs to his blend of elegance and cruelty. The idea of the cunning child who runs rings round the adults, and the polished, ironical short story ending on a sting, can be traced through a good deal of twentieth-century English humor.

In education, the story has had an enormous second life. It is probably one of the most taught short stories in the English-speaking world, especially in secondary schools. Teachers love it because it is short, funny, and a perfect example of an unreliable narrator, a twist ending, irony, and characterization through dialogue. Many people first met the idea of the surprise ending through this story.

It has also been adapted for radio, stage and film, and it is a favorite for school performances. Its basic idea, a fib so well told that it is believed, has entered popular culture as a model of the elaborate prank.

More broadly, the story helped define a particular tone: brittle, amused, and a little merciless, the tone of the Edwardian comedy of manners with a hint of the macabre. That tone remains a recognizable style in English writing today.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love The Open Window first of all because it is such a pleasure to read. It is a story with the satisfying click of a perfect joke: everything slots into place, and the ending delivers exactly the right amount of surprise. Readers often say they smiled or laughed out loud at the last line, and that they immediately wanted to read it again to catch the clues.

They love Vera. She is one of the great mischievous children in literature, a young person of dazzling poise who treats the adult world as a stage for her inventions. Readers delight in her audacity, her calm and her skill. There is something liberating about a character who exercises such power with such good humor. Many readers admit that they secretly side with her, and enjoy the way she runs rings around the grown-ups.

They also love the comic portrait of Framton Nuttel. His obsession with his own symptoms, his stumbling attempts at polite conversation and his headlong flight past the cyclist are drawn with affectionate mockery. The image of him grabbing his stick and hat and bolting down the gravel drive is one of the most vivid pieces of physical comedy in short fiction.

Writers admire the technique. Saki's handling of dialogue, in which the character of each speaker emerges through a few lines, is considered a model. The way the story seeds details, such as the window, the coat, the song, and the spaniel, and then pays them off, is widely studied. Readers who enjoy the craft of fiction see it as a lesson in how little is needed to create a complete world.

Another quality that readers prize is the wit of the sentences. Saki's gift for the dry aside, the phrase that is both elegant and savage, makes the story a pleasure at the level of the line. A reader might not remember the plot weeks later, but will remember the tone.

Finally, there is the sense of play. The story is a celebration of imagination for its own sake. Vera's tales have no purpose except the joy of inventing, and many readers find this infectious. In a world that often demands that stories mean something, it is refreshing to read one that simply enjoys itself. That playful, mischievous spirit is what has kept it alive for over a century.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Honesty requires admitting that The Open Window has not been the subject of heated critical debate. It is a short comic story, widely loved, and most of the commentary on it has been appreciative. Documentation of specific contemporary reviews is thin. But critics and readers over the years have raised several fair objections, which are worth considering.

The most common is that the story is slight. Compared with the richer, deeper fiction of near contemporaries such as Chekhov or Henry James, Saki's work can seem like clever trifles. Some critics have said that his stories are brilliant surfaces without much beneath them, witty without being wise. The Open Window, perfect as it is, has been held up as an example: a joke with no larger vision.

A second objection is about cruelty. Saki's humor is often mean-spirited, and the story's pleasure depends on a vulnerable man being frightened out of his wits. Some readers find this uncomfortable, and especially the fact that the narrative seems to take Vera's side. Her mischief has real consequences for Framton, who is, after all, a sick person, and the story offers no sympathy for him. Critics have occasionally noted a streak of misanthropy in Saki's work, a contempt for the dull, the weak and the earnest.

Related to this is the way the story treats nervous illness. Framton's condition is the engine of the joke, and a modern reader may feel uneasy about laughing at a man's anxiety. In 1914, rest cures for nervous complaints were common and often mocked. Today, many readers are more alert to the cruelty of ridiculing mental distress, and some find the story harder to enjoy as a result.

A fourth line of criticism concerns the social world of the story. It is a comfortable, upper-middle-class English country house, with servants unseen and a world of leisure. Saki was himself part of that world and satirized it, but his satire was gentle, and some critics have argued that it never really challenges the class assumptions it depicts. He is a satirist who enjoys the manners he mocks.

Finally, some have argued that the twist has been done to death since, and that the story now reads as a familiar mechanism. This is more a comment on its influence than on its quality, and most readers find that it still delights. The criticisms, then, are real but modest, and they mostly concern what the story does not attempt, not what it does.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Open Window sits in the tradition of the English comedy of manners, a form that stretches from Jane Austen to Oscar Wilde. In this tradition, the comedy arises from the gap between how people are supposed to behave and what they really do. Wilde is perhaps the nearest ancestor. His epigrams, his love of paradox and his sense of the absurdity beneath politeness all echo in Saki. But where Wilde's comedies tend to be warm and theatrical, Saki's are colder and more cruel, and often end with a sting.

It also belongs to the tradition of the short story with a twist, a form associated in America with O. Henry and in France with Guy de Maupassant. Saki shares their love of the surprise ending but uses it with a more cynical, aristocratic touch. His sketches of malicious children and fierce aunts reflect his own upbringing, and they carry a sense of the grotesque that connects him to the Victorian tradition of dark humor in writers like Lewis Carroll, whose Wonderland is also a place where manners are made absurd.

The story also touches the ghost story tradition, though as a parody. Edwardian readers loved tales of hauntings, and Saki takes the genre's machinery, the tragic backstory, the open window, the figures returning at dusk, and turns it into a prank. The effect is a kind of anti-ghost story, in which the supernatural is revealed as a fabrication. In this it is a useful companion to more serious hauntings, since it shows how a ghost story works by suggestion.

What descends from Saki is a line of British humor, dry, elegant and a little macabre. Wodehouse's brisk comedies, Waugh's satires and the dark tales of Roald Dahl, as well as the cunning children of contemporary comic fiction, share the family resemblance. The figure of the clever, amoral child who outwits grown-ups is a staple of comedy, from the stories of Richmal Crompton's William to modern television.

Saki's life adds a poignant frame. A writer who spent his career mocking the comfortable certainties of Edwardian England died in the war that ended that world, and his stories are now read as brilliant snapshots of a society just before it vanished.

Why does the story still matter? Because it is a perfect small machine, and because it contains a lasting truth about how easily we are led by a confident voice. In an age of persuasion and misinformation, Vera is a useful figure to keep in mind. Read it for the pleasure of the joke, and then notice how well it was built.
