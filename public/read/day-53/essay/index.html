Of the Passing of the Firstborn

W. E. B. Du Bois (1903) • 11 min read

Unto you a child is born, sang the bit of yellow paper that fluttered into my room one brown October morning.

Key Context & Why It Matters

W. E. B. Du Bois wrote this chapter for The Souls of Black Folk, published in 1903, and it is the book's most personal. In it he tells of the birth and death of his first child, a son named Burghardt, who was born in 1897 and died of illness in Atlanta at about eighteen months old. Du Bois was by then a prominent scholar, but here he sets aside the sociologist's voice and speaks as a grieving father. The chapter is a rare thing, an essay in which private sorrow and public injustice are fused. The joy of a new child, the wonder of watching him grow, and the shattering loss are all described with great tenderness. And in the middle of the grief, Du Bois sees the Veil, his name for the color line, fall across his baby's future. It is a short, aching piece, and one of the most moving in American literature.

“Unto you a child is born,” sang the bit of yellow paper that fluttered into my room one brown October morning. Then the fear of fatherhood mingled wildly with the joy of creation; I wondered how it looked and how it felt⁠—what were its eyes, and how its hair curled and crumpled itself. And I thought in awe of her⁠—she who had slept with Death to tear a man-child from underneath her heart, while I was unconsciously wandering. I fled to my wife and child, repeating the while to myself half wonderingly, “Wife and child? Wife and child?”⁠—fled fast and faster than boat and steam-car, and yet must ever impatiently await them; away from the hard-voiced city, away from the flickering sea into my own Berkshire Hills that sit all sadly guarding the gates of Massachusetts.

Up the stairs I ran to the wan mother and whimpering babe, to the sanctuary on whose altar a life at my bidding had offered itself to win a life, and won. What is this tiny formless thing, this newborn wail from an unknown world⁠—all head and voice? I handle it curiously, and watch perplexed its winking, breathing, and sneezing. I did not love it then; it seemed a ludicrous thing to love; but her I loved, my girl-mother, she whom now I saw unfolding like the glory of the morning⁠—the transfigured woman. Through her I came to love the wee thing, as it grew strong; as its little soul unfolded itself in twitter and cry and half-formed word, and as its eyes caught the gleam and flash of life. How beautiful he was, with his olive-tinted flesh and dark gold ringlets, his eyes of mingled blue and brown, his perfect little limbs, and the soft voluptuous roll which the blood of Africa had moulded into his features! I held him in my arms, after we had sped far away from our Southern home⁠—held him, and glanced at the hot red soil of Georgia and the breathless city of a hundred hills, and felt a vague unrest. Why was his hair tinted with gold? An evil omen was golden hair in my life. Why had not the brown of his eyes crushed out and killed the blue?⁠—for brown were his father’s eyes, and his father’s father’s. And thus in the Land of the Color-line I saw, as it fell across my baby, the shadow of the Veil.

Within the Veil was he born, said I; and there within shall he live⁠—a Negro and a Negro’s son. Holding in that little head⁠—ah, bitterly!⁠—the unbowed pride of a hunted race, clinging with that tiny dimpled hand⁠—ah, wearily!⁠—to a hope not hopeless but unhopeful, and seeing with those bright wondering eyes that peer into my soul a land whose freedom is to us a mockery and whose liberty a lie. I saw the shadow of the Veil as it passed over my baby, I saw the cold city towering above the bloodred land. I held my face beside his little cheek, showed him the star-children and the twinkling lights as they began to flash, and stilled with an evensong the unvoiced terror of my life.

So sturdy and masterful he grew, so filled with bubbling life, so tremulous with the unspoken wisdom of a life but eighteen months distant from the All-life⁠—we were not far from worshipping this revelation of the divine, my wife and I. Her own life builded and moulded itself upon the child; he tinged her every dream and idealized her every effort. No hands but hers must touch and garnish those little limbs; no dress or frill must touch them that had not wearied her fingers; no voice but hers could coax him off to Dreamland, and she and he together spoke some soft and unknown tongue and in it held communion. I too mused above his little white bed; saw the strength of my own arm stretched onward through the ages through the newer strength of his; saw the dream of my black fathers stagger a step onward in the wild phantasm of the world; heard in his baby voice the voice of the Prophet that was to rise within the Veil.

And so we dreamed and loved and planned by fall and winter, and the full flush of the long Southern spring, till the hot winds rolled from the fetid Gulf, till the roses shivered and the still stern sun quivered its awful light over the hills of Atlanta. And then one night the little feet pattered wearily to the wee white bed, and the tiny hands trembled; and a warm flushed face tossed on the pillow, and we knew baby was sick. Ten days he lay there⁠—a swift week and three endless days, wasting, wasting away. Cheerily the mother nursed him the first days, and laughed into the little eyes that smiled again. Tenderly then she hovered round him, till the smile fled away and Fear crouched beside the little bed.

Then the day ended not, and night was a dreamless terror, and joy and sleep slipped away. I hear now that Voice at midnight calling me from dull and dreamless trance⁠—crying, “The Shadow of Death! The Shadow of Death!” Out into the starlight I crept, to rouse the gray physician⁠—the Shadow of Death, the Shadow of Death. The hours trembled on; the night listened; the ghastly dawn glided like a tired thing across the lamplight. Then we two alone looked upon the child as he turned toward us with great eyes, and stretched his stringlike hands⁠—the Shadow of Death! And we spoke no word, and turned away.

He died at eventide, when the sun lay like a brooding sorrow above the western hills, veiling its face; when the winds spoke not, and the trees, the great green trees he loved, stood motionless. I saw his breath beat quicker and quicker, pause, and then his little soul leapt like a star that travels in the night and left a world of darkness in its train. The day changed not; the same tall trees peeped in at the windows, the same green grass glinted in the setting sun. Only in the chamber of death writhed the world’s most piteous thing⁠—a childless mother.

I shirk not. I long for work. I pant for a life full of striving. I am no coward, to shrink before the rugged rush of the storm, nor even quail before the awful shadow of the Veil. But hearken, O Death! Is not this my life hard enough⁠—is not that dull land that stretches its sneering web about me cold enough⁠—is not all the world beyond these four little walls pitiless enough, but that thou must needs enter here⁠—thou, O Death? About my head the thundering storm beat like a heartless voice, and the crazy forest pulsed with the curses of the weak; but what cared I, within my home beside my wife and baby boy? Wast thou so jealous of one little coign of happiness that thou must needs enter there⁠—thou, O Death?

A perfect life was his, all joy and love, with tears to make it brighter⁠—sweet as a summer’s day beside the Housatonic. The world loved him; the women kissed his curls, the men looked gravely into his wonderful eyes, and the children hovered and fluttered about him. I can see him now, changing like the sky from sparkling laughter to darkening frowns, and then to wondering thoughtfulness as he watched the world. He knew no color-line, poor dear⁠—and the Veil, though it shadowed him, had not yet darkened half his sun. He loved the white matron, he loved his black nurse; and in his little world walked souls alone, uncolored and unclothed. I⁠—yea, all men⁠—are larger and purer by the infinite breadth of that one little life. She who in simple clearness of vision sees beyond the stars said when he had flown, “He will be happy There; he ever loved beautiful things.” And I, far more ignorant, and blind by the web of mine own weaving, sit alone winding words and muttering, “If still he be, and he be There, and there be a There, let him be happy, O Fate!”

Blithe was the morning of his burial, with bird and song and sweet-smelling flowers. The trees whispered to the grass, but the children sat with hushed faces. And yet it seemed a ghostly unreal day⁠—the wraith of Life. We seemed to rumble down an unknown street behind a little white bundle of posies, with the shadow of a song in our ears. The busy city dinned about us; they did not say much, those pale-faced hurrying men and women; they did not say much⁠—they only glanced and said, “Niggers!”

We could not lay him in the ground there in Georgia, for the earth there is strangely red; so we bore him away to the northward, with his flowers and his little folded hands. In vain, in vain!⁠—for where, O God! beneath thy broad blue sky shall my dark baby rest in peace⁠—where Reverence dwells, and Goodness, and a Freedom that is free?

All that day and all that night there sat an awful gladness in my heart⁠—nay, blame me not if I see the world thus darkly through the Veil⁠—and my soul whispers ever to me saying, “Not dead, not dead, but escaped; not bond, but free.” No bitter meanness now shall sicken his baby heart till it die a living death, no taunt shall madden his happy boyhood. Fool that I was to think or wish that this little soul should grow choked and deformed within the Veil! I might have known that yonder deep unworldly look that ever and anon floated past his eyes was peering far beyond this narrow Now. In the poise of his little curl-crowned head did there not sit all that wild pride of being which his father had hardly crushed in his own heart? For what, forsooth, shall a Negro want with pride amid the studied humiliations of fifty million fellows? Well sped, my boy, before the world had dubbed your ambition insolence, had held your ideals unattainable, and taught you to cringe and bow. Better far this nameless void that stops my life than a sea of sorrow for you.

Idle words; he might have borne his burden more bravely than we⁠—aye, and found it lighter too, some day; for surely, surely this is not the end. Surely there shall yet dawn some mighty morning to lift the Veil and set the prisoned free. Not for me⁠—I shall die in my bonds⁠—but for fresh young souls who have not known the night and waken to the morning; a morning when men ask of the workman, not “Is he white?” but “Can he work?” When men ask artists, not “Are they black?” but “Do they know?” Some morning this may be, long, long years to come. But now there wails, on that dark shore within the Veil, the same deep voice, Thou shalt forego! And all have I foregone at that command, and with small complaint⁠—all save that fair young form that lies so coldly wed with death in the nest I had builded.

If one must have gone, why not I? Why may I not rest me from this restlessness and sleep from this wide waking? Was not the world’s alembic, Time, in his young hands, and is not my time waning? Are there so many workers in the vineyard that the fair promise of this little body could lightly be tossed away? The wretched of my race that line the alleys of the nation sit fatherless and unmothered; but Love sat beside his cradle, and in his ear Wisdom waited to speak. Perhaps now he knows the All-love, and needs not to be wise. Sleep, then, child⁠—sleep till I sleep and waken to a baby voice and the ceaseless patter of little feet⁠—above the Veil.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The chapter follows the plain arc of a short life. A yellow telegram arrives on a brown October morning with the news that a child is born. Du Bois, away from home, feels fear and joy mixed together and rushes to his wife and baby in the Berkshire Hills of Massachusetts. At first he does not love the child at all. He calls it a ludicrous thing to love, all head and voice, and what he loves is his wife, whom he sees transformed into a mother. Only through her does he come to love the baby. This honesty is the first thing to notice, because it makes everything after it believable. Love grows in him slowly, the way it truly does for many parents.

Then comes the long middle of the chapter, a season of delight. He describes the boy's first smiles and babble, his curls, his dancing at the window, and the way the father sees in him a continuation of his own life, the strength of my own arm stretched onward through the ages. The child becomes a vessel for hope, and Du Bois imagines him as the prophet who might rise within the Veil. This is a powerful phrase, since the Veil is Du Bois's image for the line that separates Black Americans from white society, a thin cloth that shows the world but shades the person living behind it. It means that a Black child is born into a world that has already assigned him limits.

Midway through the chapter the tone changes. Du Bois says he saw the shadow of the Veil fall across his baby, and he looked at the cold city towering over the blood-red land of the South. He asks himself whether he really wants his son to grow up there, to live, as he puts it, a Negro and a Negro's son. A father's love is already tangled with dread about what the world will do to a Black boy. Then the baby falls sick, and in the night the father runs to wake the gray physician, hearing the words the Shadow of Death repeated like a refrain. The child dies.

The most startling turn is how Du Bois responds. He confesses that for a day and a night there sat an awful gladness in his heart. In his grief he thinks, not dead, not dead, but escaped, not bond but free. His son will never have to meet the insult and the closed doors. Then he rebukes himself, in a way, by saying that the child might have borne the burden more bravely than he did. The close of the chapter is a prayer of renewal, imagining a morning when men ask of the workman not is he white but can he work, and ending with a lullaby for the dead child. The meaning is that for a Black father in America in 1903, personal sorrow and political injustice cannot be separated, and a child's death becomes a window on what a whole society takes from its children.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a case where the greatest strengths and the greatest risks lie close together. The chapter works because of its honesty and restraint at the crucial moments, even while it is written in a very elevated style. The admission that he did not love the baby at first prevents the chapter from sliding into sentimentality, because it shows a real man rather than an idealized father. When he later describes the boy's small gestures, the swaying and the dancing, we feel the force because we have watched the love form.

The structure is also well judged. The chapter begins in joy and ends in grief, but in the middle, the intrusion of the Veil complicates both. Instead of keeping the personal and political apart, Du Bois lets them flow into each other. At the moment of greatest tenderness he looks at his sleeping child and sees the long history of his race, and at the moment of greatest loss he finds a terrible comfort in escape. That paradox, that the death of a beloved child might feel like freedom, is the most daring thing in the piece, and it works because it is so unsettling and so humanly truthful.

The prose is a mixed matter. At its best it is incantatory, with refrains such as wife and child, the Shadow of Death, and above the Veil, that give the chapter a rhythm like a hymn. The final lullaby, sleep, then, child, is quietly devastating. But the style can also be overwrought. Du Bois piles up exalted language, the glory of the morning, the wild phantasm of the world, the alembic of Time, in a late Victorian register that can feel inflated, especially in the passages of paternal hope. A modern reader may find that some of these sentences lean toward purple, and that the grief sometimes speaks in a more literary voice than we expect from a man in agony.

There is also a question about the use of the child. Some readers feel a faint discomfort in the way the dead baby becomes a symbol, a prophet who might have risen, a vessel of racial destiny. It is natural for a grieving father to do this, but it means the child himself, with his particular personality, appears somewhat less than the idea of him. Du Bois does give details, the little patter of feet and the baby's laugh, but the poetic frame is always lifting us away from them.

Yet these are small faults. The chapter is short and tightly built, and its force is cumulative. It remains one of the most powerful examples in American prose of how to turn private loss into a public truth without betraying the privacy, and the final images linger because they were earned.

III. The Impact It Had

The immediate impact of this chapter was to give readers of The Souls of Black Folk a face to attach to the book's abstractions. Up to this point Du Bois had written about the problem of the color line in the language of history and sociology. This chapter shows what the color line means in the life of one family, and it helped the book reach a wide audience that might have been put off by pure argument. Many early readers, Black and white, cited it as the passage that moved them most, and it gave the book some of its lasting emotional force.

It also changed the way we think about the genre of the personal essay in American literature. Black writers had long used autobiography as a form of testimony, but this chapter is unusual in joining intimate family grief with an analysis of racism. The pattern, in which a private tragedy opens into a public condition, is an ancestor of later works. You can see it in Richard Wright's memoir, in James Baldwin's essays addressed to his nephew and to the reader of his generation, and in modern writing by Black parents about fear for their children. Ta-Nehisi Coates's Between the World and Me, written as a letter to his teenage son, is often compared to Du Bois and Baldwin in the way it ties love for a child to the danger of the world. It is fair to say that this chapter is one of the roots of that tradition.

For Du Bois himself, the chapter marks a turning point in his thinking. The death of his son occurred in Atlanta in 1899, in the same period when the city and the South were seared by racial violence, and Du Bois later said that these years convinced him that scholarship alone could not solve the problem of race. He moved toward activism, helping to found the Niagara Movement and then the NAACP. It would be too simple to say that the child's death caused the change, but the chapter shows that the personal and the political were bound together in his motives.

In education, the chapter is widely taught in courses on American literature and African American studies, and it appears in anthologies as a standalone essay. It is often used to discuss the Veil, to teach the role of personal narrative in political writing, and to show how emotion can work as evidence. Many readers who have lost a child have turned to it as a kind of companion in mourning.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing readers love is the rawness of feeling. It is hard to find a better description of the ambivalent first days of fatherhood than the passage in which Du Bois admits that the baby seemed a ludicrous thing to love. That sentence opens a door for readers, especially fathers, who may have felt the same and been ashamed of it. When the love arrives, it comes with a force that has been earned, and the glimpses of the boy dancing in the window or catching the gleam of life in his eyes are among the most tender in the book.

They also love the way the chapter does not flinch from the hardest part. The idea of an awful gladness at the moment of loss is one that many grieving people recognize but few have said aloud. The sense of release, of a loved one escaping suffering, can coexist with devastation, and Du Bois names it without apology. Readers who have lost children have often written that the chapter helped them because it did not offer false consolation.

Readers have admired, too, the way a father's fear becomes a form of political insight. Many Black readers have recognized in the chapter their own parents' anxiety, the knowledge that to love a child in America is to worry about what the world will say to him. The line about a Negro and a Negro's son compresses a whole history into a few words. White readers, for their part, have often said that this chapter was the one that made them understand what the color line meant in lived terms rather than as an idea.

The sound of the chapter is a pleasure as well. The repeated phrases carry a quiet music, and the closing lullaby, with its promise of waking to a baby's voice and the patter of little feet above the Veil, has a gentleness that gives the book some rest after its harder arguments. The final vision of a morning in which a person is judged by whether he can work or whether he knows, not by color, lifts the mourning into a prayer for the future.

Finally, readers have loved the courage of its subject matter. In 1903 it was unusual for a prominent male scholar to write publicly about grief, tenderness and the daily details of a baby's life. That choice has drawn admiration from later readers who see it as an early example of a man letting himself be vulnerable on the page, and as an act of respect to the child, who is made to live in language as he could not live in the world.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a work this moving has drawn some criticism, though the objections are fewer and gentler than those aimed at other chapters of the book. The most common is about style. Critics have said that Du Bois's rhetoric at times overwhelms the feeling, that the ornate diction of the period, its Biblical echoes and classical allusions, stands between the reader and the grief. Some have found passages in the middle sentimental in the manner of Victorian writing about children, with their talk of angels and glory, and have felt that the father's visions of his son's greatness are too idealized.

A related complaint is about the sense that the child is turned into a symbol. A few modern readers, especially those who prefer a more plain and specific style of memoir, find that the baby never quite becomes a person on the page. He is a prophet in waiting, a hope for the race, and then an escaped soul. The individual child, with his temperament and his quirks, is only glimpsed. Du Bois's wife, Nina, receives even less attention, and feminist readers have pointed out that the mother, who bears the child and shares the loss, is mostly a presence in the background. She appears as the girl-mother, transfigured, but her grief is not voiced.

There is also a debate about the chapter's treatment of the theme of escape. The claim that the child is free in death can read, to some, as a troubling consolation that edges close to resignation. It would be unfair to read it as endorsing despair, since the chapter ends with a hope for a better morning, but it has raised questions about whether the logic of the Veil can make death look like a mercy, and about what that says about the world that produces such thoughts. Du Bois himself seems to feel the tension, since he immediately admits that the boy might have borne the burden more bravely than he.

A last objection is historical. Scholars have noted that the chapter does not explain the circumstances of the death clearly. We are not told, in the essay itself, whether better medical care might have saved the boy or what role segregation played in his treatment, and some later writers have supplied context from Du Bois's other writings and letters. The essay chooses poetry over detail, which is an artistic decision but one that leaves a few questions unanswered. These are the points on which critics have pressed, and none of them diminishes the central power of the piece.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter belongs to the old and honorable tradition of the elegy for a child. Writers have always found in the death of a young one a test of language and faith. Ben Jonson's poem on his dead son, Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay Experience, with its famous confession that his own son's death left him strangely unmoved, all belong in this family. Du Bois's piece is a worthy member. What distinguishes it is that, unlike most of these, it places the loss within a social structure, treating the child as someone whose life would have been shaped by a color line.

It also belongs to the tradition of African American autobiography and testimony, from the slave narratives to the work of Du Bois's contemporaries. In the lineage that follows, it prepares the ground for the personal essay as a political form. Baldwin's The Fire Next Time, with its letter to his nephew, descends from it, as do the memoirs of Richard Wright and the reflections of later writers such as Toni Morrison, who wrote about the mother's love and loss in Beloved, and Coates. Each of these takes up the essential Du Bois insight that to be a Black parent is to hold love and fear together.

Within The Souls of Black Folk the chapter has a precise function. Its place in the book's structure, after the chapters on politics and education and before the later chapters of stories and songs, gives the volume a human center. The Veil, a metaphor the book introduces in its opening pages, is here no longer an idea but a shadow on a baby's face. Without this chapter, the book would be a work of ideas, and with it the book becomes a testimony.

The chapter also connects to a larger literary movement. Late nineteenth century writing in America was marked by realism, the effort to describe ordinary life honestly, and by a surviving Romantic tradition that prized feeling and sublime language. Du Bois fuses both. His grounded details, the telegram, the stairs, the doctor in the night, are realist, while his flights toward prophecy are Romantic. This mixture is one reason the piece feels both modern and old.

Why read it now? Because it demonstrates how a single human story can carry the weight of a whole social reality without becoming propaganda. Because it speaks to anyone who has lost someone, whatever their background. And because it reminds us that the fight for justice is, at its root, a fight for the right of children to live, to learn, and to grow up without a shadow across their faces. It takes only a few minutes to read and may stay with a reader for years.
