Later Than You Think

Fritz Leiber (1950) • 12 min read

“A blackboard with large colored crayons hinted equally at the schoolroom and the studio.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Later Than You Think, published by Fritz Leiber in 1950, is a short science fiction story built around a single, carefully hidden reversal. Leiber wrote it in the first years after Hiroshima, when ordinary people were just beginning to absorb the idea that human beings could end their own civilization. The story is nothing but a conversation. An explorer has come home from deep space, and an archeologist friend is telling him about an astonishing discovery on Earth: the buried records of an intelligent species that rose, flourished and vanished. The explorer wants to know what they were like. The archeologist is in no hurry to say. Pay attention to how the two of them talk, to what they take for granted, and to the strange room around them. The story is an old idea, the rise and fall of civilizations, turned inside out by the point of view.

Obviously the Archeologist’s study belonged to an era vastly distant from today. Familiar similarities here and there only sharpened the feeling of alienage. The sunlight that filtered through the windows in the ceiling had a wan and greenish cast and was augmented by radiation from some luminous material impregnating the walls and floor. Even the wide desk and the commodious hassocks glowed with a restful light. Across the former were scattered metal-backed wax tablets, styluses, and a pair of large and oddly formed spectacles. The crammed bookcases were not particularly unusual, but the books were bound in metal and the script on their spines would have been utterly unfamiliar to the most erudite of modern linguists. One of the books, lying open on a hassock, showed leaves of a thin, flexible, rustless metal covered with luminous characters. Between the bookcases were phosphorescent oil paintings, mainly of sea bottoms, in somber greens and browns. Their style, neither wholly realistic nor abstract, would have baffled the historian of art.

A blackboard with large colored crayons hinted equally at the schoolroom and the studio.

In the center of the room, midway to the ceiling, hung a fish with iridescent scales of breathtaking beauty. So invisible was its means of support that⁠—also taking into account the strange paintings and the greenish light⁠—one would have sworn that the object was to create an underwater scene.

The Explorer made his entrance in a theatrical swirl of movement. He embraced the Archeologist with a warmth calculated to startle that crusty old fellow. Then he settled himself on a hassock, looked up and asked a question in a speech and idiom so different from any we know that it must be called another means of communication rather than another language. The import was, “Well, what about it?”

If the Archeologist were taken aback, he concealed it. His expression showed only pleasure at being reunited with a long-absent friend.

“What about what?” he queried.

“About your discovery!”

“What discovery?” The Archeologist’s incomprehension was playful.

The Explorer threw up his arms. “Why, what else but your discovery, here on Earth, of the remains of an intelligent species? It’s the find of the age! Am I going to have to coax you? Out with it!”

“I didn’t make the discovery,” the other said tranquilly. “I only supervised the excavations and directed the correlation of material. You ought to be doing the talking. You’re the one who’s just returned from the stars.”

“Forget that.” The Explorer brushed the question aside. “As soon as our spaceship got within radio range of Earth, they started to send us a continuous newscast covering the period of our absence. One of the items, exasperatingly brief, mentioned your discovery. It captured my imagination. I couldn’t wait to hear the details.” He paused, then confessed, “You get so eager out there in space⁠—a metal-filmed droplet of life lost in immensity. You rediscover your emotions⁠ ⁠…” He changed color, then finished rapidly, “As soon as I could decently get away, I came straight to you. I wanted to hear about it from the best authority⁠—yourself.”

The Archeologist regarded him quizzically. “I’m pleased that you should think of me and my work, and I’m very happy to see you again. But admit it now, isn’t there something a bit odd about your getting so worked up over this thing? I can understand that after your long absence from Earth, any news of Earth would seem especially important. But isn’t there an additional reason?”

The Explorer twisted impatiently. “Oh, I suppose there is. Disappointment, for one thing. We were hoping to get in touch with intelligent life out there. We were specially trained in techniques for establishing mental contact with alien intelligent life forms. Well, we found some planets with life upon them, all right. But it was primitive life, not worth bothering about.”

Again he hesitated embarrassedly. “Out there you get to thinking of the preciousness of intelligence. There’s so little of it, and it’s so lonely. And we so greatly need intercourse with another intelligent species to give depth and balance to our thoughts. I suppose I set too much store by my hopes of establishing a contact.” He paused. “At any rate, when I heard that what we were looking for, you had found here at home⁠—even though dead and done for⁠—I felt that at least it was something. I was suddenly very eager. It is odd, I know, to get so worked up about an extinct species⁠—as if my interest could mean anything to them now⁠—but that’s the way it hit me.”

Several small shadows crossed the windows overhead. They might have been birds, except they moved too slowly.

“I think I understand,” the Archeologist said softly.

“So get on with it and tell me about your discovery!” the Explorer exploded.

“I’ve already told you that it wasn’t my discovery,” the Archeologist reminded him. “A few years after your expedition left, there was begun a detailed resurvey of Earth’s mineral resources. In the course of some deep continental borings, one party discovered a cache⁠—either a very large box or a rather small room⁠—with metallic walls of great strength and toughness. Evidently its makers had intended it for the very purpose of carrying a message down through the ages. It proved to contain artifacts; models of buildings, vehicles, and machines, objects of art, pictures, and books⁠—hundreds of books, along with elaborate pictorial dictionaries for interpreting them. So now we even understand their languages.”

“Languages?” interrupted the Explorer. “That’s queer. Somehow one thinks of an alien species as having just one language.”

“Like our own, this species had several, though there were some words and symbols that were alike in all their languages. These words and symbols seem to have come down unchanged from their most distant prehistory.”

The Explorer burst out, “I am not interested in all that dry stuff! Give me the wet! What were they like? How did they live? What did they create? What did they want?”

The Archeologist gently waved aside the questions. “All in good time. If I am to tell you everything you want to know, I must tell it my own way. Now that you are back on Earth, you will have to reacquire those orderly and composed habits of thought which you have partly lost in the course of your wild interstellar adventurings.”

“Curse you, I think you’re just trying to tantalize me.”

The Archeologist’s expression showed that this was not altogether untrue. He casually fondled an animal that had wriggled up onto his desk, and which looked rather more like an eel than a snake. “Cute little brute, isn’t it?” he remarked. When it became apparent that the Explorer wasn’t to be provoked into another outburst, he continued, “It became my task to interpret the contents of the cache, to reconstruct its makers’ climb from animalism and savagery to civilization, their rather rapid spread across the world’s surface, their first fumbling attempts to escape from the Earth.”

“They had spaceships?”

“It’s barely possible. I rather hope they did, since it would mean the chance of a survival elsewhere, though the negative results of your expedition rather lessen that.” He went on, “The cache was laid down when they were first attempting space flight, just after their discovery of atomic power, in the first flush of their youth. It was probably created in a kind of exuberant fancifulness, with no serious belief that it would ever serve the purpose for which it was intended.” He looked at the Explorer strangely. “If I am not mistaken, we have laid down similar caches.”

After a moment the Archeologist continued, “My reconstruction of their history, subsequent to the laying down of the cache, has been largely hypothetical. I can only guess at the reasons for their decline and fall. Supplementary material has been very slow in coming in, though we are still making extensive excavations at widely separated points. Here are the last reports.” He tossed the Explorer a small metal-leaf pamphlet. It flew with a curiously slow motion.

“That’s what struck me so queer right from the start,” the Explorer observed, putting the pamphlet aside after a glance. “If these creatures were relatively advanced, why haven’t we learned about them before? They must have left so many things⁠—buildings, machines, engineering projects, some of them on a large scale. You’d think we’d be turning up traces everywhere.”

“I have four answers to that,” the Archeologist replied. “The first is the most obvious. Time. Geologic ages of it. The second is more subtle. What if we should have been looking in the wrong place? I mean, what if the creatures occupied a very different portion of the Earth than our own? Third, it’s possible that atomic energy, out of control, finished the race and destroyed its traces. The present distribution of radioactive compounds throughout the Earth’s surface lends some support to this theory.

“Fourth,” he went on, “it’s my belief that when an intelligent species begins to retrogress, it tends to destroy, or, rather, debase all the things it has laboriously created. Large buildings are torn down to make smaller ones. Machines are broken up and worked into primitive tools and weapons. There is a kind of unraveling or erasing. A cultural Second Law of Thermodynamics begins to operate, whereby the intellect and all its works are gradually degraded to the lowest level of meaning and creativity.”

“But why?” The Explorer sounded anguished. “Why should any intelligent species end like that? I grant the possibility of atomic power getting out of hand, though one would have thought they’d have taken the greatest precautions. Still, it could happen. But that fourth answer⁠—it’s morbid.”

“Cultures and civilizations die,” said the Archeologist evenly. “That has happened repeatedly in our own history. Why not species? An individual dies⁠—and is there anything intrinsically more terrible in the death of a species than in the death of an individual?”

He paused. “With respect to the members of this one species, I think that a certain temperamental instability hastened their end. Their appetites and emotions were not sufficiently subordinated to their understanding and to their sense of drama⁠—their enjoyment of the comedy and tragedy of existence. They were impatient and easily incapacitated by frustration. They seem to have been singularly guilty in their pleasures, behaving either like gloomy moralists or gluttons.

“Because of taboos and an overgrown possessiveness,” he continued, “each individual tended to limit his affection to a tiny family; in many cases he focused his love on himself alone. They set great store by personal prestige, by the amassing of wealth and the exercise of power. Their notable capacity for thought and manipulative activity was expended on things rather than persons or feelings. Their technology outstripped their psychology. They skimped fatally when it came to hard thinking about the purpose of life and intellectual activity, and the means for preserving them.”

Again the slow shadows drifted overhead.

“And finally,” the Archeologist said, “they were a strangely haunted species. They seem to have been obsessed by the notion that others, greater than themselves, had prospered before them and then died, leaving them to rebuild a civilization from ruins. It was from those others that they thought they derived the few words and symbols common to all their languages.”

“Gods?” mused the Explorer.

The Archeologist shrugged. “Who knows?”

The Explorer turned away. His excitement had visibly evaporated, leaving behind a cold and miserable residue of feeling. “I am not sure I want to hear much more about them,” he said. “They sound too much like us. Perhaps it was a mistake, my coming here. Pardon me, old friend, but out there in space even our emotions become undisciplined. Everything becomes indescribably poignant. Moods are tempestuous. You shift in an instant from zenith to nadir⁠—and remember, out there you can see both.

“I was very eager to hear about this lost species,” he added in a sad voice. “I thought I would feel a kind of fellowship with them across the eons. Instead, I touch only corpses. It reminds me of when, out in space, there looms up before your prow, faint in the starlight, a dead sun. They were a young race. They thought they were getting somewhere. They promised themselves an eternity of effort. And all the while there was wriggling toward them out of that future for which they yearned⁠ ⁠… oh, it’s so completely futile and unfair.”

“I disagree,” the Archeologist said spiritedly. “Really, your absence from Earth has unsettled you even more than I first surmised. Look at the matter squarely. Death comes to everything in the end. Our past is strewn with our dead. That species died, it’s true. But what they achieved, they achieved. What happiness they had, they had. What they did in their short span is as significant as what they might have done had they lived a billion years. The present is always more important than the future. And no creature can have all the future⁠—it must be shared, left to others.”

“Maybe so,” the Explorer said slowly. “Yes, I guess you’re right. But I still feel a horrible wistfulness about them, and I hug to myself the hope that a few of them escaped and set up a colony on some planet we haven’t yet visited.” There was a long silence. Then the Explorer turned back. “You old devil,” he said in a manner that showed his gayer and more boisterous mood had returned, though diminished, “you still haven’t told me anything definite about them.”

“So I haven’t,” replied the Archeologist with guileful innocence. “Well, they were vertebrates.”

“Oh?”

“Yes. What’s more, they were mammals.”

“Mammals? I was expecting something different.”

“I thought you were.”

The Explorer shifted. “All this matter of evolutionary categories is pretty cut-and-dried. Even a knowledge of how they looked doesn’t mean much. I’d like to approach them in a more intimate way. How did they think of themselves? What did they call themselves? I know the word won’t mean anything to me, but it will give me a feeling⁠—of recognition.”

“I can’t say the word,” the Archeologist told him, “because I haven’t the proper vocal equipment. But I know enough of their script to be able to write it for you as they would have written it. Incidentally, it is one of those words common to all their languages, that they attributed to an earlier race of beings.”

The Archeologist extended one of his eight tentacles toward the blackboard. The suckers at its tip firmly grasped a bit of orange crayon. Another of his tentacles took up the spectacles and adjusted them over his three-inch protruding pupils.

The eel-like glittering pet drifted back into the room and nosed curiously about the crayon as it traced:

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story is a conversation between two old friends in a very strange room. The sunlight comes through the ceiling with a greenish cast, the paintings glow and show the bottoms of seas, and a fish with shimmering scales hangs in the middle of the air. An Explorer, newly back from years among the stars, arrives bursting with questions. While he was away, he heard on a radio newscast that his friend the Archeologist had discovered the remains of an intelligent species that once lived on Earth. He is desperate to hear about it, partly because his own expedition found only primitive life, and he is lonely for minds like his own.

The Archeologist is teasing and slow. He explains that the discovery was a kind of time capsule, a metal chamber buried deep underground, packed with books, models, pictures and dictionaries so that its makers' language could be decoded. The makers laid it down when they were first reaching for space and had just discovered atomic power, probably in a spirit of youthful fancy. Then they vanished. The Archeologist offers four reasons why they left so few traces: time, being in the wrong place, atomic energy out of control, and a gradual slide in which a declining civilization tears down its own works and turns them into cruder tools. He calls this a cultural version of the Second Law of Thermodynamics, the idea that things naturally run down.

Then he gives a portrait of the lost species. They were emotionally unstable, guilty about their pleasures, possessive, fond of prestige and wealth, and more interested in things than in people. Their technology outran their psychology. They were also haunted by the belief that greater beings had lived before them and left them a few mysterious words.

The Explorer reacts with grief. He says the creatures sound too much like us, and he is overcome by how futile it all seems. The Archeologist answers with a philosophy: everything dies, and what the lost species did, they did. Their happiness was as real as it would have been over a billion years. The present, he says, matters more than the future, because no one can have all of the future.

The reveal is quiet. At the end, the Archeologist describes the creatures only as vertebrates and mammals, teases the Explorer for expecting something else, and then, using one of his eight tentacles and spectacles over three-inch eyes, writes on the blackboard the word they used for themselves. We understand that the species being mourned is humanity, and that the speakers are its successors. Every clue was in the room: the underwater light, the fish in the air, the slow shadows. The phrase about us becomes very different when we realize that, for the Explorer, us means the new beings and, for the reader, it still means us.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The great virtue of Later Than You Think is its discipline. The whole story is an act of withholding, and Leiber plays fair. Nearly every detail of the opening description is both strange and meaningful: metal-backed wax tablets, spectacles with strange shapes, a fish apparently floating in air. When we reread, we see the clues were all there, including the narrator's early cheat of saying the study belonged to an era vastly distant from today. It is a puzzle that is solved by reading again, and that is the mark of a well built twist.

The dialogue is also doing double duty. The Archeologist's analysis of the lost species is a sharp critique of modern human culture, delivered coolly by someone who does not know he is describing his readers. The lines about technology outstripping psychology, affection narrowed to a tiny family or to the self alone, and love spent on things instead of people are the kind of social criticism that might have felt preachy in a direct address. Putting them in the mouth of an alien scholar lets Leiber say them with a clinical calm that stings more.

The emotional structure works as well. There is a real shift from the Explorer's hunger for a companion species, to disappointment, to grief, to something close to consolation. The Archeologist's argument that a short life is not a lesser one is one of the most humane passages in Leiber's fiction. It gives the story a heart, so that it is not just a clever trick.

But there are weaknesses. The central device, the alien narrator who turns out to be a descendant of rivals or inheritors, is one of the most familiar twists in science fiction, and modern readers may see it coming. Part of the reason is that the story itself is so eager to be fair that its hints sit on the surface. The Explorer's remark that the creatures sound too much like us is nearly a wink. Another problem is that the dialogue is expository by necessity, and it can feel stagey, with the Explorer shouting, bursting out and exploding in ways that exist mainly to give the Archeologist something to answer.

There is also an oddity in the logic of the world. The story wants us to believe that an entire species could be erased with so little trace that its remains require a deliberately buried cache, yet that the Archeologist can also read their languages and describe their psychology in detail. That is a convenience. The ending, too, is more clever than devastating. Its moral weight depends on the reader supplying the final revelation, and the story stops just short of dramatizing what that means.

Still, these are small blemishes in a very short, well made story. It earns its twist, and it has something to say after the twist.

III. The Impact It Had

The impact of Later Than You Think is best described as that of a quietly influential example. It is not one of Leiber's most celebrated works, and we do not have a detailed record of how it was received in 1950, but it belongs to a distinctive and enduring family of stories, and it has been reprinted in Leiber collections and in anthologies of science fiction about the end of the world.

For Leiber himself, the story shows his ability to turn a large abstract idea, the mortality of civilizations, into a dramatic scene with a surprise. He was by this time already known for fantasy and horror, and this piece displays his skill with a different tool: the philosophical conversation. It is also a reminder of how often his work is concerned with time, and with the way the past and the future press on the present.

Its larger influence is on the mode of the reversal of perspective. Stories that end by revealing that the observers are not who we thought, or that the ruins being studied are our own, became a staple of postwar science fiction and then of popular culture. The most famous example is the final scene of the film Planet of the Apes, in which a ruined statue reveals that the planet is Earth. That image carries the same shock that Leiber builds in a few sentences of dialogue. Many television anthology shows, including The Twilight Zone, built episodes around this same final turn, and it remains a common structure in short fiction.

The story also fits into the early atomic-age reckoning with human extinction. Before 1945, the idea that humanity might destroy itself was a matter for prophets and poets. Afterward, it was a technical possibility, and writers began to explore it in earnest. Leiber's story is one of the more philosophical entries in that effort. It does not describe a war or its victims. It simply imagines a visitor looking at the evidence, and it asks what meaning human life can have if it is going to be remembered, if at all, only as a puzzle in a museum.

In classrooms, the story is a convenient way to teach point of view, the ethics of the future, and the idea that fiction can estrange us from ourselves. Because the twist depends on a narrator's careful description, it also makes a good example for discussing how authors plant clues, and how readers can feel both surprise and recognition at the same moment. In the decades since, similar ideas have appeared in discussions about long-term nuclear waste warnings, time capsules and the question of how a civilization might speak to those who come after it.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers admire most? Many point first to the atmosphere. The opening paragraph is a small masterpiece of strange but concrete detail. The greenish light, the luminous walls, the books bound in metal, the paintings of sea bottoms and the iridescent fish hung in the air make a convincing world in a hundred words. Even before the twist, readers feel they are being given a rare and wonderful tour.

They also love the voice of the Archeologist. He is gently ironic, patient and a little vain, a teacher who enjoys making his friend wait. His remark about a cultural Second Law of Thermodynamics is memorable, and so is his calm question about whether the death of a species is any more terrible than the death of an individual. For many readers, he is the heart of the story: a wise creature who has made peace with endings.

Another source of love is the story's emotional reversal. The Explorer begins as an eager child, becomes a mourner, and ends with something like acceptance, and the whole arc takes place in a conversation. Readers appreciate that the twist does not cancel this feeling. Instead, it turns the consolation back on us. If the Archeologist is right that what a species achieves, it achieves, and what happiness it had, it had, then human life is not made meaningless by being brief. That idea gives many readers comfort in a story that could easily have been bleak.

People also admire how the story captures a particular mid-century mood. In the aftermath of the war, there was a new awareness that civilizations are fragile. The line about the species being young and thinking they were getting somewhere, promising themselves an eternity of effort, is the kind of sentence that stays in the mind. It describes the ambition of the atomic age and the shadow behind it with great economy.

Finally, readers like the humor. For all its seriousness, the story has a playful side. The Archeologist toys with his friend, strokes a pet that looks like an eel, and delivers his shock with relish. The Explorer's outburst, Curse you, I think you're just trying to tantalize me, is the line of a real friend. These touches make the characters human, or at least humanlike, and they let the reader laugh before being asked to reflect.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

There is not a large body of documented criticism of this particular story, so it is best to speak in general terms about the reservations readers have expressed.

The first and most common is that the twist is predictable. Modern readers who have grown up with a century of science fiction, and with films that end by showing that the planet was Earth all along, can spot the reversal early. Some feel that the story gives itself away with the Explorer's remark that the lost species sounds too much like us, and with the Archeologist's sly insistence on teasing him about what kind of creatures they were. For them, the story is less a surprise than a pleasant confirmation.

A second criticism is that the story tells rather than shows. Almost all of the content is exposition, in the form of two characters talking. There is no action, no conflict apart from the playful friction between the speakers, and little physical drama. Readers who want plot may find it more of a sketch than a story. The characters are also named only by their roles, the Explorer and the Archeologist, and some find them thin as people.

A third is that the portrait of humanity is harsh and simplified. The Archeologist's description of the lost species as impulsive, possessive, gloomy or gluttonous is a sweeping judgment, and it reads sometimes like a lecture. Some readers feel that this passage is the story's weakest, because it summarizes human failings in a list rather than dramatizing them. Others find it too pessimistic, since it implies that decline and self-destruction are the natural fate of intelligent life.

A fourth objection concerns the philosophical consolation. The Archeologist's claim that the present is always more important than the future and that a short life is as significant as a long one is moving, but some readers regard it as too easy. It lets the reader off the hook. If every extinction is simply the natural end of a life, then there is no reason to try to prevent one. A reader with a more activist temper might see this as a quietly fatalistic message, especially in the context of nuclear danger. Leiber seems to anticipate this, since the Explorer himself is not fully persuaded, but the story leaves the question open.

Finally, some find the science thin. The idea of a cultural Second Law of Thermodynamics is a metaphor rather than a theory, and the account of how a species might leave no trace is hand-waving. These are, however, standard features of the form, and most readers forgive them.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Later Than You Think belongs to a long tradition of stories about the fall of civilizations, and to a more specific modern tradition of stories that look back at humanity from the far future. Its oldest ancestor is the idea captured in Percy Bysshe Shelley's poem Ozymandias, from 1818, in which a traveler finds the broken statue of a mighty king in the desert and reads the boast carved on its base: look on my works, ye mighty, and despair. The point is that the proudest achievements become ruins. Leiber's story is Ozymandias with a twist of its own, in which the traveler is not human and the ruins are everything we have built.

Closer in time, it descends from H. G. Wells, whose The Time Machine, from 1895, takes its hero to a far future in which humanity has degenerated, and then to a dying Earth under a swollen red sun. Wells gave science fiction its taste for deep time and for the cooling of the universe, and Leiber inherits both. Olaf Stapledon's Last and First Men, from 1930, extends the idea across billions of years and many successor species. Leiber's story is a miniature of that vast vision, compressed into a conversation.

Its descendants are numerous. Walter M. Miller's A Canticle for Leibowitz, from 1959, follows monks who preserve the fragments of a destroyed civilization through a new dark age and a second atomic war. Planet of the Apes, in its film version of 1968, ends with the ruined Statue of Liberty. Isaac Asimov's short story The Last Question follows humanity and its descendants to the end of the universe. Later stories about archaeologists of the future, who piece together our world from fragments, owe something to Leiber's premise, and so do real-world discussions about how to warn distant generations about buried nuclear waste, a problem in which people literally try to write a message that will outlast their language.

Within Leiber's career, it sits alongside his other speculative pieces about time and consequence, and it shows a gentler, more reflective side than his sword-and-sorcery work. And in the context of the nuclear age, it gives a voice to a fear that was new in 1950: that humanity's story could end, and that someone, or something, might read it.

Why read it now? Because the question it asks is still ours. We live with powerful technologies, fragile institutions and a growing sense of deep time, and we are still deciding what our lives mean against that background. Leiber's story answers with calm and grace, and with a twist that makes us see our own age as someone else's archaeology. It is short, quiet and unexpectedly consoling, and it rewards a second reading, when you can catch the clues that were there all along.
