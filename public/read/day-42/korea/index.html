The Gyeongui Line Forest Park: How a Severed 1906 Trunk Railway Became Seoul's 6.3-Kilometer Living Room

Korea — From Imperial Japan's wartime rail corridor to the subterranean train tunnel that unlocked 'Yeontral Park' and Hongdae's Book Street (1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion) • 9 min read

“Where iron wheels once carried soldiers toward the northern border, children now read picture books on wooden sleepers and cherry blossoms drift into the stream.” — Mapo-gu Gyeongui Line Park Dedication (2016)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stretching 6.3 kilometers across western Seoul from Hyochang Park in Yongsan through Gongdeok, Daeheung, Sogang, and Hongdae to Yeonnam-dong and Gajwa in Mapo-gu, the Gyeongui Line Forest Park (popularly nicknamed 'Yeontral Park' along its Yeonnam-dong stretch) is South Korea's premier rail-to-greenway transformation. Originally laid between 1904 and 1906 during the Russo-Japanese War to connect Seoul (Gyeongseong) 499 kilometers north to Sinuiju on the Yalu River, and severed at the DMZ in 1950, the surface tracks sliced western Seoul neighborhoods in two for a century until engineers buried the double-track commuter rail twenty meters underground in 2005–2012 and handed the sunlit surface corridor back to citizens.

Step out of Exit 3 at Hongik University Station in Mapo-gu, Seoul, on a warm Friday evening in May. Instead of a fenced, gravel-strewn railway embankment clanging with freight trains and diesel fumes, a 1.3-kilometer-long green meadow shaded by metasequoias, weeping willows, and cherry trees stretches straight ahead through Yeonnam-dong. Hundreds of students, families, and musicians sit on picnic blankets beside a babbling granite stream fed by reclaimed subway groundwater while independent bakeries, vinyl bars, and small bookshops glow inside converted two-story brick houses on both sides of the lawn. Locals call this corridor Yeontral Park (a portmanteau of Yeonnam-dong and New York’s Central Park)—the most vibrant sector of the 6.3-kilometer Gyeongui Line Forest Park.

To understand why walking along these rusted steel rails embedded in the grass feels so moving to older Koreans, you have to trace the century-long history of the Gyeongui Line. Notice the two syllables of Gyeong-Ui: Gyeong stood for the capital Gyeongseong (Seoul), and Ui stood for Sinuiju, the border city 499.3 kilometers northwest at the mouth of the Amnok (Yalu) River facing Manchuria! Originally, in the late 1890s, King Gojong’s Korean Empire (Daehan Jeguk) established the Northwest Railway Bureau (Seobuk Cheoldoguk) hoping to build a sovereign Korean railway linking Seoul, Kaesong, Pyongyang, and Uiju.

When the Russo-Japanese War broke out in February 1904, however, the Imperial Japanese Army seized the right-of-way by military decree to rush troops and artillery north toward the Manchurian front. Mobilizing tens of thousands of conscripted Korean laborers at breakneck speed, Japanese military engineers completed the entire 499-kilometer Gyeongui Line between Yongsan and Sinuiju by April 1906. For the next forty years, the Gyeongui Line was the transcontinental artery of Northeast Asia. An express passenger train departing Seoul Station or Yongsan steamed through Mapo, Kaesong, and Pyongyang, crossed the Yalu River steel bridge into Manchuria, connected to the Chinese Eastern and Trans-Siberian Railways, and carried travelers—including Korean independence fighters heading to Shanghai, poet Yun Dong-ju traveling home to North Jiandao, and Sohn Kee-jung traveling to Berlin for the 1936 Olympic marathon!—all the way to Harbin, Moscow, Berlin, and Paris!

Then came the division of the peninsula and the Korean War (1950–1953). With the Military Demarcation Line of the DMZ slicing directly across the tracks just north of Munsan and Dorasan, the international trunk highway to Pyongyang and Europe became a dead-end stub line! For the next half-century, from the 1950s through the early 2000s, the surface tracks of the Gyeongui Line running diagonally through Yongsan-gu and Mapo-gu functioned only as a slow, infrequent single-track diesel commuter and coal-freight spur (delivering anthracite briquette coal to the Susaek and Dangin-ri depots). Even worse, the fenced surface railway track acted as a 6.3-kilometer physical scar across western Seoul: dividing neighborhoods into 'front of the tracks' and 'back of the tracks,' causing frequent level-crossing accidents, and lining the embankment with shanties and scrap yards.

In 2005, when the Korea Rail Network Authority launched the modernization and electrification of the Gyeongui–Jungang Line (and the AREX Airport Railroad linking Incheon International Airport to Seoul Station), urban planners and Mapo citizens made a transformative decision: rather than expanding the surface railway corridor, bury the entire 6.3-kilometer urban rail corridor twenty to thirty meters underground in double-deck tunnels, and donate the 102,008-square-meter surface strip to the Seoul Metropolitan Government to build a continuous linear public park! Constructed in three phases between 2011 and May 2016, landscape architects refused to impose a uniform cookie-cutter park across all 6.3 kilometers.

Sector 1: Yeonnam-dong ('Yeontral Park,' 1.3 km from Hongik Univ. Station to Gajwa): Formerly a quiet residential pocket of 1970s two-story Western-style brick houses (yangok) and long-time Chinese-Korean (Hwagyo) dumpling restaurants around Donggyo-dong, Yeonnam-dong was given a wide central lawn flanked by a shallow ecological stream fed by clean groundwater pumped up from the underground Airport Railroad station below! Old cast-iron railway tracks and wooden crossties (chimmok) were left embedded flush in the stone walkways so pedestrians can walk the exact gauge of the 1906 trains. Sector 2: Waugyo & Gyeongui Line Book Street (0.6 km behind Hongdae): Mapo-gu is the historic capital of South Korea’s publishing industry—more than 4,400 independent publishers, book designers, and literary editors are headquartered in the alleys of Seogyo-dong, Hapjeong, and Mangwon! To honor that literary heritage, the park sector at Hongik University Station Exit 6 was transformed into Gyeongui Line Book Street: nine train-car-shaped pavilion booths housing independent thematic bookstores (Literature, Art, Travel, Children’s Books), author reading stages, and a monument to the historic Waugyo railway bridge.

Sector 3: Sinchon, Sogang & Daeheung-dong (1.4 km): Winding past the campuses of Sogang and Yonsei Universities, this shaded forest walk features groves of tall metasequoias, community reading benches, and historic railway signals where university students and elderly residents share the path. Sector 4: Yeomni-dong & Gongdeok to Hyochang Park (1.8 km): Here, where salt merchants (yeomni) once unloaded Han River boats and retired railway engineers lived beside the tracks, the park weaves through cherry-blossom tunnels and open plazas that host weekend farmers’ markets. The urban economic and ecological impact of the 2016 linear park stunned planners. Like Manhattan’s High Line or Paris’s Coulée verte, converting a noisy, dividing industrial barrier into a continuous car-free pedestrian spine stitched severed neighborhoods back together: pedestrian foot traffic in Yeonnam-dong and Daeheung-dong tripled, microclimate summer surface temperatures along the tree-lined corridor dropped by 2.5°C, and small dead-end residential alleys (golmok) blossomed into galleries and studios.

Even the dream of the original transcontinental railway remains alive beneath your feet: twenty meters below the grass where picnickers sip coffee in Yeonnam-dong, electrified Gyeongui–Jungang commuter trains and airport express trains glide silently every few minutes toward Munsan and Imjingak at the edge of the DMZ. Walk from the train-car bookshops of Gyeongui Line Book Street across the street into the willow-shaded stream of Yeontral Park at twilight. Step onto a weathered 1906 oak railroad tie set into the garden path, look north toward Sinuiju and the stars, and feel how a city turned a century-old scar of division into its most welcoming living room.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Linear Infrastructure Conversion: Burying Double-Deck Rail to Reclaim 102,000 m²

Civil engineers and urban designers point to the Gyeongui Line Forest Park as a model for high-density Asian cities lacking large central land parcels for new parks. By stacking the electrified Gyeongui–Jungang commuter line and the AREX Incheon Airport Express in deep underground box tunnels between Yongsan and Gajwa (2005–2012), the Korea Rail Network Authority freed a 6.3-kilometer-long, 15-to-40-meter-wide continuous surface right-of-way (102,008 m²) without demolishing a single home.

2. Hydrological Upcycling & Mapo's 4,400-Publisher Literary Ecosystem

Two local innovations anchor the park's design. First, rather than discharging the thousands of tons of natural groundwater that seep daily into the deep Hongik University subway and airport-rail tunnels into the storm sewer, engineers daylighted the water along a 600-meter granite channel through Yeonnam-dong to cool the urban heat island and attract wetland birds. Second, Gyeongui Line Book Street connects visitors directly to Mapo-gu's cluster of over 4,400 independent publishing houses.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three terms in mind: first, Gyeongui-seon, the 1906 trunk railway named after Gyeongseong (Seoul) and Sinuiju on the northern Yalu border; second, Yeontral Park, the beloved nickname for the Yeonnam-dong lawn and stream sector of the Gyeongui Line Forest Park; and third, Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori, the train-car bookshop village at Hongdae celebrating Korean print culture.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Start at Hongik University Station Exit 6 to browse the independent book pavilions of Gyeongui Line Book Street, then cross to Exit 3 and walk northwest for 1.3 kilometers along the grassy stream of Yeonnam-dong's Yeontral Park, or walk southeast along the shaded metasequoia trail through Sogang and Daeheung all the way to Gongdeok and Hyochang Park.
