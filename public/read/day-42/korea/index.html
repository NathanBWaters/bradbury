The Gyeongui Line Forest Park: How a Severed 1906 Trunk Railway Became Seoul's 6.3-Kilometer Living Room

Korea — From Imperial Japan's wartime rail corridor to the subterranean train tunnel that unlocked 'Yeontral Park' and Hongdae's Book Street (1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion) • 20 min read

“Where iron wheels once carried soldiers toward the northern border, children now read picture books on wooden sleepers and cherry blossoms drift into the stream.” — Mapo-gu Gyeongui Line Park Dedication (2016)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stretching 6.3 kilometers across western Seoul from Hyochang Park in Yongsan through Gongdeok, Daeheung, Sogang, and Hongdae to Yeonnam-dong and Gajwa in Mapo-gu, the Gyeongui Line Forest Park (popularly nicknamed 'Yeontral Park' along its Yeonnam-dong stretch) is South Korea's premier rail-to-greenway transformation. Originally laid between 1904 and 1906 during the Russo-Japanese War to connect Seoul (Gyeongseong) 499 kilometers north to Sinuiju on the Yalu River, and severed at the DMZ in 1950, the surface tracks sliced western Seoul neighborhoods in two for a century until engineers buried the double-track commuter rail twenty meters underground in 2005–2012 and handed the sunlit surface corridor back to citizens. Anchored at Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

Step out of Exit 3 at **Hongik University Station** in Mapo-gu, Seoul, on a warm Friday evening in May. Instead of a fenced, gravel-strewn railway embankment clanging with freight trains and diesel fumes, a 1.3-kilometer-long green meadow shaded by metasequoias, weeping willows, and cherry trees stretches straight ahead through **Yeonnam-dong** (연남동). Hundreds of students, families, and musicians sit on picnic blankets beside a babbling granite stream fed by reclaimed subway groundwater while independent bakeries, vinyl bars, and small bookshops glow inside converted two-story brick houses on both sides of the lawn. Locals call this corridor **Yeontral Park** (연트럴파크, a portmanteau of *Yeonnam-dong* and New York’s *Central Park*)—the most vibrant sector of the **6.3-kilometer Gyeongui Line Forest Park** (경의선숲길). When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Gyeongui Line Forest Park (Yeonnam-dong & Book Street), Mapo-gu & Yongsan-gu, Seoul (37.5605° N, 126.9242° E) to study Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu shaped every decision made during the 1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion. Nothing in this setting is accidental: the orientation of the ground, the seasonal angle of sunlight, and the acoustic stillness surrounding Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) establish an immediate sensory bridge between twenty-first-century observers and the historical artisans who worked here.

To understand why walking along these rusted steel rails embedded in the grass feels so moving to older Koreans, you have to trace the century-long history of the **Gyeongui Line** (경의선, 京義線). In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the 1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion, the rise of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Notice the two syllables of **Gyeong-Ui**: **Gyeong** (경, 京) stood for the capital **Gyeongseong (Seoul)**, and **Ui** (의, 義) stood for **Sinuiju** (신의주), the border city 499.3 kilometers northwest at the mouth of the Amnok (Yalu) River facing Manchuria! Originally, in the late 1890s, King Gojong’s Korean Empire (*Daehan Jeguk*) established the *Northwest Railway Bureau* (*Seobuk Cheoldoguk*) hoping to build a sovereign Korean railway linking Seoul, Kaesong, Pyongyang, and Uiju. Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

When the **Russo-Japanese War** broke out in February **1904**, however, the Imperial Japanese Army seized the right-of-way by military decree to rush troops and artillery north toward the Manchurian front. Mobilizing tens of thousands of conscripted Korean laborers at breakneck speed, Japanese military engineers completed the entire 499-kilometer **Gyeongui Line** between Yongsan and Sinuiju by **April 1906**. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) aligned Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

For the next forty years, the Gyeongui Line was the transcontinental artery of Northeast Asia. An express passenger train departing Seoul Station or Yongsan steamed through Mapo, Kaesong, and Pyongyang, crossed the Yalu River steel bridge into Manchuria, connected to the Chinese Eastern and **Trans-Siberian Railways**, and carried travelers—including Korean independence fighters heading to Shanghai, poet **Yun Dong-ju** traveling home to North Jiandao, and **Sohn Kee-jung** traveling to Berlin for the 1936 Olympic marathon!—all the way to Harbin, Moscow, Berlin, and Paris! Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Every raw element gathered near Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu had to be tested for density, moisture response, and structural resilience before it could be incorporated into Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park), ensuring that the interplay between Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) would withstand centuries of monsoon humidity and freezing winter temperatures.

Then came the division of the peninsula and the **Korean War (1950–1953)**. With the Military Demarcation Line of the DMZ slicing directly across the tracks just north of Munsan and Dorasan, the international trunk highway to Pyongyang and Europe became a **dead-end stub line**! Closer inspection of the primary mechanism reveals a level of quantitative precision that still astonishes contemporary engineers. By calibrating the tolerances of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) and balancing the physical forces acting across Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor), the builders eliminated points of mechanical failure while maximizing the functional efficiency of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) under real-world operating conditions.

For the next half-century, from the 1950s through the early 2000s, the surface tracks of the Gyeongui Line running diagonally through Yongsan-gu and Mapo-gu functioned only as a slow, infrequent single-track diesel commuter and coal-freight spur (delivering anthracite briquette coal to the Susaek and Dangin-ri depots). Even worse, the fenced surface railway track acted as a **6.3-kilometer physical scar** across western Seoul: dividing neighborhoods into 'front of the tracks' and 'back of the tracks,' causing frequent level-crossing accidents, and lining the embankment with shanties and scrap yards. Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. When temperature, airflow, or human movement shifted around Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor), the subtle geometry of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) absorbed the stress smoothly, turning potential environmental hazards into sources of long-term stability.

In **2005**, when the Korea Rail Network Authority launched the modernization and electrification of the **Gyeongui–Jungang Line** (and the **AREX Airport Railroad** linking Incheon International Airport to Seoul Station), urban planners and Mapo citizens made a transformative decision: rather than expanding the surface railway corridor, **bury the entire 6.3-kilometer urban rail corridor twenty to thirty meters underground** in double-deck tunnels, and donate the 102,008-square-meter surface strip to the Seoul Metropolitan Government to build a continuous **linear public park**! That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu. Whenever an early prototype or seasonal cycle threatened to derail Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park), the masters refined their approach to Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and recalibrated Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street), passing hard-won empirical lessons down from mentor to apprentice.

Constructed in three phases between **2011 and May 2016**, landscape architects refused to impose a uniform cookie-cutter park across all 6.3 kilometers. Because the railway cuts through four neighborhoods with completely different historical personalities, each sector of the **Gyeongui Line Forest Park** was designed as a distinct cultural chapter: Primary documentary evidence from the 1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion confirms the extraordinary care devoted to recording these breakthroughs. Scholars examining the surviving texts and inscriptions associated with Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) can trace the exact terminology of Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street), verifying that what once seemed like legend was grounded in rigorous institutional documentation.

**Sector 1: Yeonnam-dong ('Yeontral Park,' 1.3 km from Hongik Univ. Station to Gajwa)**: Formerly a quiet residential pocket of 1970s two-story Western-style brick houses (*yangok*) and long-time Chinese-Korean (*Hwagyo*) dumpling restaurants around Donggyo-dong, Yeonnam-dong was given a wide central lawn flanked by a shallow ecological stream fed by clean groundwater pumped up from the underground Airport Railroad station below! Old cast-iron railway tracks and wooden crossties (*침목*, *chimmok*) were left embedded flush in the stone walkways so pedestrians can walk the exact gauge of the 1906 trains. To experience Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. From the tactile grain of Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) beneath the fingers to the rhythmic cadence of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) echoing across Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu, every detail reinforced a shared sense of civic and spiritual belonging.

**Sector 2: Waugyo & Gyeongui Line Book Street (경의선 책거리, 0.6 km behind Hongdae)**: Mapo-gu is the historic capital of South Korea’s publishing industry—more than **4,400 independent publishers, book designers, and literary editors** are headquartered in the alleys of Seogyo-dong, Hapjeong, and Mangwon! To honor that literary heritage, the park sector at Hongik University Station Exit 6 was transformed into **Gyeongui Line Book Street**: nine train-car-shaped pavilion booths housing independent thematic bookstores (Literature, Art, Travel, Children’s Books), author reading stages, and a monument to the historic *Waugyo* railway bridge. Yet history repeatedly tested the survival of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) through the crucible of war, regime change, and rapid modernization. When external upheavals swept across Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu, the fragile equilibrium between Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) faced existential threats that could easily have erased centuries of accumulated knowledge in a single generation.

**Sector 3: Sinchon, Sogang & Daeheung-dong (1.4 km)**: Winding past the campuses of Sogang and Yonsei Universities, this shaded forest walk features groves of tall metasequoias, community reading benches, and historic railway signals where university students and elderly residents share the path. That Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion. Ordinary guardians, local families, and dedicated researchers risked their livelihoods to shelter Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and preserve the oral and written manuals of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) until a peaceful era could appreciate their full value once more.

**Sector 4: Yeomni-dong & Gongdeok to Hyochang Park (1.8 km)**: Here, where salt merchants (*yeomni*) once unloaded Han River boats and retired railway engineers lived beside the tracks, the park weaves through cherry-blossom tunnels and open plazas that host weekend farmers’ markets. When modern laboratory scientists and conservation specialists finally examined Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) using non-invasive imaging and chemical analysis, the empirical data validated centuries of traditional wisdom. Measurements of Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and structural modeling of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) revealed optimization strategies that rival twenty-first-century materials science.

The urban economic and ecological impact of the 2016 linear park stunned planners. Like Manhattan’s High Line or Paris’s *Coulée verte*, converting a noisy, dividing industrial barrier into a continuous car-free pedestrian spine stitched severed neighborhoods back together: pedestrian foot traffic in Yeonnam-dong and Daeheung-dong tripled, microclimate summer surface temperatures along the tree-lined corridor dropped by 2.5°C, and small dead-end residential alleys (*golmok*) blossomed into galleries and studios. Today at Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu, a new generation of practitioners and cultural stewards continues to breathe fresh vitality into Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park). By honoring the authentic craft protocols of Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) while introducing contemporary audiences to the philosophy of Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street), they ensure that heritage remains a living workshop rather than a frozen museum relic.

Even the dream of the original transcontinental railway remains alive beneath your feet: twenty meters below the grass where picnickers sip coffee in Yeonnam-dong, electrified Gyeongui–Jungang commuter trains and airport express trains glide silently every few minutes toward Munsan and Imjingak at the edge of the DMZ. Placed in comparative global perspective, the story of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) challenges Eurocentric narratives of scientific and artistic history. Long before parallel developments appeared elsewhere, Korean innovators at Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu had already synthesized Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) and Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) into a coherent system tailored to human dignity and ecological balance.

1. Linear Infrastructure Conversion: Burying Double-Deck Rail to Reclaim 102,000 m²

Civil engineers and urban designers point to the Gyeongui Line Forest Park as a model for high-density Asian cities lacking large central land parcels for new parks. By stacking the electrified Gyeongui–Jungang commuter line and the AREX Incheon Airport Express in deep underground box tunnels between Yongsan and Gajwa (2005–2012), the Korea Rail Network Authority freed a 6.3-kilometer-long, 15-to-40-meter-wide continuous surface right-of-way (102,008 m²) without demolishing a single home. Studying the structural interplay between Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) and Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor) within the context of the 1904–1906 Railway Era to 2016 Linear Park Completion demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint. Taken together, these insights from Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu deepen our appreciation for the enduring legacy of Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) and Yeontral Park (연트럴파크 Yeonnam-dong meadow and stream corridor).

2. Hydrological Upcycling & Mapo's 4,400-Publisher Literary Ecosystem

Two local innovations anchor the park's design. First, rather than discharging the thousands of tons of natural groundwater that seep daily into the deep Hongik University subway and airport-rail tunnels into the storm sewer, engineers daylighted the water along a 600-meter granite channel through Yeonnam-dong to cool the urban heat island and attract wetland birds. Second, Gyeongui Line Book Street connects visitors directly to Mapo-gu's cluster of over 4,400 independent publishing houses. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae in Mapo-gu, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Gyeongui-seon Supgil (경의선숲길 6.3 km linear rail-to-trail park) to Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리 Hongdae publishing Book Street) across the centuries.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three terms in mind: first, Gyeongui-seon (경의선, 京義線), the 1906 trunk railway named after Gyeongseong (Seoul) and Sinuiju on the northern Yalu border; second, Yeontral Park (연트럴파크), the beloved nickname for the Yeonnam-dong lawn and stream sector of the Gyeongui Line Forest Park; and third, Gyeongui-seon Chaek-geori (경의선 책거리), the train-car bookshop village at Hongdae celebrating Korean print culture.
