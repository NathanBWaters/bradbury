San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • Victorian Architecture, Redwood Forestry & Colorist Movement

Alamo Square's 1892 'Painted Ladies' on Steiner Street, Redwood Balloon Framing, and Butch Kardum

San Francisco — How developer Matthew Kavanaugh mass-produced 'Postcard Row' out of old-growth coast redwood—and how a 1963 psychedelic artist broke fifty years of battleship-gray paint (Gilded Age Queen Anne Era (1892–1896) & 1960s Colorist Revival) • 8 min read

“Old-growth coast redwood had almost no resin, never warped in the ocean fog, and carved like butter under a steam jigsaw; without the Mendocino forest, Victorian San Francisco could never have existed.” — Randolph Delehanty (1989)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stand on the grassy western crest of Alamo Square Park looking east toward the downtown skyscrapers across the seven shoulder-to-shoulder Queen Anne Victorian houses at 710–722 Steiner Street, and you are gazing at the most photographed streetscape in the American West: 'Postcard Row,' or 'The Seven Sisters.' Built on speculation between 1892 and 1896 by Irish American carpenter-developer Matthew Kavanaugh (who lived at 722 Steiner) out of rot-resistant old-growth coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) milled in Mendocino and Humboldt counties, these survivors of the 1906 fire were painted drab wartime battleship gray for decades—until artist Butch Kardum ignited the 1963 San Francisco Colorist Movement that transformed them into radiant polychrome 'Painted Ladies.'

Between 1849 and 1915, carpenters and speculative homebuilders erected approximately 48,000 Victorian and Edwardian houses across the hills of San Francisco—constituting the largest, densest concentration of ornate wooden row housing ever built on earth. Why was nineteenth-century San Francisco built almost entirely of wood when contemporaneous New York, Boston, London, and Paris built their row houses out of brick and brownstone? Three local facts dictated the choice: unreinforced brick cracked in California earthquakes, local brick clay was scarce, and just north of the Golden Gate along the Mendocino and Humboldt coasts stood the greatest forest of old-growth coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) and Douglas fir on the planet.

Transported down the rough Pacific coast aboard nimble two-masted 'doghole schooners' that loaded timber by wire cable off wave-lashed Mendocino cliffs, old-growth coast redwood possessed three miraculous structural properties for San Francisco builders: its tight straight grain resisted shrinking or warping in wet summer fog, its high natural tannin content repelled termites and dry rot without chemical treatment, and its low pitch-resin content made it far slower to ignite than pine. Equally important, inside the steam-powered planing mills South of Market along Bryant and Brannan streets, coast redwood was soft enough to be turned on lathes and cut by steam jigsaws into an exuberant encyclopedia of brackets, spindles, sunburst pediments, fish-scale shingles, and Corinthian capitals that builders bought right out of mail-order pattern catalogs.

To erect narrow houses side-by-side on standard San Francisco lots measuring just 25 feet wide by 100 or 120 feet deep, nineteenth-century carpenters used balloon framing: running continuous two-by-four redwood studs two or three stories straight up from the sill plate to the roof rafters, sheathed in diagonal one-inch redwood boards that acted as a flexible structural box capable of swaying intact during earthquakes. Between 1892 and 1896, at the height of the exuberant Queen Anne Victorian style, an enterprising Irish American carpenter and speculative developer named Matthew Kavanaugh purchased a sloping strip of land along the east side of Steiner Street between Hayes and Grove streets directly facing the hilltop oasis of Alamo Square Park.

Working systematically down the hillside from south to north so that each house's gabled turret, cutaway bay window, and recessed porch stepped rhythmically down the grade at a uniform slope, Kavanaugh built the seven sister houses at 710, 712, 714, 716, 718, 720, and 722 Steiner Street—moving his own family into the grand corner mansion at 722 Steiner Street in 1892 while selling the neighboring six homes for roughly $3,500 apiece. Look closely at the facades of 710–722 Steiner Street and you see the genius of Gilded Age speculative carpentry: behind the varied surface ornament, all seven houses share virtually identical floor plans and structural bays, yet Kavanaugh varied the spindlework friezes, porch railings, and attic pediments on each facade so every middle-class buyer felt they owned a unique custom palace.

Eleven years after Kavanaugh hammered the last nail at 710 Steiner Street, the earthquake and firestorm of April 18–20, 1906 burned 28,000 buildings across eastern San Francisco—consuming the great Victorian mansions of Nob Hill and South of Market—before firefighters and the U.S. Army halted the flames at the wide avenue of Van Ness, sparing the Western Addition and Alamo Square. During the two World Wars and the Great Depression, however, Victorian ornament fell deeply out of fashion: modernist critics derided Queen Anne houses as 'gingerbread monstrosities,' landlords stripped off rotting brackets or covered facades in asbestos siding and stucco, and thousands of Victorians were painted in surplus Navy 'battleship gray' or whitewash—while nearby in the Fillmore, Redevelopment Agency bulldozers leveled 2,500 Victorians in the 1950s and 1960s.

Then, in the summer of 1963, a bold aesthetic rebellion began a few blocks from Alamo Square: San Francisco psychedelic artist Butch Kardum stood in front of his dull gray Italianate Victorian house, mixed cans of intense blues, greens, and golds, and painted the exterior moldings, dentils, and recessed panels in dazzling multi-colored contrasts. Though traditional neighbors initially scoffed, fellow artists and designers including Tony Canaletich, Bob Buckter, and Jazon Wonders soon joined Kardum, discovering that using five to seven contrasting colors—highlighting the structural bones, window sashes, brackets, and gold-leaf accents—brought the three-dimensional shadow play of redwood millwork vividly back to life against San Francisco's gray summer fog.

As the Haight-Ashbury and Alamo Square preservation renaissance gathered momentum in the 1970s, the homeowners along 710–722 Steiner Street stripped off decades of grime and repainted Kavanaugh's seven Queen Anne houses in harmonious pastel and jewel tones—creating a foreground symphony of color framed against the rising glass-and-steel towers of the downtown Financial District. In 1978, San Francisco writers Elizabeth Pomada and Michael Larsen published their landmark photo book Painted Ladies: San Francisco's Resplendent Victorians (with photographs by Douglas Keister), coining the phrase that made 710–722 Steiner Street globally famous and sparking a national historic-preservation movement from Cape May to New Orleans.

Immortalized in hundreds of films, postcards, and television opening credits—most famously the picnic scene of Full House—Alamo Square's 'Seven Sisters' also embody a vital ecological truth: their walls preserve priceless centuries-old coast redwood timber that can never be logged again, making historic preservation one of the highest forms of forest conservation. Sit on the sloping lawn of Alamo Square Park at late afternoon when the western sun turns the bay windows and spindlework of 710–722 Steiner Street to warm gold while the Transamerica Pyramid gleams on the horizon behind them, and you see two centuries of San Francisco architecture conversing across a single hilltop.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Material Science of Old-Growth Coast Redwood Balloon Framing

Why have Matthew Kavanaugh's 1892–1896 wooden row houses on Steiner Street survived both the magnitude 7.9 earthquake of 1906 and the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta quake of 1989 with minimal structural damage? First, old-growth heartwood of Sequoia sempervirens is naturally impregnated with decay-resistant extractives and maintains exceptional dimensional stability in coastal humidity; second, nineteenth-century redwood balloon framing nailed with horizontal redwood lath and diagonal board sheathing creates a light, ductile wooden diaphragm that flexes and dissipates seismic shear energy rather than shattering like brittle unreinforced masonry.

2. Butch Kardum and the 1963 Colorist Movement

Were Victorian houses originally painted in bright polychrome colors in the 1890s? Historic paint-chip microscopy shows that 1870s Stick and Italianate homes typically used muted earth tones (ochre, russet, olive, and sandstone brown), while 1890s Queen Anne houses began introducing livelier contrasts before twentieth-century white and battleship gray dulled the city. When artist Butch Kardum launched the San Francisco Colorist Movement in 1963—later codified by color consultants like Dr. Color (Bob Buckter) and chronicled by Pomada and Larsen in 1978—he fused 1960s psychedelic color theory with nineteenth-century woodcarving.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three architectural touchstones in mind: first, carpenter-developer Matthew Kavanaugh, who built 710–722 Steiner Street between 1892 and 1896; second, the four major eras of San Francisco Victorian domestic architecture— Gothic Revival (1850s), Italianate (1860s–1870s), Stick/Eastlake (1880s), and Queen Anne (1890s, exemplified by the Seven Sisters); and third, the Van Ness Avenue firebreak of April 1906 that saved the Western Addition's wooden heritage.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Climb to the eastern lawn of Alamo Square Park along Steiner Street between Hayes and Grove streets at sunset—remembering that 710–722 Steiner Street are private residences best admired respectfully from the park lawn and sidewalk. After studying how the seven rooflines step down the hill in front of the downtown skyline, walk the perimeter of Alamo Square along Fulton, Scott, and Fell streets to compare Italianate, Stick-Eastlake, and Queen Anne mansions.
