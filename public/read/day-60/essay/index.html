The World of Universals

Bertrand Russell (1912) • 15 min read

The world of universals may also be described as the world of being: it is unchangeable, rigid, exact, delightful to the mathematician, the logician, the builder of metaphysical systems.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This chapter comes from The Problems of Philosophy, Bertrand Russell's short 1912 introduction to the subject, written in 1911 for readers with no special training. It tackles a question that sounds technical but is actually about everything: when we say that two different things are both white, or that one city is north of another, what exactly are we talking about? Is there something real called whiteness, apart from white things? This is the ancient problem of universals, and it goes back to Plato, whom Russell openly admires here. Russell was at the time deep in Principia Mathematica, his great work with Alfred North Whitehead on the logical foundations of mathematics, and his love of logic and mathematics colours everything he says. His overall meaning is bold: general qualities and relations are real, they do not live in anyone's mind, and they belong to a timeless world that is just as important as the changing one we inhabit.

At the end of the preceding chapter we saw that such entities as relations appear to have a being which is in some way different from that of physical objects, and also different from that of minds and from that of sense-data. In the present chapter we have to consider what is the nature of this kind of being, and also what objects there are that have this kind of being. We will begin with the latter question.

The problem with which we are now concerned is a very old one, since it was brought into philosophy by Plato. Plato’s “theory of ideas” is an attempt to solve this very problem, and in my opinion it is one of the most successful attempts hitherto made. The theory to be advocated in what follows is largely Plato’s, with merely such modifications as time has shown to be necessary.

The way the problem arose for Plato was more or less as follows. Let us consider, say, such a notion as justice. If we ask ourselves what justice is, it is natural to proceed by considering this, that, and the other just act, with a view to discovering what they have in common. They must all, in some sense, partake of a common nature, which will be found in whatever is just and in nothing else. This common nature, in virtue of which they are all just, will be justice itself, the pure essence the admixture of which with facts of ordinary life produces the multiplicity of just acts. Similarly with any other word which may be applicable to common facts, such as “whiteness” for example. The word will be applicable to a number of particular things because they all participate in a common nature or essence. This pure essence is what Plato calls an “idea” or “form.” (It must not be supposed that “ideas,” in his sense, exist in minds, though they may be apprehended by minds.) The “idea” justice is not identical with anything that is just: it is something other than particular things, which particular things partake of. Not being particular, it cannot itself exist in the world of sense. Moreover it is not fleeting or changeable like the things of sense: it is eternally itself, immutable and indestructible.

Thus Plato is led to a supra-sensible world, more real than the common world of sense, the unchangeable world of ideas, which alone gives to the world of sense whatever pale reflection of reality may belong to it. The truly real world, for Plato, is the world of ideas; for whatever we may attempt to say about things in the world of sense, we can only succeed in saying that they participate in such and such ideas, which, therefore, constitute all their character. Hence it is easy to pass on into a mysticism. We may hope, in a mystic illumination, to see the ideas as we see objects of sense; and we may imagine that the ideas exist in heaven. These mystical developments are very natural, but the basis of the theory is in logic, and it is as based in logic that we have to consider it.

The word “idea” has acquired, in the course of time, many associations which are quite misleading when applied to Plato’s “ideas.” We shall therefore use the word “universal” instead of the word “idea,” to describe what Plato meant. The essence of the sort of entity that Plato meant is that it is opposed to the particular things that are given in sensation. We speak of whatever is given in sensation, or is of the same nature as things given in sensation, as a particular; by opposition to this, a universal will be anything which may be shared by many particulars, and has those characteristics which, as we saw, distinguish justice and whiteness from just acts and white things.

When we examine common words, we find that, broadly speaking, proper names stand for particulars, while other substantives, adjectives, prepositions, and verbs stand for universals. Pronouns stand for particulars, but are ambiguous: it is only by the context or the circumstances that we know what particulars they stand for. The word “now” stands for a particular, namely the present moment; but like pronouns, it stands for an ambiguous particular, because the present is always changing.

It will be seen that no sentence can be made up without at least one word which denotes a universal. The nearest approach would be some such statement as “I like this.” But even here the word “like” denotes a universal, for I may like other things, and other people may like things. Thus all truths involve universals, and all knowledge of truths involves acquaintance with universals.

Seeing that nearly all the words to be found in the dictionary stand for universals, it is strange that hardly anybody except students of philosophy ever realizes that there are such entities as universals. We do not naturally dwell upon those words in a sentence which do not stand for particulars; and if we are forced to dwell upon a word which stands for a universal, we naturally think of it as standing for some one of the particulars that come under the universal. When, for example, we hear the sentence, “Charles I’s head was cut off,” we may naturally enough think of Charles I, of Charles I’s head, and of the operation of cutting off his head, which are all particulars; but we do not naturally dwell upon what is meant by the word “head” or the word “cut,” which is a universal: We feel such words to be incomplete and insubstantial; they seem to demand a context before anything can be done with them. Hence we succeed in avoiding all notice of universals as such, until the study of philosophy forces them upon our attention.

Even among philosophers, we may say, broadly, that only those universals which are named by adjectives or substantives have been much or often recognized, while those named by verbs and prepositions have been usually overlooked. This omission has had a very great effect upon philosophy; it is hardly too much to say that most metaphysics, since Spinoza, has been largely determined by it. The way this has occurred is, in outline, as follows: Speaking generally, adjectives and common nouns express qualities or properties of single things, whereas prepositions and verbs tend to express relations between two or more things. Thus the neglect of prepositions and verbs led to the belief that every proposition can be regarded as attributing a property to a single thing, rather than as expressing a relation between two or more things. Hence it was supposed that, ultimately, there can be no such entities as relations between things. Hence either there can be only one thing in the universe, or, if there are many things, they cannot possibly interact in any way, since any interaction would be a relation, and relations are impossible.

The first of these views, advocated by Spinoza and held in our own day by Bradley and many other philosophers, is called monism; the second, advocated by Leibniz but not very common nowadays, is called monadism, because each of the isolated things is called a monad. Both these opposing philosophies, interesting as they are, result, in my opinion, from an undue attention to one sort of universals, namely the sort represented by adjectives and substantives rather than by verbs and prepositions.

As a matter of fact, if anyone were anxious to deny altogether that there are such things as universals, we should find that we cannot strictly prove that there are such entities as qualities, i.e. the universals represented by adjectives and substantives, whereas we can prove that there must be relations, i.e. the sort of universals generally represented by verbs and prepositions. Let us take in illustration the universal whiteness. If we believe that there is such a universal, we shall say that things are white because they have the quality of whiteness. This view, however, was strenuously denied by Berkeley and Hume, who have been followed in this by later empiricists. The form which their denial took was to deny that there are such things as “abstract ideas.” When we want to think of whiteness, they said, we form an image of some particular white thing, and reason concerning this particular, taking care not to deduce anything concerning it which we cannot see to be equally true of any other white thing. As an account of our actual mental processes, this is no doubt largely true. In geometry, for example, when we wish to prove something about all triangles, we draw a particular triangle and reason about it, taking care not to use any characteristic which it does not share with other triangles. The beginner, in order to avoid error, often finds it useful to draw several triangles, as unlike each other as possible, in order to make sure that his reasoning is equally applicable to all of them. But a difficulty emerges as soon as we ask ourselves how we know that a thing is white or a triangle. If we wish to avoid the universals whiteness and triangularity, we shall choose some particular patch of white or some particular triangle, and say that anything is white or a triangle if it has the right sort of resemblance to our chosen particular. But then the resemblance required will have to be a universal. Since there are many white things, the resemblance must hold between many pairs of particular white things; and this is the characteristic of a universal. It will be useless to say that there is a different resemblance for each pair, for then we shall have to say that these resemblances resemble each other, and thus at last we shall be forced to admit resemblance as a universal. The relation of resemblance, therefore, must be a true universal. And having been forced to admit this universal, we find that it is no longer worth while to invent difficult and unplausible theories to avoid the admission of such universals as whiteness and triangularity.

Berkeley and Hume failed to perceive this refutation of their rejection of “abstract ideas,” because, like their adversaries, they only thought of qualities, and altogether ignored relations as universals. We have therefore here another respect in which the rationalists appear to have been in the right as against the empiricists, although, owing to the neglect or denial of relations, the deductions made by rationalists were, if anything, more apt to be mistaken than those made by empiricists.

Having now seen that there must be such entities as universals, the next point to be proved is that their being is not merely mental. By this is meant that whatever being belongs to them is independent of their being thought of or in any way apprehended by minds. We have already touched on this subject at the end of the preceding chapter, but we must now consider more fully what sort of being it is that belongs to universals.

Consider such a proposition as “Edinburgh is north of London.” Here we have a relation between two places, and it seems plain that the relation subsists independently of our knowledge of it. When we come to know that Edinburgh is north of London, we come to know something which has to do only with Edinburgh and London: we do not cause the truth of the proposition by coming to know it, on the contrary we merely apprehend a fact which was there before we knew it. The part of the earth’s surface where Edinburgh stands would be north of the part where London stands, even if there were no human being to know about north and south, and even if there were no minds at all in the universe. This is, of course, denied by many philosophers, either for Berkeley’s reasons or for Kant’s. But we have already considered these reasons, and decided that they are inadequate. We may therefore now assume it to be true that nothing mental is presupposed in the fact that Edinburgh is north of London. But this fact involves the relation “north of,” which is a universal; and it would be impossible for the whole fact to involve nothing mental if the relation “north of,” which is a constituent part of the fact, did involve anything mental. Hence we must admit that the relation, like the terms it relates, is not dependent upon thought, but belongs to the independent world which thought apprehends but does not create.

This conclusion, however, is met by the difficulty that the relation “north of” does not seem to exist in the same sense in which Edinburgh and London exist. If we ask “Where and when does this relation exist?” the answer must be “Nowhere and nowhen.” There is no place or time where we can find the relation “north of.” It does not exist in Edinburgh any more than in London, for it relates the two and is neutral as between them. Nor can we say that it exists at any particular time. Now everything that can be apprehended by the senses or by introspection exists at some particular time. Hence the relation “north of” is radically different from such things. It is neither in space nor in time, neither material nor mental; yet it is something.

It is largely the very peculiar kind of being that belongs to universals which has led many people to suppose that they are really mental. We can think of a universal, and our thinking then exists in a perfectly ordinary sense, like any other mental act. Suppose, for example, that we are thinking of whiteness. Then in one sense it may be said that whiteness is “in our mind.” We have here the same ambiguity as we noted in discussing Berkeley in Chapter IV. In the strict sense, it is not whiteness that is in our mind, but the act of thinking of whiteness. The connected ambiguity in the word “idea,” which we noted at the same time, also causes confusion here. In one sense of this word, namely the sense in which it denotes the object of an act of thought, whiteness is an “idea.” Hence, if the ambiguity is not guarded against, we may come to think that whiteness is an “idea” in the other sense, i.e. an act of thought; and thus we come to think that whiteness is mental. But in so thinking, we rob it of its essential quality of universality. One man’s act of thought is necessarily a different thing from another man’s; one man’s act of thought at one time is necessarily a different thing from the same man’s act of thought at another time. Hence, if whiteness were the thought as opposed to its object, no two different men could think of it, and no one man could think of it twice. That which many different thoughts of whiteness have in common is their object, and this object is different from all of them. Thus universals are not thoughts, though when known they are the objects of thoughts.

We shall find it convenient only to speak of things existing when they are in time, that is to say, when we can point to some time at which they exist (not excluding the possibility of their existing at all times). Thus thoughts and feelings, minds and physical objects exist. But universals do not exist in this sense; we shall say that they subsist or have being, where “being” is opposed to “existence” as being timeless. The world of universals, therefore, may also be described as the world of being. The world of being is unchangeable, rigid, exact, delightful to the mathematician, the logician, the builder of metaphysical systems, and all who love perfection more than life. The world of existence is fleeting, vague, without sharp boundaries, without any clear plan or arrangement, but it contains all thoughts and feelings, all the data of sense, and all physical objects, everything that can do either good or harm, everything that makes any difference to the value of life and the world. According to our temperaments, we shall prefer the contemplation of the one or of the other. The one we do not prefer will probably seem to us a pale shadow of the one we prefer, and hardly worthy to be regarded as in any sense real. But the truth is that both have the same claim on our impartial attention, both are real, and both are important to the metaphysician. Indeed no sooner have we distinguished the two worlds than it becomes necessary to consider their relations.

But first of all we must examine our knowledge of universals. This consideration will occupy us in the following chapter, where we shall find that it solves the problem of a priori knowledge, from which we were first led to consider universals.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Russell opens by noting that this is a very old problem, introduced into philosophy by Plato, and that Plato's theory of ideas is, in his opinion, one of the most successful attempts yet made to solve it. The problem is this. We use general words constantly: justice, whiteness, triangle, brother. A word like justice applies to many different acts, and we feel that they are all just because they share something. Plato called that shared something a form or idea, a pure essence that the many particular things take part in. Russell wants to keep the core of this, while pointing out that Plato's word idea is misleading, since the thing in question is not in anyone's mind.

He then draws his central distinction. A particular is something that exists in time, a thing you could point to, like a patch of white or a person. A universal is anything that can be shared by many particulars. And when you look at ordinary language, he says, you find that proper names stand for particulars, while other nouns, adjectives, prepositions and verbs stand for universals. Whiteness, triangularity, and relations like being north of are all universals.

Why believe in them? The most important argument concerns relations. Consider the statement that Edinburgh is north of London. This is true whether or not anyone knows it. We do not cause it by thinking it. We merely discover a fact that was there before we knew it. But the fact involves the relation north of, and that relation is not one particular thing, since it holds between many other places too. So it must be a universal, and it cannot depend on any mind.

Next he deals with the philosophers who said there are no universals. Berkeley and Hume, he reports, denied there are abstract ideas, claiming that when we think of whiteness we really picture some particular white thing. Russell's reply is that if you say something is white because it resembles a chosen white patch, then you need the resemblance, and resemblance holds among many pairs, so you have smuggled in a universal after all. Denying universals in one place just forces you to accept them in another.

Finally, he says that universals do not exist in the way particulars do, because they are not in time. They subsist, or have being. The world of being is unchanging, rigid and exact, delightful to the mathematician and the logician. The world of existence is fleeting and vague, but it contains everything that matters to life. Both are real, and both deserve our attention.

The overall shape of the argument, then, is a chain with three links. Language is full of general terms. Some of those terms, especially relations, cannot be explained away as mental pictures. Therefore there must be general things, which he calls universals, and these belong to a realm of their own, outside time and independent of us. Seen that way, the chapter is less a pile of examples than a single, tidy piece of reasoning, and it is worth listening for how each stage rests on the one before.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The core of this chapter is a genuinely strong argument, and it is presented with great economy. The case from relations is the real achievement. Philosophers before Russell had mostly thought about universals as qualities, like redness. By turning to relations, such as north of, he picks examples that are difficult to reduce to our mental images. It is easy to say that redness is just a way we group similar patches in our minds. It is much harder to say that Edinburgh's being north of London is something we construct. That sharpening of the debate was a real contribution, and Russell is right to point out that both Berkeley and Hume overlooked it because they only considered qualities.

The resemblance argument is also neat. It shows in a few lines that the nominalist, who denies universals, cannot escape having to speak of some repeated feature. Even if the objection is not fatal, as some later thinkers argued, it forces the opponent to pay a price, and it is a good example of how philosophical argument works as a kind of accounting.

The prose rises, too, at the end. The passage about the world of being, rigid, exact, delightful to the mathematician, set against the fleeting and vague world of existence, is some of the most eloquent writing in the book. It reveals the temperament behind the argument, a love of perfection and permanence, and it is also fair to the other temperament, which prefers the living world.

But there are real weaknesses. First, the argument often proves less than it claims. That a relation holds between many pairs does not by itself show that there is a separate entity, the relation, existing in a realm of being. It might only show that our language needs general terms. Russell moves from the requirement that we speak generally to the claim that there is a world of general things, and that is a large leap.

Second, the notion of subsistence is obscure. He says universals have being but not existence, yet he gives little help in understanding what that being amounts to. How can something be real without being anywhere or at any time, and how do we come to know it? The chapter promises an answer in the next, but here the picture is more poetic than precise.

Third, the argument against the idea that universals are thoughts is quick. And finally, the chapter's confident dismissal of Berkeley and Kant by reference to earlier chapters feels a little breezy. Still, as an introductory case for a very old theory, it is strikingly effective.

III. The Impact It Had

This chapter belongs to a stretch of Russell's career in which he was a convinced realist about universals, and it helped make that position a live option in twentieth-century philosophy. It was not unique to him. G. E. Moore, his Cambridge colleague, and others were also reacting against the idealism that had dominated British thought, and a revival of Platonism in logic and mathematics was in the air. But the book put the case before a very wide audience, and for many students it was their first encounter with the debate between realists and nominalists.

Within Russell's work, the position played a role in his logic. In Principia Mathematica, the notion of propositional functions, which are general forms with a slot to be filled, owes a debt to the idea that general features are real things we can talk about. His stance also supported a Platonist view of mathematics, in which numbers and logical truths are discovered, not invented. That view was shared by Gottlob Frege and later by Kurt Gödel, who defended a robust mathematical Platonism, and it remains one of the main positions in the philosophy of mathematics.

The debate that followed was lively. Frank Ramsey, a young Cambridge philosopher, wrote a famous short paper in 1925 arguing that the division between particulars and universals is not as clear as Russell supposed. In the middle of the century, Nelson Goodman and W. V. Quine defended nominalist scepticism about abstract entities, while D. C. Williams proposed that properties are particular instances, sometimes called tropes. In the 1970s and later, David Armstrong built an elaborate realism about universals grounded in science, in a direct line from Russell. Russell himself later modified some of these views, and in his later writings he took a more guarded stand on the particular and universal distinction.

The chapter also influenced the way the problem is taught. The terms particular and universal, and the examples of whiteness and being north of, are standard in introductory metaphysics. Whenever students are asked whether redness is a thing or just a way of talking, they are repeating a conversation that Russell's chapter helped to organise.

Beyond philosophy, the idea of a timeless realm of exact forms has had a long cultural echo, in mathematics, in aesthetics, and in the picture of science as the discovery of laws that hold whether or not anyone is there to know them.

IV. What Readers Loved

One thing readers admire is the sheer nerve of defending Plato. In an age that was supposed to have outgrown him, Russell says that Plato's theory is largely right, with merely such modifications as time has shown to be necessary. There is something refreshing about a modern logician treating a twenty-four-century-old idea as live. Readers who think philosophy only ever moves forward are often delighted to find that an ancient answer can still hold its own.

They also love the example of Edinburgh and London. It is homely and exact. The thought that north of is not something we make up, that it would hold even if no minds existed at all, produces a distinct, slightly giddy feeling that something real has been grasped. Students often report that this is the moment the whole subject clicks, when they see why anyone would believe in universals in the first place.

The eloquent passage near the end is another favourite. Russell is usually dry, but here he allows himself a glow of feeling for the world of being, which he calls delightful to the mathematician and all who love perfection more than life. Readers who have ever felt the beauty of a proof or a geometric truth recognise that sentiment immediately. And he is generous, adding that those who prefer the living, changing world should not look down on the other, since both have the same claim on our impartial attention.

There is, too, the satisfaction of the argument against Berkeley and Hume. Many readers have been taught to admire those two philosophers, and it is a treat to watch Russell find a hole in their view using only a humble example, the relation of resemblance. People who enjoy seeing a clever argument done in a few strokes have always valued this chapter.

Finally, readers appreciate how the chapter connects. It ties together earlier discussion of what we know, in the account of things and truths, with the coming discussion of how we know general truths, and it hints that the world of universals will solve the puzzle of knowledge not gained by experience. That sense of a plan unfolding gives the book momentum.

Many readers also value what the chapter does for the ordinary words they use every day. After reading it, a term like justice, or a phrase like to the left of, no longer feels like a throwaway. You begin to notice that every sentence you speak is full of general terms, and that you understand them without effort. Russell makes that quiet daily miracle visible, and readers often say the world looks a little richer afterwards.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The main pushback comes from nominalists, who hold that only individual things exist and that general words are simply labels we apply to groups of similar things. They think Russell has multiplied entities beyond necessity. William of Ockham's old maxim, that entities should not be multiplied without need, is the usual weapon. On this view, Russell takes a fact about language, that we use general terms, and turns it into a population of mysterious objects. Quine and Goodman pressed this objection in the twentieth century, and their scepticism about abstract entities was widely shared.

The resemblance argument has been challenged directly. Some nominalists respond that resemblance does not need to be a universal; they say it is a primitive fact about the individual things, or that an endless regress of resemblances is harmless. Others argue that the argument assumes what it is meant to prove, that anything which holds between many pairs must be a thing in its own right. Frank Ramsey made a related point, saying that the supposed difference between names for particulars and words for universals is a matter of grammar, not of real kinds of being.

Wittgenstein, in his later philosophy, challenged the whole demand for something common to all cases. In his remarks on family resemblance, he argued that things we call by one word, like games, need not share a single essence. This undercuts the Platonic picture that Russell defends, in which each general word corresponds to one shared form.

Empiricists object to the epistemology. If universals exist outside space and time, how can we know them? We have no senses by which to meet a timeless object, and critics say the chapter gestures at the problem without solving it. This worry is sometimes put as a question about how Platonists can explain our contact with entities that cannot cause anything.

There are also complaints about the notion of subsistence. Critics find the distinction between being and existence an evasion, a way of having things both ways, and even sympathetic readers find it hard to say what it adds. Russell himself later became uneasy with some of this language.

Lastly, a more general objection holds that the chapter is simply too quick, covering centuries of dispute in a few pages and dismissing great philosophers in a sentence. That is an unavoidable cost of a popular book, and Russell would likely have said so himself.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The problem of universals is among the oldest in philosophy, and this chapter is a short, vivid statement of one side of it. The realist side descends from Plato and runs through the medieval realists, such as Anselm, and later through the rationalists of the seventeenth century. The nominalist and conceptualist side runs from Ockham through Locke, Berkeley and Hume. Russell's chapter is explicitly an effort to reopen the case that the empiricists had closed, and he says that here the rationalists appear to have been in the right as against the empiricists.

That is a striking admission, because Russell is usually counted among the empiricists. The book as a whole is a blend, and this chapter shows its rationalist streak. It leads directly into the next chapter, on how we know universals, and from there to the claim that some knowledge, such as logic and arithmetic, is independent of experience. In that way the chapter is a hinge in the book's argument, answering the problem of a priori knowledge that the previous chapters left hanging.

In the larger picture, the chapter links to the revival of Platonism in the philosophy of mathematics, represented by Frege and Gödel, and to contemporary metaphysics, where the debate between realists and nominalists, with its newer options such as tropes and resemblance classes, is still very much alive. Science also supplies a modern angle: when physicists speak of laws that hold everywhere, they seem to rely on something like universals, and Armstrong's later work made this the centrepiece of his realism.

Compare it with the Platonic dialogues themselves, where the forms are presented with myth and poetry, or with Aristotle's more earthy view that universals exist only in the things that have them. Russell's version sits between mathematical rigour and Platonic enthusiasm, and it is more argumentative than either.

Why read it now? Because it asks what it means for general truths to be real, a question that comes up whenever we ask whether mathematics is discovered or invented, whether moral facts are objective, or whether categories like species and justice carve nature at its joints. The chapter will not settle those questions. But it gives a clear, memorable and spirited account of one answer, and it shows how a good argument can start from nothing more than a city map.
