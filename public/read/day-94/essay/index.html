Discourses I.29: On Constancy (Or Firmness)

Epictetus (c. 108 AD) • 14 min read

The essence of the Good is a certain kind of moral will; the essence of the Bad is a certain kind of moral will. What then are external things? Materials for the will.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This is a long, fierce lecture from the Discourses of Epictetus, a Greek Stoic teacher who lived under the Roman emperors around the year 100. Born a slave and later freed, he taught in a school in northwestern Greece, and a student named Arrian took down his words. Epictetus never published anything himself, so we hear a living classroom voice, with its jokes, jabs and sudden stories. The subject is constancy, meaning steadiness of mind when someone more powerful threatens you. His argument is that a tyrant can take your body, your property and your freedom of movement, but cannot touch your will, your power to judge and choose. Along the way he draws on Socrates, on a stolen lamp, and on the stage and the athletic arena. The chapter is demanding, even stern, but it is also a vivid account of how people have faced prison and death with their dignity intact.

The being (nature) of the Good is a certain Will; the being of the Bad is a certain kind of Will. What then are externals? Materials for the Will, about which the will being conversant shall obtain its own good or evil. How shall it obtain the good? If it does not admire (overvalue) the materials; for the opinions about the materials, if the opinions are right, make the will good: but perverse and distorted opinions make the will bad. God has fixed this law, and says, “If you would have anything good, receive it from yourself.” You say, “No, but I will have it from another.” Do not so: but receive it from yourself. Therefore when the tyrant threatens and calls me, I say, “Whom do you threaten?” If he says, “I will put you in chains,” I say, “You threaten my hands and my feet.” If he says, “I will cut off your head,” I reply, “You threaten my head.” If he says, “I will throw you into prison,” I say, “You threaten the whole of this poor body.” If he threatens me with banishment, I say the same. Does he then not threaten you at all? If I feel that all these things do not concern me, he does not threaten me at all; but if I fear any of them, it is I whom he threatens. Whom then do I fear? the master of what? The master of things which are in my own power? There is no such master. Do I fear the master of things which are not in my power? And what are these things to me?

“Do you philosophers then teach us to despise kings?” I hope not. Who among us teaches to claim against them the power over things which they possess? Take my poor body, take my property, take my reputation, take those who are about me. If I advise any persons to claim these things, they may truly accuse me. “Yes, but I intend to command your opinions also.” And who has given you this power? How can you conquer the opinion of another man? “By applying terror to it,” he replies, “I will conquer it.” Do you not know that opinion conquers itself, and is not conquered by another? But nothing else can conquer Will except the Will itself. For this reason too the law of God is most powerful and most just, which is this: Let the stronger always be superior to the weaker. Ten are stronger than one. For what? For putting in chains, for killing, for dragging whither they choose, for taking away what a man has. The ten therefore conquer the one in this in which they are stronger. In what then are the ten weaker? If the one possesses right opinions and the others do not. Well then, can the ten conquer in this matter? How is it possible? If we were placed in the scales, must not the heavier draw down the scale in which it is?

How strange then that Socrates should have been so treated by the Athenians. Slave, why do you say Socrates? Speak of the thing as it is: how strange that the poor body of Socrates should have been carried off and dragged to prison by stronger men, and that anyone should have given hemlock to the poor body of Socrates, and that it should breathe out the life. Do these things seem strange, do they seem unjust, do you on account of these things blame God? Had Socrates then no equivalent for these things? Where then for him was the nature of good? Whom shall we listen to, you or him? And what does Socrates say? Anytus and Melitus can kill me, but they cannot hurt me: and further, he says, “If it so pleases God, so let it be.”

But show me that he who has the inferior principles overpowers him who is superior in principles. You will never show this, nor come near showing it; for this is the law of nature and of God that the superior shall always overpower the inferior. In what? In that in which it is superior. One body is stronger than another: many are stronger than one: the thief is stronger than he who is not a thief. This is the reason why I also lost my lamp, because in wakefulness the thief was superior to me. But the man bought the lamp at this price: for a lamp he became a thief, a faithless fellow, and like a wild beast. This seemed to him a good bargain. Be it so. But a man has seized me by the cloak, and is drawing me to the public place: then others bawl out, “Philosopher, what has been the use of your opinions? see you are dragged to prison, you are going to be beheaded.” And what system of philosophy (εἰσαγωγήν) could I have made so that, if a stronger man should have laid hold of my cloak, I should not be dragged off; that if ten men should have laid hold of me and cast me into prison, I should not be cast in? Have I learned nothing else then? I have learned to see that everything which happens, if it be independent of my will, is nothing to me. I may ask, if you have not gained by this. Why then do you seek advantage in anything else than in that in which you have learned that advantage is?

Then sitting in prison I say: The man who cries out in this way neither hears what words mean, nor understands what is said, nor does he care at all to know what philosophers say or what they do. Let him alone.

But now he says to the prisoner, “Come out from your prison.”⁠—If you have no further need of me in prison, I come out: if you should have need of me again, I will enter the prison.⁠—“How long will you act thus?”⁠—So long as reason requires me to be with the body: but when reason does not require this, take away the body, and fare you well. Only we must not do it inconsiderately, nor weakly, nor for any slight reason; for, on the other hand, God does not wish it to be done, and he has need of such a world and such inhabitants in it. But if he sounds the signal for retreat, as he did to Socrates, we must obey him who gives the signal, as if he were a general.

Well then, ought we to say such things to the many? Why should we? Is it not enough for a man to be persuaded himself? When children come clapping their hands and crying out, “Today is the good Saturnalia,” do we say, “The Saturnalia are not good”? By no means, but we clap our hands also. Do you also then, when you are not able to make a man change his mind, be assured that he is a child, and clap your hands with him; and if you do not choose to do this, keep silent.

A man must keep this in mind; and when he is called to any such difficulty, he should know that the time is come for showing if he has been instructed. For he who is come into a difficulty is like a young man from a school who has practiced the resolution of syllogisms; and if any person proposes to him an easy syllogism, he says: rather propose to me a syllogism which is skillfully complicated that I may exercise myself on it. Even athletes are dissatisfied with slight young men, and say, “He cannot lift me.”⁠—“This is a youth of noble disposition.” [You do not so]; but when the time of trial is come, one of you must weep and say, “I wish that I had learned more.” A little more of what? If you did not learn these things in order to show them in practice, why did you learn them? I think that there is someone, among you who are sitting here, who is suffering like a woman in labor, and saying, “Oh, that such a difficulty does not present itself to me as that which has come to this man; oh, that I should be wasting my life in a corner, when I might be crowned at Olympia. When will anyone announce to me such a contest?” Such ought to be the disposition of all of you. Even among the gladiators of Caesar (the Emperor) there are some who complain grievously that they are not brought forward and matched, and they offer up prayers to God and address themselves to their superintendents entreating that they may fight. And will no one among you show himself such? I would willingly take a voyage [to Rome] for this purpose and see what my athlete is doing, how he is studying his subject.⁠—I do not choose such a subject, he says. Why, is it in your power to take what subject you choose? There has been given to you such a body as you have, such parents, such brethren, such a country, such a place in your country:⁠—then you come to me and say, “Change my subject.” Have you not abilities which enable you to manage the subject which has been given to you? [You ought to say]: “It is your business to propose; it is mine to exercise myself well.” However, you do not say so, but you say, “Do not propose to me such a tropic, but such [as I would choose]: do not urge against me such an objection, but such [as I would choose].” There will be a time perhaps when tragic actors will suppose that they are [only] masks and buskins and the long cloak. I say, these things, man, are your material and subject. Utter something that we may know whether you are a tragic actor or a buffoon; for both of you have all the rest in common. If anyone then should take away the tragic actor’s buskins and his mask, and introduce him on the stage as a phantom, is the tragic actor lost, or does he still remain? If he has voice, he still remains.

An example of another kind: “Assume the governorship of a province.” I assume it, and when I have assumed it, I show how an instructed man behaves. “Lay aside the laticlave (the mark of senatorial rank), and clothing yourself in rags, come forward in this character.” What then, have I not the power of displaying a good voice (that is, of doing something that I ought to do)? How then do you now appear (on the stage of life)? As a witness summoned by God. “Come forward, you, and bear testimony for me, for you are worthy to be brought forward as a witness by me: is anything external to the will good or bad? do I hurt any man? have I made every man’s interest dependent on any man except himself? What testimony do you give for God?”⁠—I am in a wretched condition, Master (Lord), and I am unfortunate; no man cares for me, no man gives me anything; all blame me, all steak ill of me.⁠—Is this the evidence that you are going to give, and disgrace his summons, who has conferred so much honor on you, and thought you worthy of being called to bear such testimony?

But suppose that he who has the power has declared, “I judge you to be impious and profane.” What has happened to you? I have been judged to be impious and profane? Nothing else? Nothing else. But if the same person had passed judgment on a hypothetical syllogism (συνημμένου), and had made a declaration, “the conclusion that, if it is day, it is light, I declare to be false,” what has happened to the hypothetical syllogism? who is judged in this case? who has been condemned? the hypothetical syllogism, or the man who has been deceived by it? Does he then who has the power of making any declaration about you know what is pious or impious? Has he studied it, and has he learned it? Where? From whom? Then is it the fact that a musician pays no regard to him who declares that the lowest chord in the lyre is the highest; nor yet a geometrician, if he declares that the lines from the centre of a circle to the circumference are not equal; and shall he who is really instructed pay any regard to the uninstructed man when he pronounces judgment on what is pious and what is impious, on what is just and unjust? Oh, the signal wrong done by the instructed. Did they learn this here?

Will you not leave the small arguments (λογάρια) about these matters to others, to lazy fellows, that they may sit in a corner and receive their sorry pay, or grumble that no one gives them anything; and will you not come forward and make use of what you have learned? For it is not these small arguments that are wanted now: the writings of the Stoics are full of them. What then is the thing which is wanted? A man who shall apply them, one who by his acts shall bear testimony to his words. Assume, I entreat you, this character, that we may no longer use in the schools the examples of the ancients, but may have some example of our own.

To whom then does the contemplation of these matters (philosophical inquiries) belong? To him who has leisure, for man is an animal that loves contemplation. But it is shameful to contemplate these things as runaway slaves do: we should sit, as in a theatre, free from distraction, and listen at one time to the tragic actor, at another time to the lute-player; and not do as slaves do. As soon as the slave has taken his station he praises the actor and at the same time looks round: then if anyone calls out his master’s name, the slave is immediately frightened and disturbed. It is shameful for philosophers thus to contemplate the works of nature. For what is a master? Man is not the master of man; but death is, and life and pleasure and pain; for if he comes without these things, bring Caesar to me and you will see how firm I am. But when he shall come with these things, thundering and lightning, and when I am afraid of them, what do I do then except to recognize my master like the runaway slave? But so long as I have any respite from these terrors, as a runaway slave stands in the theatre, so do I: I bathe, I drink, I sing; but all this I do with terror and uneasiness. But if I shall release myself from my masters, that is from those things by means of which masters are formidable, what further trouble have I, what master have I still?

What then, ought we to publish these things to all men? No, but we ought to accommodate ourselves to the ignorant (τοῖς ἰδιώταις) and to say: “This man recommends to me that which he thinks good for himself: I excuse him.” For Socrates also excused the jailor, who had the charge of him in prison and was weeping when Socrates was going to drink the poison, and said, “How generously he laments over us.” Does he then say to the jailor that for this reason we have sent away the women? No, but he says it to his friends who were able to hear (understand) it; and he treats the jailor as a child.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The chapter begins with a bold claim: the good and the bad are both located in the will, the faculty of choosing and judging. Everything outside the will, the body, property, reputation, other people, is only raw material. A person becomes good or bad by the opinions they form about that material. If you overvalue it, you will be at its mercy. If you judge it rightly, you cannot be hurt. God, Epictetus says, has given a simple law: if you want something good, take it from yourself.

The famous demonstration is the tyrant. When someone threatens to chain him, he replies that they threaten his hands and feet. When they threaten his head, his head. When they threaten prison, the whole poor body. If I truly feel that none of this concerns me, then he threatens me not at all. If I fear any of it, then I have handed over the power myself. The man who threatens is the master only of things I should not have made my masters.

Epictetus then asks a subtler question: what about the claim that the ten always beat the one? He accepts it, but only in the area where they are stronger. Ten men can drag you off, chain you, kill you. They cannot make you believe something false against your will, because opinion is conquered only by itself. The law that the stronger prevails is true, he says, but it applies strictly: the superior overcomes the inferior in that in which he is superior. A thief is stronger than a sleeper, and that is how he lost his lamp. But the thief, who bought a lamp at the price of becoming a thief, made a poor bargain, since he gave up his honesty for a cheap object.

Socrates is the proof case. He was dragged to prison and given hemlock, and people call it strange. Epictetus says to speak accurately: it was the poor body of Socrates that was dragged. Socrates himself said that his accusers could kill him but not harm him.

The second half turns from defense to practice. When a hard moment comes, he says, it is the moment to show you have learned something. He compares it to an athlete who is bored by weak opponents, and to a student who wants a hard logic puzzle. He mocks those who say they wish for different material, a different body, parents or country. The material is what it is, and the test is how you work with it. Stoic philosophers who only write clever arguments, he says, have missed the point. What the world needs is someone whose acts testify to their words.

He ends with the runaway slave watching a play, who enjoys it while looking over his shoulder for his master. Philosophy done in fear, he says, is like that. The real masters are death, life, pleasure and pain, and only when we are free of those can we watch the world with calm.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This chapter is one of the most forceful things in the Discourses, and its force comes from the combination of logic and theatre. The argument is simple enough to state in a sentence, but Epictetus makes it live through a rapid string of scenes: the tyrant, the cloak, the lamp, the prison, the stage. A mind that might nod at an abstract claim is made to hear it said in a threatening voice and answered back.

The repeated answer to the tyrant is the best of these. It is almost a formula, and the turn from "you threaten me" to "you threaten my leg" is a memorable piece of reframing. It also teaches by repetition, which suits a spoken lecture. A listener can leave with a short script to hold.

The stolen lamp is also well chosen. It is a tiny loss, treated seriously enough to make a point and lightly enough to be funny. The domestic touch helps. He does not mourn the lamp. He says the thief paid a high price for it in honesty. That inversion of who has lost what is clever and stays in the mind.

The chapter's weaknesses are in its length and its argument. It is very long for what it says, and it circles. Several of the same moves, the athlete, the actor, the tyrant, come back in different clothes. In a classroom this repetition would have helped. On the page it can feel loose, and some of the sections, such as the comparison of logic exercises to trials in life, do not add much.

The central claim also depends on a very strong view: that only the will matters, and that the body is not truly "me." A reader who thinks of the body as part of the self, and of torture as an attack on a person rather than on a possession, may feel that Epictetus has made things too easy. He is describing an ideal in which pain and loss simply do not count. Even sympathetic readers wonder how many people can truly live that way under real torture, and the chapter gives little help for the case where the will itself begins to break.

There is also a rhetorical problem. He often puts objections in the mouths of fools, "Slave, why do you say Socrates?" and the scolding can seem unfair. The imagined opponent is always weaker than a real one would be.

And yet the ending, with its quiet distinction between what to tell philosophers and what to tell the jailer, shows a gentler side. Epictetus says that we should not trumpet these ideas to everyone, and that when someone cannot be persuaded, we may treat them as a child and clap along. That final note of tact tempers the severity and is the most humane part of the chapter.

III. The Impact It Had

This lecture contains perhaps the best-known statement of what later writers have called the Stoic "inner citadel," the idea that there is a part of a person that no external force can reach. That phrase belongs to the French scholar Pierre Hadot, who used it as the title of his book on Marcus Aurelius, but it fits Epictetus equally well, and this chapter is one of its sources.

Marcus Aurelius, who knew Epictetus's teaching through Arrian's notes or through his teacher, repeats the point again and again in the Meditations, reminding himself that what is not in his power cannot hurt his character. The idea of a mind protected from tyranny also shaped the life and death of other Romans. Stoic senators who defied emperors drew on this tradition, and later historians celebrated them as figures of moral resistance.

In Christian thought, the picture of Socrates drinking hemlock and the idea that a good man cannot be truly harmed influenced early writers who spoke of martyrdom. Boethius, writing in prison, used similar arguments to console himself. In the Renaissance and Enlightenment, the Stoic image of the free mind in a captive body was echoed by writers on liberty and conscience, including Montaigne, and the idea that tyrants control only what is external became part of the vocabulary of resistance.

The most striking modern case is Admiral James Stockdale, a United States Navy pilot shot down over North Vietnam in 1965 who spent more than seven years as a prisoner. Stockdale had studied Epictetus before his capture, and he later said that the Enchiridion and the Discourses helped him endure torture and isolation. He taught the lesson to other prisoners and described it in lectures on leadership. His account made this very chapter, the one about tyrants threatening the body while the will remains free, an unusually practical text for soldiers.

Elsewhere, therapists who follow cognitive approaches draw on the passage about how we respond to threats. The idea that we are disturbed by our judgments of events, and not by events themselves, appears throughout Stoic writing and was cited by Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck. In the recent revival of Stoicism, this chapter is often quoted for its tyrant scripts.

In teaching, the chapter is used to introduce the Stoic division between things in our power and things not in it, and also to illustrate how ancient philosophy was understood as a way of life. Scholars of Roman history use it as evidence for what it felt like to live under emperors who could, and did, order the deaths of philosophers.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved most in this chapter is the sheer nerve of it. Here is a man who was once a slave, speaking calmly about chains and prison and beheading as if they were minor inconveniences. The repeated answer to the tyrant, that he threatens only my leg, my head, my poor body, has a bracing effect. It makes fear seem smaller. People who face intimidating situations, whether a bully, a boss or a regime, often find that rehearsing these lines is steadying.

They also love the Socrates passage. By saying that it was only the poor body of Socrates that was dragged off, Epictetus cuts through the sense of tragedy and replaces it with admiration. Readers who know Plato's account of the trial and death of Socrates find that this reframing gives them a way to think about his end as a victory, not a defeat. The line "Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot hurt me" is a favorite of those who have to bear injustice.

The humor is another source of affection. The stolen lamp is a nice example: a philosopher mildly pointing out that the thief got a bad bargain. The scenes of men crying, "I wish I had learned more," or of gladiators begging to fight, have the grit of real teaching. Epictetus is funny in the way a good coach is funny, by naming what everyone is thinking.

Readers also respond to the challenge. The chapter is not a comfort for the idle. It tells the reader that difficulty is the moment philosophy was made for, and that wishing for easy material is a kind of cowardice. Many people say they have taken on a hard task partly because of the athlete comparison, in which an athlete is displeased by weak opponents and wants someone who can really test him.

The image of the tragic actor stripped of mask and costume, who still has his voice, is often admired. It says that when everything external is taken away, what is left is the thing that mattered, and that you can find out whether you are a real actor or a buffoon only when the props are gone.

Finally, readers have valued the tact of the ending. The idea that we do not need to argue with everyone, that we can let a weeping jailer be and not correct him, strikes many as wise. It suggests that the Stoic is not a preacher but a person who has quietly worked something out and is content to live by it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The classic criticism is that this kind of teaching leaves injustice standing. If a tyrant can only threaten what is not truly yours, then there is no reason to oppose him. Hegel, in his account of ancient philosophy, treated Stoic freedom as a retreat inward, a freedom that is only in thought. A slave who has learned to say "you threaten only my body" is still in chains. Marxist and other readers have made the same charge: that Stoicism makes oppression easier to bear and therefore easier to maintain.

A defense is available. Stoic senators did resist emperors, and some died for it. Epictetus's students were being prepared for public life. But the chapter itself emphasizes endurance and says little about changing laws or ending tyranny. It tells you to accept your arrest, not to organize against it. When he says that the tyrant is not to be opposed over body and property, "take my poor body, take my property," it seems an easy surrender of everything people might fight for.

A second criticism is about psychology. It is one thing to say that the will is free, and another to live as though pain were nothing. Many who have experienced torture or grief say that the mind does not stay detached on command. Critics ask whether the teaching sets a standard that makes suffering people feel they have failed. Epictetus has little patience for tears, and a person weeping for a lost child might find his words unkind. Even Stockdale, in his own writing, acknowledged that the discipline was hard and that he felt the weight of what he had endured.

Third, Pascal in the seventeenth century said that Epictetus knew our duties but not our weakness, and flattered us by suggesting we could be self-sufficient. This is a religious critique, but it points to something real: the chapter has a heroic picture of human beings that does not fit everyone.

Fourth, the tone is often harsh. The chapter calls listeners slaves, women in labor, buffoons, cowards. It is meant to shake them, but readers sometimes find it bullying and preachy. Philosophers of a gentler temper have found Seneca or Marcus Aurelius easier company.

Finally, some scholars note the limits of what we know. These are notes taken by a student, and nobody can say how much was Epictetus and how much Arrian's shaping. The ending, in which we should not tell these ideas to everyone, has also been read as elitist, as though wisdom were for a few and the many were children to be humored. That remark has troubled modern readers more than it did ancient ones.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter sits within Roman Stoicism, which began as a Greek school founded in Athens around 300 BC and became, under the Roman emperors, a philosophy of survival and dignity. The Stoics taught that virtue is the only real good and that the universe is governed by reason. Under Nero and later emperors, the school became associated with a small, principled opposition, and its teachers were exiled or killed. Epictetus himself was banished from Rome when the emperor Domitian expelled philosophers, which is why he ended up teaching in Greece.

The chapter's chief ancestor is Socrates, who in Plato's Apology says that no harm can come to a good man, and who in the Crito and the Phaedo walks calmly to his death. Epictetus treats him as a model, and the claim that Socrates' accusers could not hurt him is a direct echo of Plato.

Its descendants include almost every tradition that locates freedom inside. Early Christian martyr accounts, from the second century onward, depict believers calmly facing wild beasts and executioners. Boethius' Consolation of Philosophy was written while he awaited execution. Modern writing from prison, such as the letters of Dietrich Bonhoeffer or Viktor Frankl's account of the camps, with his claim that the last human freedom is to choose one's attitude, belong to the same family, although their authors had different sources.

In the history of ideas, the line that opinion cannot be conquered by force also resonates with early modern defenses of freedom of conscience, and with political thinkers who argue that tyranny can control bodies but not minds. The counter-argument, that regimes do succeed in shaping minds by terror and propaganda, is a reminder that the claim is an ideal.

In relation to other chapters of the Discourses, this is among the longest and most varied. It contains in miniature several of Epictetus's favorite devices: the imagined opponent, the athletic comparison, the stage metaphor, the appeal to Socrates. It is therefore a good representative of his style.

Why read it now? Because the situation it addresses has not disappeared. People still face threats, from governments, employers and crowds, and they still ask what part of them is beyond reach. The chapter will not settle that for you, and you may end up agreeing with Pascal or Hegel against it. But it makes you decide what you would actually protect, and that is a question worth asking before you have to.
