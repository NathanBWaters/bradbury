The Interlopers

Saki (H. H. Munro) (1919) • 11 min read

“A Feud and Its End…”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Interlopers is among the most widely read short stories in the English-speaking world, partly because it is short and partly because its ending is so abrupt that readers remember it for the rest of their lives. It was written by Hector Hugh Munro, who published under the name Saki. Born in Burma in 1870 and raised in Devon by stern aunts, he became a journalist and wit in Edwardian London, known for comic stories that skewered polite society. In 1914 he enlisted in the army, though he was over forty, and in 1916 he was killed by a sniper in France. This story appeared in a collection published after his death, in 1919, which gives its theme an extra weight. It is about two men and a feud inherited from their grandfathers, set in a winter forest. Listen for the irony hidden in the title.

A Feud and Its End

In a forest of mixed growth somewhere on the eastern spurs of the Carpathians, a man stood one winter night watching and listening, as though he waited for some beast of the woods to come within the range of his vision, and, later, of his rifle. But the game for whose presence he kept so keen an outlook was none that figured in the sportsman’s calendar as lawful and proper for the chase; Ulrich von Gradwitz patrolled the dark forest in quest of a human enemy.

The forest lands of Gradwitz were of wide extent and well stocked with game; the narrow strip of precipitous woodland that lay on its outskirt was not remarkable for the game it harboured or the shooting it afforded, but it was the most jealously guarded of all its owner’s territorial possessions. A famous lawsuit, in the days of his grandfather, had wrested it from the illegal possession of a neighbouring family of petty landowners; the dispossessed party had never acquiesced in the judgment of the Courts, and a long series of poaching affrays and similar scandals had embittered the relationships between the families for three generations. The neighbour feud had grown into a personal one since Ulrich had come to be head of his family; if there was a man in the world whom he detested and wished ill to it was Georg Znaeym, the inheritor of the quarrel and the tireless game-snatcher and raider of the disputed border-forest. The feud might, perhaps, have died down or been compromised if the personal ill-will of the two men had not stood in the way; as boys they had thirsted for one another’s blood, as men each prayed that misfortune might fall on the other, and this wind-scourged winter night Ulrich had banded together his foresters to watch the dark forest, not in quest of four-footed quarry, but to keep a lookout for the prowling thieves whom he suspected of being afoot from across the land boundary. The roebuck, which usually kept in the sheltered hollows during a storm-wind, were running like driven things tonight, and there was movement and unrest among the creatures that were wont to sleep through the dark hours. Assuredly there was a disturbing element in the forest, and Ulrich could guess the quarter from whence it came.

He strayed away by himself from the watchers whom he had placed in ambush on the crest of the hill, and wandered far down the steep slopes amid the wild tangle of undergrowth, peering through the tree trunks and listening through the whistling and skirling of the wind and the restless beating of the branches for sight and sound of the marauders. If only on this wild night, in this dark, lone spot, he might come across Georg Znaeym, man to man, with none to witness⁠—that was the wish that was uppermost in his thoughts. And as he stepped round the trunk of a huge beech he came face to face with the man he sought.

The two enemies stood glaring at one another for a long silent moment. Each had a rifle in his hand, each had hate in his heart and murder uppermost in his mind. The chance had come to give full play to the passions of a lifetime. But a man who has been brought up under the code of a restraining civilisation cannot easily nerve himself to shoot down his neighbour in cold blood and without word spoken, except for an offence against his hearth and honour. And before the moment of hesitation had given way to action a deed of Nature’s own violence overwhelmed them both. A fierce shriek of the storm had been answered by a splitting crash over their heads, and ere they could leap aside a mass of falling beech tree had thundered down on them. Ulrich von Gradwitz found himself stretched on the ground, one arm numb beneath him and the other held almost as helplessly in a tight tangle of forked branches, while both legs were pinned beneath the fallen mass. His heavy shooting-boots had saved his feet from being crushed to pieces, but if his fractures were not as serious as they might have been, at least it was evident that he could not move from his present position till someone came to release him. The descending twig had slashed the skin of his face, and he had to wink away some drops of blood from his eyelashes before he could take in a general view of the disaster. At his side, so near that under ordinary circumstances he could almost have touched him, lay Georg Znaeym, alive and struggling, but obviously as helplessly pinioned down as himself. All round them lay a thick-strewn wreckage of splintered branches and broken twigs.

Relief at being alive and exasperation at his captive plight brought a strange medley of pious thank-offerings and sharp curses to Ulrich’s lips. Georg, who was nearly blinded with the blood which trickled across his eyes, stopped his struggling for a moment to listen, and then gave a short, snarling laugh.

“So you’re not killed, as you ought to be, but you’re caught, anyway,” he cried; “caught fast. Ho, what a jest, Ulrich von Gradwitz snared in his stolen forest. There’s real justice for you!”

And he laughed again, mockingly and savagely.

“I’m caught in my own forest-land,” retorted Ulrich. “When my men come to release us you will wish, perhaps, that you were in a better plight than caught poaching on a neighbour’s land, shame on you.”

Georg was silent for a moment; then he answered quietly:

“Are you sure that your men will find much to release? I have men, too, in the forest tonight, close behind me, and they will be here first and do the releasing. When they drag me out from under these damned branches it won’t need much clumsiness on their part to roll this mass of trunk right over on the top of you. Your men will find you dead under a fallen beech tree. For form’s sake I shall send my condolences to your family.”

“It is a useful hint,” said Ulrich fiercely. “My men had orders to follow in ten minutes time, seven of which must have gone by already, and when they get me out⁠—I will remember the hint. Only as you will have met your death poaching on my lands I don’t think I can decently send any message of condolence to your family.”

“Good,” snarled Georg, “good. We fight this quarrel out to the death, you and I and our foresters, with no cursed interlopers to come between us. Death and damnation to you, Ulrich von Gradwitz.”

“The same to you, Georg Znaeym, forest-thief, game-snatcher.”

Both men spoke with the bitterness of possible defeat before them, for each knew that it might be long before his men would seek him out or find him; it was a bare matter of chance which party would arrive first on the scene.

Both had now given up the useless struggle to free themselves from the mass of wood that held them down; Ulrich limited his endeavours to an effort to bring his one partially free arm near enough to his outer coat-pocket to draw out his wine-flask. Even when he had accomplished that operation it was long before he could manage the unscrewing of the stopper or get any of the liquid down his throat. But what a Heaven-sent draught it seemed! It was an open winter, and little snow had fallen as yet, hence the captives suffered less from the cold than might have been the case at that season of the year; nevertheless, the wine was warming and reviving to the wounded man, and he looked across with something like a throb of pity to where his enemy lay, just keeping the groans of pain and weariness from crossing his lips.

“Could you reach this flask if I threw it over to you?” asked Ulrich suddenly; “there is good wine in it, and one may as well be as comfortable as one can. Let us drink, even if tonight one of us dies.”

“No, I can scarcely see anything; there is so much blood caked round my eyes,” said Georg, “and in any case I don’t drink wine with an enemy.”

Ulrich was silent for a few minutes, and lay listening to the weary screeching of the wind. An idea was slowly forming and growing in his brain, an idea that gained strength every time that he looked across at the man who was fighting so grimly against pain and exhaustion. In the pain and languor that Ulrich himself was feeling the old fierce hatred seemed to be dying down.

“Neighbour,” he said presently, “do as you please if your men come first. It was a fair compact. But as for me, I’ve changed my mind. If my men are the first to come you shall be the first to be helped, as though you were my guest. We have quarrelled like devils all our lives over this stupid strip of forest, where the trees can’t even stand upright in a breath of wind. Lying here tonight thinking I’ve come to think we’ve been rather fools; there are better things in life than getting the better of a boundary dispute. Neighbour, if you will help me to bury the old quarrel I⁠—I will ask you to be my friend.”

Georg Znaeym was silent for so long that Ulrich thought, perhaps, he had fainted with the pain of his injuries. Then he spoke slowly and in jerks.

“How the whole region would stare and gabble if we rode into the market-square together. No one living can remember seeing a Znaeym and a von Gradwitz talking to one another in friendship. And what peace there would be among the forester folk if we ended our feud tonight. And if we choose to make peace among our people there is none other to interfere, no interlopers from outside⁠ ⁠… You would come and keep the Sylvester night beneath my roof, and I would come and feast on some high day at your castle⁠ ⁠… I would never fire a shot on your land, save when you invited me as a guest; and you should come and shoot with me down in the marshes where the wildfowl are. In all the countryside there are none that could hinder if we willed to make peace. I never thought to have wanted to do other than hate you all my life, but I think I have changed my mind about things too, this last half-hour. And you offered me your wine-flask⁠ ⁠… Ulrich von Gradwitz, I will be your friend.”

For a space both men were silent, turning over in their minds the wonderful changes that this dramatic reconciliation would bring about. In the cold, gloomy forest, with the wind tearing in fitful gusts through the naked branches and whistling round the tree trunks, they lay and waited for the help that would now bring release and succour to both parties. And each prayed a private prayer that his men might be the first to arrive, so that he might be the first to show honourable attention to the enemy that had become a friend.

Presently, as the wind dropped for a moment, Ulrich broke silence.

“Let’s shout for help,” he said, “in this lull our voices may carry a little way.”

“They won’t carry far through the trees and undergrowth,” said Georg, “but we can try. Together, then.”

The two raised their voices in a prolonged hunting call.

“Together again,” said Ulrich a few minutes later, after listening in vain for an answering halloo.

“I heard nothing but the pestilential wind,” said Georg hoarsely.

There was silence again for some minutes, and then Ulrich gave a joyful cry.

“I can see figures coming through the wood. They are following in the way I came down the hillside.”

Both men raised their voices in as loud a shout as they could muster.

“They hear us! They’ve stopped. Now they see us. They’re running down the hill towards us,” cried Ulrich.

“How many of them are there?” asked Georg.

“I can’t see distinctly,” said Ulrich; “nine or ten.”

“Then they are yours,” said Georg; “I had only seven out with me.”

“They are making all the speed they can, brave lads,” said Ulrich gladly.

“Are they your men?” asked Georg. “Are they your men?” he repeated impatiently as Ulrich did not answer.

“No,” said Ulrich with a laugh, the idiotic chattering laugh of a man unstrung with hideous fear.

“Who are they?” asked Georg quickly, straining his eyes to see what the other would gladly not have seen.

“Wolves.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story opens on a winter night in a forest on the eastern slopes of the Carpathian Mountains. A man named Ulrich von Gradwitz is out hunting, but not for game. He is looking for a human enemy. The prize in question is a narrow strip of steep woodland that is not especially valuable. Its owners have fought over it for three generations, ever since a famous lawsuit in the days of Ulrich's grandfather took it away from a neighboring family of petty landowners. They never accepted the court's decision. Over the years the dispute has produced poaching, brawls and scandals, and now it has become a personal hatred between Ulrich and Georg Znaeym, the heir to the other side of the quarrel.

On this stormy night, the two men meet. Each has a rifle, each has murder on his mind, and each stands in the dark, glaring at the other. But men who have been raised in a civilized society, Saki observes, cannot easily shoot a neighbor without a word. While they hesitate, a gust of wind splits a giant beech tree, and the trunk crashes down on both of them. Ulrich is pinned to the ground, one arm numb, the other tangled in branches, both legs trapped beneath the wood. Georg lies close beside him, half blinded by blood, and when he realizes his enemy is alive and helpless he laughs, savagely, and calls it a fine jest that Ulrich is snared in his stolen forest.

What follows is the real heart of the story. Helpless, they trade insults. Each tells the other that his own men will soon arrive, and that the other will then be sorry to be found in such a plight. But as the cold sets in, something changes. Ulrich manages to get out a flask of wine and offers it to Georg, saying that one may as well be comfortable even if one of them dies. Georg, softened, begins to speak about what it would mean to end the quarrel. He imagines riding into the market square together, as friends, with the whole region staring. He imagines how much peace there would be among the forest people if the feud ended, and says that no outsiders, no interlopers, could stop them from making peace if they chose.

They agree and shout for help. Ulrich sees figures coming through the trees, and the two men start to argue happily about whose men they are. Then Georg asks again, and Ulrich answers with a laugh that Saki calls idiotic and chattering, the laugh of a man unstrung with fear. The single word that ends the story is wolves.

The meaning lies in that last turn. The title refers to the men's belief that the outsiders, the interlopers, were the only ones who could interfere with their peace. The true interlopers are the wolves, representing a nature indifferent to human feuds and human reconciliation. Saki's point is not simply that hatred is foolish. It is that the right moment for reconciliation may not last, and that nature does not care how the story is meant to end.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Interlopers is a model of compression, and it is worth admiring on those grounds. In under two thousand words Saki establishes a long feud, a setting, two characters, a crisis, a change of heart and an ending that lands like a blow. The opening paragraphs do their work quickly, laying out the history of the feud in a few sentences and sketching the landscape in a handful of strokes. The reader is never lost and never bored.

The moral turning point is handled with unusual skill. The reconciliation is not sentimental. It grows out of pain, cold and fear, and each man moves a little at a time. Ulrich offers wine, Georg remarks on the kindness, and each admits he has been wrong in small steps. The dialogue is crisp and plausible, and Georg's description of what peace would look like, the stares in the market, the shared feasts, the shooting parties in the marshes, gives the future a warmth that makes its loss sharper.

The ending is the story's great trick, and its reputation depends on it. Saki keeps the reader leaning forward by building hope, then withdraws it in a single word. The last scene works in part because he does not explain it. There is no narrator's comment, no moral. The story simply stops, and silence does the rest. The dramatic irony is cruel, and effective.

Yet there are weaknesses. The whole plot depends on coincidences: the tree falls at the exact moment of confrontation, the two men are pinned within speaking distance and, at the critical moment, the wolves arrive from nowhere. Readers who like their stories to feel inevitable may find the ending too neat, too much a bolt from the blue. A modern audience, used to twist endings, may also see it coming. The characters are little more than types, the proud landowner and the poacher, and the wolves are a somewhat convenient symbol of nature's indifference. The prose, too, is less polished and witty than in Saki's best comic work. Compared with the glitter of stories such as Sredni Vashtar or The Open Window, this one is plainer and more fable-like. That plainness is part of its power, but it also makes it feel slightly mechanical on the page. All the same, it does what it sets out to do, and it does it with admirable economy.

It is also worth asking whether the story earns its irony or merely imposes it. The best defense is that Saki seeds the idea from the beginning: the forest is described as wild and dangerous, the storm is violent, and the men's talk of their own servants is built on an assumption that rescue will come in the shape they expect. The reader, like the characters, is invited to relax, and the final word punishes that relaxation.

III. The Impact It Had

Few short stories have been read by as many young people as this one. The Interlopers is included in school anthologies and examination syllabuses around the world, particularly in Britain, India and other countries with a British educational tradition. For many readers, it is the first short story whose ending they have really had to think about. Teachers use it to explain irony, foreshadowing, symbolism and the idea of the twist ending, and it often appears in discussions of how a short story can work through a single decisive image.

Its influence on later writing is mostly indirect, spread through the many writers who learned the craft of the sudden ending. Stories that end on a sharp, ironic turn, from O. Henry's to those of Roald Dahl and the television tradition of the twist, belong to the family of tales that Saki helped refine. The Interlopers is probably the clearest example of how a single last word can recast everything that came before.

The story also influenced how people talk about feuds. The idea that two sides in a long quarrel might be reconciled only when it is too late has become a common image in discussions of family quarrels, border disputes and political rivalries. It has often been cited in connection with the First World War, in which many men who might have been friends were taught to hate one another and died in the mud.

Whether or not that reading is fair, it has helped keep the story alive in an age that has seen more than enough of such waste. That connection was not in Saki's mind when he wrote the story, as far as we can tell, but it was added to it by history. The story was published after his death at the front, and many readers have found it impossible to read without thinking of the war.

In the end, its main impact has been educational and cultural. It has been reprinted in countless textbooks and read aloud in countless classrooms. It has served as a reference point for writers who wanted to show that a story can be small and still carry a large moral. Its impact lies not in any shift in literary history, but in the number of readers who have met it, felt its sting and remembered it.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers admire The Interlopers for its efficiency. It is the kind of story that can be read in a few minutes and remembered for decades. There is satisfaction in watching a tale about a century-old feud turn on a single wine flask, and in the way each speech nudges the men a step toward friendship.

Many love the emotional shape of the story. It begins with hatred, moves through pain and fear to understanding, and ends in an abrupt reversal. The reconciliation scene is the part readers cite most often, because it feels hard-won and humane. When Georg says that he has changed his mind and offers his friendship, the reader is moved, and the more moved they are, the more the ending hurts.

The setting is another pleasure. The dark winter forest, with the wind tearing through the bare branches, is described in a few strokes and gives the tale its fairy-tale atmosphere. It feels like a legend from the Carpathians, a story told by a fire. The sense of a lonely, wild place where human quarrels seem both enormous and tiny is a large part of its appeal.

Finally, readers value the story's moral clarity and its refusal to preach. It does not tell us what to think. It simply shows two men who waste a lifetime on hatred, learn better in an hour, and are denied the chance to use what they have learned. The result is a parable that feels fresh each time, and that has an emotional force out of proportion to its length. Many people say it taught them to see how silly some of their own quarrels were, and how important it is to make peace while there is still time. That quiet effect on readers' lives is perhaps its greatest tribute.

Readers also enjoy how well the story rewards a second look. Once you know the ending, details leap out: the word interlopers placed in Georg's mouth just before the wolves appear, the hope each man silently holds that his own servants will arrive first, so that he can be the first to show kindness to a former enemy. That detail is especially touching. It shows how fast a lifetime's hatred can turn into eagerness to be generous, and it makes the final reveal feel like a cruel joke played by the universe on two men who have only just grown decent. Many readers say they felt a physical drop in the stomach at the last word, which is perhaps the highest praise a short story can earn. They also appreciate that Saki trusts them to supply the horror that follows. We do not see the wolves reach the men. We do not need to.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Detailed reviews of this story from the time of its first publication are scarce, and the best-documented criticism comes from later readers and teachers. Saki's collections were generally praised for wit and cleverness, though some reviewers thought him a little cold. This story, which is warmer than most of his work, did not provoke much controversy.

The most common objection is that the ending is contrived. Critics argue that Saki manipulates events to deliver his message, bringing in the wolves as a deus ex machina, a sudden outside force that solves or ruins the plot. The coincidence of the falling tree and the arriving wolves, they say, makes the story feel like a lesson rather than a slice of life. Others note that the moral, that hatred is futile and reconciliation is precious, is so simple that it borders on cliche.

Some readers find the story too cynical. If the men are punished for their change of heart, they ask, does the story say that good intentions are pointless? Saki's ironic worldview, they argue, can slide into a shrug. Others argue the opposite, that the story is not cynical at all, since the men's reconciliation is real and moving even if brief. This disagreement about the ending's meaning is one of the reasons the story is so often debated in classrooms.

There are also complaints about thin characters and a dated setting. The two men are almost allegorical figures, and their feud is described in generalities. The aristocratic setting, with its landowners, foresters and game laws, may feel remote to modern readers. Some also feel the story is almost too familiar, having been read so often that its twist has lost its shock. Finally, a few critics have noted that the use of wolves as symbols of nature's cruelty is a conventional device, drawn from a long tradition of fairy tales. These are not damning charges, but they explain why some regard the story as a minor piece compared with Saki's best work, which is wittier and stranger.

A last objection concerns the lack of any real consequence for the men's earlier hatred. We are told that their feud has produced brawls and scandals, but we never see anyone suffer from it, so the loss of the friendship feels less devastating than it might. A more ambitious story might have shown a wife or a child on one side or the other, giving the reconciliation more to heal.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Where does The Interlopers fit in the larger story of literature? It belongs to the Edwardian short story, the tradition of sharp, concentrated tales in the years before the First World War, and more specifically to the ironic fable, a form that uses a small, simple plot to make a larger point. Its closest relatives are the twist-ending stories of Guy de Maupassant, Ambrose Bierce and O. Henry, in which a sudden reversal changes the meaning of everything.

It also draws on one of the oldest stories in the world: the feud between families that ends in tragedy. Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet tells of two houses whose hatred is healed only after the deaths of their children. Saki's version is smaller and colder, since there are no lovers, but the point is similar: the reconciliation comes too late. Another ancestor is the tradition of nature as an indifferent force, found in Thomas Hardy's novels and in the American naturalists. Jack London's To Build a Fire, in which a man is killed by cold and his own stubbornness, is a cousin in spirit. Saki, however, turns the lens on human pettiness, not on human endurance.

There is a further echo with tales of feud in folk ballads and the family sagas of Iceland, where long cycles of revenge over small plots of land are a major theme.

Its descendants include the many modern stories that use a last-line twist to deliver a moral, and the anti-war fables of the twentieth century. Read after 1914, the tale's picture of men who are taught to hate and who die before they can make peace has often been taken as a miniature of the war itself. Any later story in which two bitter enemies are forced to depend on each other, only to be overtaken by some larger danger, shares its basic logic.

Why read it today? Because it is brief and unforgettable, because it asks a question that never goes away, and because it demonstrates how much can be done in a short space. In a world in which long quarrels still divide families and nations, the story's picture of two men who discover their common humanity too late remains painfully relevant. It is not Saki's cleverest work, but it may be his most humane, and its closing word still lands with a chill.
