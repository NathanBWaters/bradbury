The Masque of the Red Death

Edgar Allan Poe (1842) • 12 min read

“The “Red Death” had long devastated the country.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Edgar Allan Poe published "The Masque of the Red Death" in 1842, in Graham's Magazine in Philadelphia, where he worked as an editor. The earliest version was titled "The Mask of the Red Death." It was a hard year for him. His young wife, Virginia, had begun coughing blood that January, the first sign of the tuberculosis that would kill her, and Poe wrote about disease and dying with the dread of someone watching it happen at home. In the story, a plague is killing the countryside while a rich prince invites a thousand friends into a sealed abbey to party until it passes. It is short, vivid and built like a dream. It is often read as a parable about death, and about the foolishness of thinking money, walls or pleasure can shut it out. Pay attention to the seven colored rooms and to the black clock, because they carry much of the meaning.

The “Red Death” had long devastated the country. No pestilence had ever been so fatal, or so hideous. Blood was its Avatar and its seal⁠—the redness and the horror of blood. There were sharp pains, and sudden dizziness, and then profuse bleeding at the pores, with dissolution. The scarlet stains upon the body and especially upon the face of the victim, were the pest ban which shut him out from the aid and from the sympathy of his fellow-men. And the whole seizure, progress and termination of the disease, were the incidents of half an hour.

But the Prince Prospero was happy and dauntless and sagacious. When his dominions were half depopulated, he summoned to his presence a thousand hale and lighthearted friends from among the knights and dames of his court, and with these retired to the deep seclusion of one of his castellated abbeys. This was an extensive and magnificent structure, the creation of the prince’s own eccentric yet august taste. A strong and lofty wall girdled it in. This wall had gates of iron. The courtiers, having entered, brought furnaces and massy hammers and welded the bolts. They resolved to leave means neither of ingress or egress to the sudden impulses of despair or of frenzy from within. The abbey was amply provisioned. With such precautions the courtiers might bid defiance to contagion. The external world could take care of itself. In the meantime it was folly to grieve, or to think. The prince had provided all the appliances of pleasure. There were buffoons, there were improvisatori, there were ballet-dancers, there were musicians, there was Beauty, there was wine. All these and security were within. Without was the “Red Death.”

It was toward the close of the fifth or sixth month of his seclusion, and while the pestilence raged most furiously abroad, that the Prince Prospero entertained his thousand friends at a masked ball of the most unusual magnificence.

It was a voluptuous scene, that masquerade. But first let me tell of the rooms in which it was held. There were seven⁠—an imperial suite. In many palaces, however, such suites form a long and straight vista, while the folding doors slide back nearly to the walls on either hand, so that the view of the whole extent is scarcely impeded. Here the case was very different; as might have been expected from the duke’s love of the bizarre. The apartments were so irregularly disposed that the vision embraced but little more than one at a time. There was a sharp turn at every twenty or thirty yards, and at each turn a novel effect. To the right and left, in the middle of each wall, a tall and narrow Gothic window looked out upon a closed corridor which pursued the windings of the suite. These windows were of stained glass whose color varied in accordance with the prevailing hue of the decorations of the chamber into which it opened. That at the eastern extremity was hung, for example, in blue⁠—and vividly blue were its windows. The second chamber was purple in its ornaments and tapestries, and here the panes were purple. The third was green throughout, and so were the casements. The fourth was furnished and lighted with orange⁠—the fifth with white⁠—the sixth with violet. The seventh apartment was closely shrouded in black velvet tapestries that hung all over the ceiling and down the walls, falling in heavy folds upon a carpet of the same material and hue. But in this chamber only, the color of the windows failed to correspond with the decorations. The panes here were scarlet⁠—a deep blood color. Now in no one of the seven apartments was there any lamp or candelabrum, amid the profusion of golden ornaments that lay scattered to and fro or depended from the roof. There was no light of any kind emanating from lamp or candle within the suite of chambers. But in the corridors that followed the suite, there stood, opposite to each window, a heavy tripod, bearing a brazier of fire that projected its rays through the tinted glass and so glaringly illumined the room. And thus were produced a multitude of gaudy and fantastic appearances. But in the western or black chamber the effect of the firelight that streamed upon the dark hangings through the blood-tinted panes, was ghastly in the extreme, and produced so wild a look upon the countenances of those who entered, that there were few of the company bold enough to set foot within its precincts at all.

It was in this apartment, also, that there stood against the western wall, a gigantic clock of ebony. Its pendulum swung to and fro with a dull, heavy, monotonous clang; and when the minute-hand made the circuit of the face, and the hour was to be stricken, there came from the brazen lungs of the clock a sound which was clear and loud and deep and exceedingly musical, but of so peculiar a note and emphasis that, at each lapse of an hour, the musicians of the orchestra were constrained to pause, momentarily, in their performance, to hearken to the sound; and thus the waltzers perforce ceased their evolutions; and there was a brief disconcert of the whole gay company; and, while the chimes of the clock yet rang, it was observed that the giddiest grew pale, and the more aged and sedate passed their hands over their brows as if in confused reverie or meditation. But when the echoes had fully ceased, a light laughter at once pervaded the assembly; the musicians looked at each other and smiled as if at their own nervousness and folly, and made whispering vows, each to the other, that the next chiming of the clock should produce in them no similar emotion; and then, after the lapse of sixty minutes (which embrace three thousand and six hundred seconds of the Time that flies), there came yet another chiming of the clock, and then were the same disconcert and tremulousness and meditation as before.

But, in spite of these things, it was a gay and magnificent revel. The tastes of the duke were peculiar. He had a fine eye for colors and effects. He disregarded the decora of mere fashion. His plans were bold and fiery, and his conceptions glowed with barbaric lustre. There are some who would have thought him mad. His followers felt that he was not. It was necessary to hear and see and touch him to be sure that he was not.

He had directed, in great part, the moveable embellishments of the seven chambers, upon occasion of this great fête; and it was his own guiding taste which had given character to the masqueraders. Be sure they were grotesque. There were much glare and glitter and piquancy and phantasm⁠—much of what has been since seen in Hernani. There were arabesque figures with unsuited limbs and appointments. There were delirious fancies such as the madman fashions. There was much of the beautiful, much of the wanton, much of the bizarre, something of the terrible, and not a little of that which might have excited disgust. To and fro in the seven chambers there stalked, in fact, a multitude of dreams. And these⁠—the dreams⁠—writhed in and about, taking hue from the rooms, and causing the wild music of the orchestra to seem as the echo of their steps. And, anon, there strikes the ebony clock which stands in the hall of the velvet. And then, for a moment, all is still, and all is silent save the voice of the clock. The dreams are stiff-frozen as they stand. But the echoes of the chime die away⁠—they have endured but an instant⁠—and a light, half-subdued laughter floats after them as they depart. And now again the music swells, and the dreams live, and writhe to and fro more merrily than ever, taking hue from the many-tinted windows through which stream the rays from the tripods. But to the chamber which lies most westwardly of the seven, there are now none of the maskers who venture; for the night is waning away; and there flows a ruddier light through the blood-colored panes; and the blackness of the sable drapery appals; and to him whose foot falls upon the sable carpet, there comes from the near clock of ebony a muffled peal more solemnly emphatic than any which reaches their ears who indulge in the more remote gaieties of the other apartments.

But these other apartments were densely crowded, and in them beat feverishly the heart of life. And the revel went whirlingly on, until at length there commenced the sounding of midnight upon the clock. And then the music ceased, as I have told; and the evolutions of the waltzers were quieted; and there was an uneasy cessation of all things as before. But now there were twelve strokes to be sounded by the bell of the clock; and thus it happened, perhaps, that more of thought crept, with more of time, into the meditations of the thoughtful among those who revelled. And thus, too, it happened, perhaps, that before the last echoes of the last chime had utterly sunk into silence, there were many individuals in the crowd who had found leisure to become aware of the presence of a masked figure which had arrested the attention of no single individual before. And the rumor of this new presence having spread itself whisperingly around, there arose at length from the whole company a buzz, or murmur, expressive of disapprobation and surprise⁠—then, finally, of terror, of horror, and of disgust.

In an assembly of phantasms such as I have painted, it may well be supposed that no ordinary appearance could have excited such sensation. In truth the masquerade license of the night was nearly unlimited; but the figure in question had out-Heroded Herod, and gone beyond the bounds of even the prince’s indefinite decorum. There are chords in the hearts of the most reckless which cannot be touched without emotion. Even with the utterly lost, to whom life and death are equally jests, there are matters of which no jest can be made. The whole company, indeed, seemed now deeply to feel that in the costume and bearing of the stranger neither wit nor propriety existed. The figure was tall and gaunt, and shrouded from head to foot in the habiliments of the grave. The mask which concealed the visage was made so nearly to resemble the countenance of a stiffened corpse that the closest scrutiny must have had difficulty in detecting the cheat. And yet all this might have been endured, if not approved, by the mad revellers around. But the mummer had gone so far as to assume the type of the Red Death. His vesture was dabbled in blood⁠—and his broad brow, with all the features of the face, was besprinkled with the scarlet horror.

When the eyes of Prince Prospero fell upon this spectral image (which with a slow and solemn movement, as if more fully to sustain its role, stalked to and fro among the waltzers) he was seen to be convulsed, in the first moment with a strong shudder either of terror or distaste; but, in the next, his brow reddened with rage.

“Who dares?” he demanded hoarsely of the courtiers who stood near him⁠—“who dares insult us with this blasphemous mockery? Seize him and unmask him⁠—that we may know whom we have to hang at sunrise, from the battlements!”

It was in the eastern or blue chamber in which stood the Prince Prospero as he uttered these words. They rang throughout the seven rooms loudly and clearly⁠—for the prince was a bold and robust man, and the music had become hushed at the waving of his hand.

It was in the blue room where stood the prince, with a group of pale courtiers by his side. At first, as he spoke, there was a slight rushing movement of this group in the direction of the intruder, who at the moment was also near at hand, and now, with deliberate and stately step, made closer approach to the speaker. But from a certain nameless awe with which the mad assumptions of the mummer had inspired the whole party, there were found none who put forth hand to seize him; so that, unimpeded, he passed within a yard of the prince’s person; and, while the vast assembly, as if with one impulse, shrank from the centres of the rooms to the walls, he made his way uninterruptedly, but with the same solemn and measured step which had distinguished him from the first, through the blue chamber to the purple⁠—through the purple to the green⁠—through the green to the orange⁠—through this again to the white⁠—and even thence to the violet, ere a decided movement had been made to arrest him. It was then, however, that the Prince Prospero, maddening with rage and the shame of his own momentary cowardice, rushed hurriedly through the six chambers, while none followed him on account of a deadly terror that had seized upon all. He bore aloft a drawn dagger, and had approached, in rapid impetuosity, to within three or four feet of the retreating figure, when the latter, having attained the extremity of the velvet apartment, turned suddenly and confronted his pursuer. There was a sharp cry⁠—and the dagger dropped gleaming upon the sable carpet, upon which, instantly afterwards, fell prostrate in death the Prince Prospero. Then, summoning the wild courage of despair, a throng of the revellers at once threw themselves into the black apartment, and, seizing the mummer, whose tall figure stood erect and motionless within the shadow of the ebony clock, gasped in unutterable horror at finding the grave-cerements and corpse-like mask which they handled with so violent a rudeness, untenanted by any tangible form.

And now was acknowledged the presence of the Red Death. He had come like a thief in the night. And one by one dropped the revellers in the blood-bedewed halls of their revel, and died each in the despairing posture of his fall. And the life of the ebony clock went out with that of the last of the gay. And the flames of the tripods expired. And Darkness and Decay and the Red Death held illimitable dominion over all.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The surface of the story is easy to follow. A disease called the Red Death is sweeping the land. It kills quickly and horribly, with sharp pain, dizziness and then bleeding through the pores, and the victim is dead within half an hour. Prince Prospero, who is described as happy and fearless, responds by gathering a thousand healthy friends and shutting them inside a fortified abbey. The gates are welded shut. Inside, there is food, wine, musicians, entertainers and every pleasure, while outside, as the narrator puts it, the Red Death is allowed to rage. After five or six months, the prince holds a masked ball in a suite of seven rooms.

Those rooms are the center of the story's symbolism. They are arranged in a winding row, so that no one can see more than one at a time. Each is a different color, blue, purple, green, orange, white and violet, and the seventh is black with windows of deep blood red. Beyond the black room stands an ebony clock whose chime is so loud and strange that the orchestra stops and the dancers go pale each time it strikes. Many readers see the seven rooms as the stages of life, from the blue of birth toward the black of death, with the sun setting along the way. The clock is time itself, and its interruptions remind the revelers that their pleasure is borrowed. The fact that the westernmost room is black, the color of sunset and of death, helps this reading, and the fact that few guests dare to enter it shows how much they avoid the thought of their end.

At midnight a new figure appears among the guests, a tall, gaunt mask wrapped in the cerements of the grave, its face made to look like the face of a corpse, and spattered with blood. This is the Red Death in person, or someone dressed as it. It was not stopped at the gate, because the guests are too busy to look. Prospero is furious and orders it seized, but the courtiers shrink back, and the prince chases the figure through all seven rooms, dagger drawn, until he reaches the black one. The figure turns, the prince falls dead, and when the guests dare to seize the mummer, they find nothing underneath the costume. One by one they die.

The key point is that the horror comes from inside the walls. Prospero thinks that wealth and fortification can keep death out, but the story says the opposite: death was always already present, in the guests and in time itself. The strange line that the Red Death had come "like a thief in the night" echoes the Bible, and the final sentence gives a grand, almost liturgical finish, with darkness and decay and the Red Death holding "illimitable dominion over all."

A less obvious layer is that Prospero himself behaves with a kind of madness. His rooms are bizarre and his taste is wild, and the narrator says that some people thought him mad, though his followers did not. His response to catastrophe is an act of staged denial. The masquerade, a party in which everyone hides behind a face, is the perfect symbol for a society that will not look at what is happening to it. That is why the story still lands. It is not simply about a plague. It is about the human habit of dressing up our fears and calling it a celebration.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As an exercise in atmosphere, "The Masque of the Red Death" is one of Poe's most successful pieces. It is short, and that is a virtue. There is almost no plot beyond a party and an intruder, and no characters in the usual sense. Prospero and his guests are types, almost figures on a tapestry. The story depends instead on a series of strong images, and Poe arranges them with great care. The sequence of rooms, the colored light, the throbbing of the clock and the appearance of the masked figure all build a feeling of dread that is nearly physical. You can almost hear the clock.

The prose is a major part of the effect. Poe's sentences are long and ceremonious, with a slightly archaic grandeur, and they suit a story that wants to feel like a legend. The description of the black chamber, with its scarlet panes and its lack of any lamp or candle, makes the room feel like a nightmare. The clock's chime, which makes even the most reckless dancers stop and then recover with nervous laughter, is a beautiful dramatic device. It allows Poe to say something about time and death without explaining anything. When he later writes that the life of the clock went out with that of the last of the revelers, the image closes the circle neatly.

The weaknesses are also clear. Because the characters are symbols, the story has little emotional depth. We never come to know or care about a single person in the abbey, so the deaths at the end are impressive but not moving. You are meant to feel awe rather than grief, and some readers find the result cold. The allegory is also very schematic. The seven rooms, the clock, the mummer and the prince line up so tidily with ideas about life and mortality that the story can feel like a puzzle with one right answer. Good fiction usually leaves more room to wonder.

There is a related problem with the ending. The unmasking, in which the figure turns out to have no body at all, is a striking moment, but it is also somewhat theatrical. Poe's habit of ending with a mighty chord can shade into melodrama, and the final sentence is a good example. It is magnificent, but it is also a little too much like a curtain line. A reader in a skeptical mood may feel manipulated.

Finally, some of the story's rhetoric has aged. The overripe vocabulary, with its "gorgeous" and "bizarre" and "wild," can read as decoration rather than description. Poe also tells us a great deal about the prince's taste but little about what the guests feel. Even so, the story holds together because the logic of the dream is consistent. Few writers have matched the cold perfection of its structure. It may not move you the way a story about a single human life can, but as a piece of design, it is hard to fault.

III. The Impact It Had

The story has been one of the most influential pieces of horror writing in English, even though it is slight. It helped establish the image of the doomed party, where a group of privileged people tries to enjoy itself while catastrophe waits outside or within. That image has been reused for generations in novels, plays, films and games. The idea that wealth cannot buy safety, and that a luxury shelter may become a tomb, is now such a familiar pattern that it is easy to forget it has a source.

It also became a landmark of the plague story. Writers had told tales of epidemics before, notably Boccaccio in the Decameron, where a group of young people flee plague to tell stories in the countryside, and Daniel Defoe in his account of the plague year in London. Poe took the same situation, an escape from disease, and turned it into a fable of fate. Later readers have returned to it whenever epidemics enter the news. During outbreaks of cholera and influenza in earlier eras, and more recently during the global pandemic of 2020, the story was widely recirculated, because its picture of the powerful trying to wall themselves off from a public health crisis felt uncomfortably current.

The film world adopted it quickly. Roger Corman made a well-known 1964 film version starring Vincent Price as Prospero, which turned the prince into a sinister, sadistic aristocrat and added a good deal of invented plot. It is a landmark of low-budget gothic cinema. The figure in the red robe also turns up, directly or indirectly, in many other films and in other media, including music, painting and costume. Ingmar Bergman's grim, allegorical film about death walking among the living is a different work, but it belongs to the same large family of death-as-a-visitor stories that Poe's tale made vivid.

The story also matters to the teaching of literature. Because it is short and symbolically rich, it is a favorite for introducing students to allegory. Teachers use it to explain how a setting can stand for a larger idea, and to show that a tale can be read both as a plain narrative and as a layered symbolic puzzle. It has generated a large amount of commentary, including scholarly readings that connect it to Poe's own life, to the 1832 cholera epidemic that reached America, and to ideas about the body and sickness.

On Poe's career, its effect was to reinforce his reputation as a master of mood. The year 1842 was important for him, and this story shows the strength of his approach. It does not tell a complicated tale. It builds a single, overwhelming feeling and leaves it hanging in the air. Many writers since, from the early Lovecraft to modern gothic novelists, have learned from the way Poe lets an image, rather than an argument, do the main work.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved most is the sheer visual power of the story. Few short tales are so easy to picture. The seven rooms, glowing in their different colors, are a gift to anyone with an imagination. The black room with its blood-colored windows sticks in the memory, as does the ebony clock, whose sound is like a bell tolling for the hour of the end. Readers often say they can see the story as a film in their heads, and filmmakers and illustrators have felt the same.

They love, too, the way it gives shape to an old fear. Almost everyone has some version of the thought that if we are rich enough, careful enough or busy enough, we can keep death away. Poe stages that idea as a party and then ends it with a single arrival. The simplicity of the argument makes it satisfying. The reader does not need to be an expert in anything to feel the force of the final image, in which the crowd of dancers falls one by one in the rooms where they danced.

Many admire its tone, which mixes the gorgeous and the horrible. There is real beauty in the descriptions, and the beauty makes the dread worse. Poe does not warn us that something terrible is coming in any obvious way, but the music and light carry a sense of strain. Readers who enjoy atmosphere over action tend to rank this story near the top of Poe's work. It is the kind of piece that can be read aloud, and it sounds wonderful, with a rolling, bell-like rhythm in its sentences.

Fellow writers have also valued the story as a model of compression. In a few pages, Poe sets up a world, a threat, a symbol system and a climax, and he does it without a single wasted scene. That economy matters to short story writers who want to learn how to make a small space feel big. The tale proves that a story can be little more than an image sequence and still feel complete.

Finally, there is its emotional undertone. Although the characters are symbols, many readers sense the author's own fear behind the story. Poe knew illness and bereavement intimately, having lost his mother and his foster mother and now watching his wife decline. The story does not mention any of that, but the dread of someone who knows that death cannot be locked out runs through it. Some readers find in it a strange kind of comfort too, since it suggests that everyone, rich or poor, shares the same ending. It is bleak, but it is honest, and that honesty has kept it alive.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Not everyone has been convinced by the story. One of the oldest complaints is that it is more of a tableau than a tale. The characters are shadows, the plot is thin, and the action is mostly a procession. A reader who wants conflict, change or psychological depth may feel that nothing really happens until the final page, and that what happens then is predictable. The reaction of some critics has been that the story is a beautiful trick rather than a deep piece of storytelling.

The allegory has drawn its own objections. Because the story is so schematic, some critics feel it reduces death to a symbol and plays it for effect, without grappling with the real pain of an epidemic. Poe never shows us a single sufferer outside the abbey. The people dying in the countryside are only a backdrop, and the story's only concern is the fate of the rich. Modern readers sometimes point out that the story treats the disease as a moral lesson for the privileged rather than as a human disaster for everyone, and that the poor are simply erased.

There is also a long debate over whether the story should be read as having a single meaning at all. Scholars have proposed many interpretations. Some see it as a religious parable about divine judgment, others as a reflection of Poe's fear for his wife, and others as a political fable about aristocratic indifference. Still others read it as a psychological story about the mind's attempt to shut out unpleasant truths. Critics have complained that the story invites such a pile of readings that none can be proven, and that Poe was more interested in making a cool pattern than in saying something specific. Admirers reply that the openness is its strength.

Some have objected to the prose. Poe's gothic vocabulary, filled with words like "fantastic," "grotesque" and "arabesque," can feel overdone, and the descriptions of the revelers' costumes sometimes slip into list-making. Early readers who preferred plainer styles found Poe affected, and that view has persisted among critics who prefer the clean prose of later realism.

Finally, there is a more general critique of Poe's reputation. Some have argued that he is overrated as a thinker and valued mainly for mood. In that view, this story is his best example, an excellent piece of decoration and no more. The counterargument is that mood is itself a form of meaning, and that writing capable of producing a lasting dread in the reader is a real achievement. The truth is probably that the tale is limited and nearly perfect within its limits.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Poe's story belongs to American Dark Romanticism, the movement in which writers like Hawthorne, Melville and Poe looked at the darker side of the human mind at a time when many Americans were celebrating progress. Where the English Romantics often found comfort in nature, the dark Romantics found mystery, guilt and decay. Poe's contribution was to make the Gothic story of crumbling castles and ghostly figures into something compact and psychological. This tale also draws on the older European tradition of the dance of death, a medieval and Renaissance image in which a skeletal figure leads rich and poor alike to the grave, a reminder that rank means nothing in the end.

It is also a descendant of allegory, the old form in which each character or object stands for an idea. You can find that approach in John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, and in medieval morality plays. Poe, though, hides the allegory beneath the surface of a Gothic tale, so that it works even if you do not notice the symbols. That blend of the symbolic and the sensational is part of what makes him distinctive. It looks forward to later symbolist writers and painters, who prized suggestion over statement, and the French poets of the nineteenth century, such as Baudelaire, who translated Poe, were deeply moved by this quality.

Among related works, it is helpful to compare it with Poe's own "The Fall of the House of Usher," which also ends with a collapse of a doomed house, and with "The Cask of Amontillado," another carnival story in which a masked party hides a death. Beyond Poe, it recalls Mary Shelley's apocalyptic novel The Last Man, about a plague that ends humanity, and, later, H. G. Wells and Albert Camus, whose novel The Plague uses an epidemic as a way of studying how people behave under threat. Poe's version is far shorter, and more like a fable, but the shared question is how human beings cope with something they cannot defeat.

In modern times, its descendants include the many stories of elite shelters, from science fiction about luxury bunkers to horror films about parties gone wrong. Whenever a story shows wealthy people sealing themselves off from a danger that eventually finds them, it is walking in Prospero's footsteps.

Why read it today? One reason is that it takes about fifteen minutes, and the experience is lasting. Another is that its central image has become more relevant, not less. We live in a world in which the powerful sometimes imagine they can buy distance from shared problems, and in which entertainment can be a way of avoiding the news. Poe did not offer a solution, but he offered a picture, and the picture has not faded. Read it once for the shiver, and again for what it says about the parties we throw to forget what is waiting.
