Nightly Short Story • Philosophical Parable

God Sees the Truth, but Waits

Leo Tolstoy (1872) • 15 min read

“In the town of Vladímir lived a young merchant named Iván Dmítritch Aksyónof. He had two shops and a house of his own.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Leo Tolstoy published this short tale in 1872, about a decade before the spiritual crisis that changed his life, and years before he became known as a preacher of forgiveness and nonviolence. At the time he was teaching peasant children on his estate and compiling readers and primers for them, and he wanted stories that were simple enough for a village child and deep enough for anyone. The title is a Russian proverb, and the plot is borrowed from the kind of folk tale people told in villages. It follows a cheerful young merchant who is wrongly convicted of murder and sent to Siberia for decades. What makes it more than a miscarriage of justice story is the question it asks: what do you do with a truth that could free you, or avenge you, but only at someone else's cost? Pay attention to what Aksyonov does in the prison, and to what he does not do.

In the town of Vladímir lived a young merchant named Iván Dmítritch Aksyónof. He had two shops and a house of his own.

Aksyónof was a handsome, fair-haired, curly-headed fellow, full of fun, and very fond of singing. When quite a young man he had been given to drink, and was riotous when he had had too much; but after he married he gave up drinking, except now and then.

One summer Aksyónof was going to the Nízhny Fair, and as he bade goodbye to his family his wife said to him, “Iván Dmítritch, do not start today; I have had a bad dream about you.”

Aksyónof laughed, and said, “You are afraid that when I get to the fair I shall go on the spree.”

His wife replied: “I do not know what I am afraid of; all I know is that I had a bad dream. I dreamt you returned from the town, and when you took off your cap I saw that your hair was quite grey.”

Aksyónof laughed. “That’s a lucky sign,” said he. “See if I don’t sell out all my goods, and bring you some presents from the fair.”

So he said goodbye to his family, and drove away.

When he had travelled halfway, he met a merchant whom he knew, and they put up at the same inn for the night. They had some tea together, and then went to bed in adjoining rooms.

It was not Aksyónof’s habit to sleep late, and, wishing to travel while it was still cool, he aroused his driver before dawn, and told him to put in the horses.

Then he made his way across to the landlord of the inn (who lived in a cottage at the back), paid his bill, and continued his journey.

When he had gone about twenty-five miles, he stopped for the horses to be fed. Aksyónof rested awhile in the passage of the inn, then he stepped out into the porch, and, ordering a samovar to be heated, got out his guitar and began to play.

Suddenly a troika drove up with tinkling bells, and an official alighted, followed by two soldiers. He came to Aksyónof and began to question him, asking him who he was and whence he came. Aksyónof answered him fully, and said, “Won’t you have some tea with me?” But the official went on cross-questioning him and asking him, “Where did you spend last night? Were you alone, or with a fellow-merchant? Did you see the other merchant this morning? Why did you leave the inn before dawn?”

Aksyónof wondered why he was asked all these questions, but he described all that had happened, and then added, “Why do you cross-question me as if I were a thief or a robber? I am travelling on business of my own, and there is no need to question me.”

Then the official, calling the soldiers, said, “I am the police-officer of this district, and I question you because the merchant with whom you spent last night has been found with his throat cut. We must search your things.”

They entered the house. The soldiers and the police-officer unstrapped Aksyónof’s luggage and searched it. Suddenly the officer drew a knife out of a bag, crying, “Whose knife is this?”

Aksyónof looked, and seeing a bloodstained knife taken from his bag, he was frightened.

“How is it there is blood on this knife?”

Aksyónof tried to answer, but could hardly utter a word, and only stammered: “I⁠—I don’t know⁠—not mine.”

Then the police-officer said, “This morning the merchant was found in bed with his throat cut. You are the only person who could have done it. The house was locked from inside, and no one else was there. Here is this bloodstained knife in your bag, and your face and manner betray you! Tell me how you killed him, and how much money you stole?”

Aksyónof swore he had not done it; that he had not seen the merchant after they had had tea together; that he had no money except eight thousand roubles of his own, and that the knife was not his. But his voice was broken, his face pale, and he trembled with fear as though he were guilty.

The police-officer ordered the soldiers to bind Aksyónof and to put him in the cart. As they tied his feet together and flung him into the cart, Aksyónof crossed himself and wept. His money and goods were taken from him, and he was sent to the nearest town and imprisoned there. Enquiries as to his character were made in Vladímir. The merchants and other inhabitants of that town said that in former days he used to drink and waste his time, but that he was a good man. Then the trial came on: he was charged with murdering a merchant from Ryazán, and robbing him of twenty thousand roubles.

His wife was in despair, and did not know what to believe. Her children were all quite small; one was a baby at her breast. Taking them all with her, she went to the town where her husband was in gaol. At first she was not allowed to see him; but, after much begging, she obtained permission from the officials, and was taken to him. When she saw her husband in prison-dress and in chains, shut up with thieves and criminals, she fell down, and did not come to her senses for a long time. Then she drew her children to her, and sat down near him. She told him of things at home, and asked about what had happened to him. He told her all, and she asked, “What can we do now?”

“We must petition the Tsar not to let an innocent man perish.”

His wife told him that she had sent a petition to the Tsar, but that it had not been accepted.

Aksyónof did not reply, but only looked downcast.

Then his wife said, “It was not for nothing I dreamt your hair had turned grey. You remember? You should not have started that day.” And passing her fingers through his hair, she said: “Ványa dearest, tell your wife the truth; was it not you who did it?”

“So you, too, suspect me!” said Aksyónof, and, hiding his face in his hands, he began to weep. Then a soldier came to say that the wife and children must go away; and Aksyónof said goodbye to his family for the last time.

When they were gone, Aksyónof recalled what had been said, and when he remembered that his wife also had suspected him, he said to himself, “It seems that only God can know the truth; it is to Him alone we must appeal, and from Him alone expect mercy.”

And Aksyónof wrote no more petitions; gave up all hope, and only prayed to God.

Aksyónof was condemned to be flogged and sent to the mines. So he was flogged with a knout, and when the wounds made by the knout were healed, he was driven to Siberia with other convicts.

For twenty-six years Aksyónof lived as a convict in Siberia. His hair turned white as snow, and his beard grew long, thin, and grey. All his mirth went; he stooped; he walked slowly, spoke little, and never laughed, but he often prayed.

In prison Aksyónof learnt to make boots, and earned a little money, with which he bought The Lives of the Saints. He read this book when there was light enough in the prison; and on Sundays in the prison-church he read the lessons and sang in the choir; for his voice was still good.

The prison authorities liked Aksyónof for his meekness, and his fellow-prisoners respected him: they called him “Grandfather,” and “The Saint.” When they wanted to petition the prison authorities about anything, they always made Aksyónof their spokesman, and when there were quarrels among the prisoners they came to him to put things right, and to judge the matter.

No news reached Aksyónof from his home, and he did not even know if his wife and children were still alive.

One day a fresh gang of convicts came to the prison. In the evening the old prisoners collected round the new ones and asked them what towns or villages they came from, and what they were sentenced for. Among the rest Aksyónof sat down near the newcomers, and listened with downcast air to what was said.

One of the new convicts, a tall, strong man of sixty, with a closely-cropped grey beard, was telling the others what he had been arrested for.

“Well, friends,” he said, “I only took a horse that was tied to a sledge, and I was arrested and accused of stealing. I said I had only taken it to get home quicker, and had then let it go; besides, the driver was a personal friend of mine. So I said, ‘It’s all right.’ ‘No,’ said they, ‘you stole it.’ But how or where I stole it they could not say. I once really did something wrong, and ought by rights to have come here long ago, but that time I was not found out. Now I have been sent here for nothing at all.⁠ ⁠… Eh, but it’s lies I’m telling you; I’ve been to Siberia before, but I did not stay long.”

“Where are you from?” asked someone.

“From Vladímir. My family are of that town. My name is Makár, and they also call me Semyónitch.”

Aksyónof raised his head and said: “Tell me, Semyónitch, do you know anything of the merchants Aksyónof, of Vladímir? Are they still alive?”

“Know them? Of course I do. The Aksyónofs are rich, though their father is in Siberia: a sinner like ourselves, it seems! As for you, Gran’dad, how did you come here?”

Aksyónof did not like to speak of his misfortune. He only sighed, and said, “For my sins I have been in prison these twenty-six years.”

“What sins?” asked Makár Semyónitch.

But Aksyónof only said, “Well, well⁠—I must have deserved it!” He would have said no more, but his companions told the newcomer how Aksyónof came to be in Siberia: how someone had killed a merchant, and had put a knife among Aksyónof’s things, and Aksyónof had been unjustly condemned.

When Makár Semyónitch heard this, he looked at Aksyónof, slapped his own knee, and exclaimed, “Well, this is wonderful! Really wonderful! But how old you’ve grown, Gran’dad!”

The others asked him why he was so surprised, and where he had seen Aksyónof before; but Makár Semyónitch did not reply. He only said: “It’s wonderful that we should meet here, lads!”

These words made Aksyónof wonder whether this man knew who had killed the merchant; so he said, “Perhaps, Semyónitch, you have heard of that affair, or maybe you’ve seen me before?”

“How could I help hearing? The world’s full of rumours. But it’s long ago, and I’ve forgotten what I heard.”

“Perhaps you heard who killed the merchant?” asked Aksyónof.

Makár Semyónitch laughed, and replied, “It must have been him in whose bag the knife was found! If someone else hid the knife there, ‘He’s not a thief till he’s caught,’ as the saying is. How could anyone put a knife into your bag while it was under your head? It would surely have woke you up?”

When Aksyónof heard these words, he felt sure this was the man who had killed the merchant. He rose and went away. All that night Aksyónof lay awake. He felt terribly unhappy, and all sorts of images rose in his mind. There was the image of his wife as she was when he parted from her to go to the fair. He saw her as if she were present; her face and her eyes rose before him; he heard her speak and laugh. Then he saw his children, quite little, as they were at that time: one with a little cloak on, another at his mother’s breast. And then he remembered himself as he used to be⁠—young and merry. He remembered how he sat playing the guitar in the porch of the inn where he was arrested, and how free from care he had been. He saw, in his mind, the place where he was flogged, the executioner, and the people standing around; the chains, the convicts, all the twenty-six years of his prison life, and his premature old age. The thought of it all made him so wretched that he was ready to kill himself.

“And it’s all that villain’s doing!” thought Aksyónof. And his anger was so great against Makár Semyónitch that he longed for vengeance, even if he himself should perish for it. He kept repeating prayers all night, but could get no peace. During the day he did not go near Makár Semyónitch, nor even look at him.

A fortnight passed in this way. Aksyónof could not sleep at nights, and was so miserable that he did not know what to do.

One night as he was walking about the prison he noticed some earth that came rolling out from under one of the shelves on which the prisoners slept. He stopped to see what it was. Suddenly Makár Semyónitch crept out from under the shelf, and looked up at Aksyónof with frightened face. Aksyónof tried to pass without looking at him, but Makár seized his hand and told him that he had dug a hole under the wall, getting rid of the earth by putting it into his high-boots, and emptying it out every day on the road when the prisoners were driven to their work.

“Just you keep quiet, old man, and you shall get out too. If you blab they’ll flog the life out of me, but I will kill you first.”

Aksyónof trembled with anger as he looked at his enemy. He drew his hand away, saying, “I have no wish to escape, and you have no need to kill me; you killed me long ago! As to telling of you⁠—I may do so or not, as God shall direct.”

Next day, when the convicts were led out to work, the convoy soldiers noticed that one or other of the prisoners emptied some earth out of his boots. The prison was searched, and the tunnel found. The Governor came and questioned all the prisoners to find out who had dug the hole. They all denied any knowledge of it. Those who knew, would not betray Makár Semyónitch, knowing he would be flogged almost to death. At last the Governor turned to Aksyónof, whom he knew to be a just man, and said:

“You are a truthful old man; tell me, before God, who dug the hole?”

Makár Semyónitch stood as if he were quite unconcerned, looking at the Governor and not so much as glancing at Aksyónof. Aksyónof’s lips and hands trembled, and for a long time he could not utter a word. He thought, “Why should I screen him who ruined my life? Let him pay for what I have suffered. But if I tell, they will probably flog the life out of him, and maybe I suspect him wrongly. And, after all, what good would it be to me?”

“Well, old man,” repeated the Governor, “tell us the truth: who has been digging under the wall?”

Aksyónof glanced at Makár Semyónitch, and said, “I cannot say, your honour. It is not God’s will that I should tell! Do what you like with me; I am in your hands.”

However much the Governor tried, Aksyónof would say no more, and so the matter had to be left.

That night, when Aksyónof was lying on his bed and just beginning to doze, someone came quietly and sat down on his bed. He peered through the darkness and recognized Makár.

“What more do you want of me?” asked Aksyónof. “Why have you come here?”

Makár Semyónitch was silent. So Aksyónof sat up and said, “What do you want? Go away, or I will call the guard!”

Makár Semyónitch bent close over Aksyónof, and whispered, “Iván Dmítritch, forgive me!”

“What for?” asked Aksyónof.

“It was I who killed the merchant and hid the knife among your things. I meant to kill you too, but I heard a noise outside; so I hid the knife in your bag and escaped out of the window.”

Aksyónof was silent, and did not know what to say. Makár Semyónitch slid off the bed-shelf and knelt upon the ground. “Iván Dmítritch,” said he, “forgive me! For the love of God, forgive me! I will confess that it was I who killed the merchant, and you will be released and can go to your home.”

“It is easy for you to talk,” said Aksyónof, “but I have suffered for you these twenty-six years. Where could I go to now?⁠ ⁠… My wife is dead, and my children have forgotten me. I have nowhere to go.⁠ ⁠…”

Makár Semyónitch did not rise, but beat his head on the floor. “Iván Dmítritch, forgive me!” he cried. “When they flogged me with the knout it was not so hard to bear as it is to see you now⁠ ⁠… yet you had pity on me, and did not tell. For Christ’s sake forgive me, wretch that I am!” And he began to sob.

When Aksyónof heard him sobbing he, too, began to weep.

“God will forgive you!” said he. “Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you.” And at these words his heart grew light, and the longing for home left him. He no longer had any desire to leave the prison, but only hoped for his last hour to come.

In spite of what Aksyónof had said, Makár Semyónitch confessed his guilt. But when the order for his release came, Aksyónof was already dead.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Ivan Dmitritch Aksyonov is a young merchant in the town of Vladimir, handsome, fair, fond of singing, and happily married. He is setting out for a trade fair when his wife begs him not to go, because she has dreamed that when he came back his hair had turned gray. He laughs it off. On the road he shares an inn with another merchant, leaves before dawn, and plays his guitar at the next stop. Then police arrive. The other merchant has been found with his throat cut, and a bloody knife turns up in Aksyonov's bag. He is frightened and tongue-tied, which makes him look guilty, and he is flogged and sent to Siberia. The wife's dream has come true in a way no one could have imagined.

The most painful moment is not in the courtroom but in the prison, when his wife visits with the children. She has petitioned the Tsar without result. Then she runs her fingers through his hair, remembers her dream, and asks, "Vanya dearest, tell your wife the truth; was it not you who did it?" He answers, "So you, too, suspect me!" and weeps. That is the real injury, since the loss of his family's trust takes more from him than the loss of his freedom. After that scene he stops petitioning and gives himself to prayer, deciding that only God can know the truth.

Twenty-six years pass. Aksyonov's hair is white, his laughter is gone, and he has become the prison's quiet saint, called Grandfather by the other convicts, who bring him their quarrels. He makes boots and reads the lives of the saints. Then a new prisoner arrives, Makar Semyonitch, who is from Vladimir and who reacts oddly when he hears Aksyonov's story. Aksyonov begins to suspect that this man is the real killer. When the guards discover that Makar has been digging a tunnel under the wall, the governor asks Aksyonov, the most trusted man in the prison, who did it. Aksyonov could have taken his revenge by telling. Instead he says, "It is not God's will that I should tell.

That night Makar kneels and confesses. He did kill the merchant, and he had planned to kill Aksyonov too, but a noise outside forced him to flee. He says the flogging he later suffered was nothing compared with seeing Aksyonov now, and that Aksyonov's mercy has crushed him. Aksyonov weeps with him, says God will forgive him, and adds that maybe he himself is a hundred times worse. In that moment his longing for home leaves him. Makar confesses publicly, and the order for Aksyonov's release arrives, but Aksyonov is already dead.

The title, God sees the truth, but waits, is a comfort and a rebuke at once. It promises that truth exists and is known, but it also says that truth is slow and does not owe us a happy ending. The story's meaning is what it does with that waiting. Aksyonov is not rewarded by freedom. He is freed in a different sense, from bitterness, which the story presents as the only release that was ever really available to him.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a story of remarkable compression. In less than three thousand words Tolstoy gives us a whole life, its sudden fall, a quarter century of prison, and a final transformation. The first part moves like a trap closing. Every small, ordinary decision, leaving early, playing a guitar, answering questions honestly, helps convict him, and the frightening thing is how reasonable the police officer's logic is. The door was locked from inside, no one else was there, and the knife was in his bag. Tolstoy shows how innocence can look like guilt when a man is afraid.

The prison section is also superbly handled. Notice how Tolstoy avoids melodrama. There are no scenes of torture beyond a single sentence about the knout, no long speeches about injustice. We see Aksyonov change through small things, boots, a book, a voice still good enough for the choir, and his nickname. The conversation in which Makar casually tells his own story and then reacts strangely to Aksyonov's is a lovely piece of quiet suspense, since we can almost watch Makar's face change.

The turning point, the governor's question, works because it is so undramatized. Aksyonov's thoughts are given to us in two sides. He wonders why he should protect the man who ruined his life, and then he wonders whether he is mistaken, and what good revenge would do. He does not become suddenly noble. The decision is murky, which makes it believable.

There are weaknesses. The plot depends on a huge coincidence, that the real killer should be sent to the very same prison, decades later, and should happen to be a man from the same town. Folk tales run on this kind of fate, but a skeptical modern reader may feel the author's hand pushing the pieces together. The moral is also stated rather plainly, and the final line, that Aksyonov was already dead when the order came, has a bleak tidiness that some find too neat. And Makar's confession, with its quick repentance and tears, is thinner than the rest. We believe in Aksyonov's long suffering far more than in the murderer's sudden remorse.

Still, the story's plainness is its power. The language is stripped of ornament, the sentences simple, the emotions understated. Tolstoy trusts the situation, and he is right to.

A word on the narration is also in order. Tolstoy tells the story in a calm, almost reportorial voice that never tells us how to feel, and that calm makes the injustice more upsetting, not less. When the police officer says that Aksyonov's face and manner betray him, we know he is wrong, but the narrator does not rush to say so. We are left to feel what it is to be disbelieved, which is exactly the experience the story is about.

III. The Impact It Had

This is early Tolstoy, written before Anna Karenina was finished and years before the crisis he described in A Confession. It came out when he was deeply involved in teaching village children and had produced a primer and a series of readers. Those books were for him as serious as any novel, and the simple stories in them, folk tales and moral anecdotes, taught him the style that would define his later work. So this tale belongs to the moment when the great novelist began to practice writing as plainly as a proverb.

The theme it develops, that goodness means forgiving and not resisting evil, runs straight into his later philosophy. After his religious conversion Tolstoy came to believe that the Sermon on the Mount should be taken literally, and that violence, even in self-defense, was wrong. That teaching influenced Mohandas Gandhi, who corresponded with him, and through Gandhi it reached Martin Luther King Jr. and the broader tradition of nonviolent protest. A story about a man who refuses to take revenge when he easily could is a small ancestor of that great idea, though I would not claim it shaped any of those movements directly.

The story has been widely translated and anthologized, and is a regular in collections of Tolstoy's short fiction. It is often given to students because of its brevity and its clarity of theme, and it is a favorite for discussions of justice, forgiveness, and faith. It also has a place in the history of the wrongful-conviction story, which has become a staple of modern fiction and film. The details differ, but a man shut away for a crime he did not commit and slowly finding a kind of freedom inside is an image that has recurred often since.

It is worth saying plainly how little is documented about its reception when it first appeared. In the early 1870s Tolstoy was already the famous author of War and Peace, so anything he published drew attention, but the attention was on his novels, and these children's-reader stories were treated by the literary world as a curious sideline. Their real audience was in the schools. Their lasting impact came later, once readers looked back and saw in these simple pieces the seed of the moral voice that would define his last decades.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved the story's emotional honesty, especially the scene with the wife. Many people say that this is the moment that stays with them, since the pain of being doubted by the person closest to you is a feeling nearly everyone understands. It makes the injustice personal. Tolstoy knew that being punished by the state is terrible, but being disbelieved by a loved one is worse.

They have also loved the transformation. Aksyonov does not turn into a saint by going through a conversion. He grows into it over many years, and his quiet decency feels earned. His refusal to name Makar is a surprise, and the reader can only feel the difficulty of what he does. People often describe a sense of a weight lifting at that moment, even though nothing has changed in Aksyonov's circumstances.

The ending is another favorite. Because the release comes too late, the story refuses to console us with a typical reward. Instead it suggests that the inner change mattered more than the outer one. Many readers find that moving, and also somewhat unsettling, and the combination keeps the tale alive in memory.

And the story is easy to share. It can be read aloud in a quarter of an hour, and listeners find it as gripping as a thriller and as thoughtful as a sermon. Tolstoy wanted stories that anyone could follow, and this is a good example of how he succeeded.

Teachers and translators have also been fond of it, and that fondness says something. Because the vocabulary is plain and the events are clear, the story is a favorite for students learning Russian or English, and for classes that want to talk about big ideas without drowning in difficult prose. Many readers first meet Tolstoy this way, and report being surprised that the author of enormous novels could be so spare.

Another thing readers prize is its sense of time. We feel twenty-six years pass in a single sentence, and yet the weight of those years is present in every line afterward. The white hair, the stoop, the silence, and the unused laugh all tell us what the years have done. It is a model of how a short story can cover a lifetime without feeling rushed, and writers still study it for that reason.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The first objection is that the story seems to recommend passivity. Aksyonov accepts an injustice for twenty-six years and never fights it, and when he finally has the chance to speak, he chooses silence. Some readers find this admirable, but others see a dangerous message, that suffering should be borne meekly and that wrongdoing may be forgiven without being answered. Critics of Tolstoyan nonviolence have long argued that it can leave victims at the mercy of the powerful.

A second objection is about realism. The coincidence that brings the murderer to the same prison is hard to accept, and the speed of Makar's repentance feels more like a moral device than a human reaction. Some critics have also said that the story's religious frame is so strong that it settles questions too easily. The title says that God sees the truth, but the story never tests whether that comfort is enough for a man who has lost everything.

There is also the matter of the wife. She has little role beyond her doubt, and some readers feel she is treated harshly by the story, which turns her one moment of uncertainty into a wound that Aksyonov never heals. We never learn how she fared or whether she believed him in the end. The children, too, vanish. A modern reader may find that the story's moral clarity comes at the expense of its women and its human complexity.

Finally, a few readers find the tale simply too schematic, a parable more than a story. That is a fair description, and it was what Tolstoy intended, but those who prefer the subtle psychological detail of his big novels sometimes feel that the short folk tales, for all their power, leave out too much.

A related complaint is the way the story handles guilt and innocence. Aksyonov, at the start, is described as someone who used to drink and be riotous, and the tale hints that his suffering may be partly a punishment for his sins. That reading, that the innocent man is secretly being paid back for something else, strikes many modern readers as troubling, since it can excuse an unjust system. Tolstoy leaves the idea open, but the unease remains, and it leads some to read the story as more consoling toward authority than it should be.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The story belongs to the tradition of the moral tale, which stretches back to the parables of Jesus and the fables of every culture. It also draws on the Russian folk story and the Russian tradition of the holy fool and the suffering saint. Tolstoy was writing in a country where Orthodox Christianity shaped how people thought about suffering, and the idea that undeserved pain can purify the soul was widespread.

It invites comparison with other stories of wrongful imprisonment. Alexandre Dumas's The Count of Monte Cristo is the great opposite, since Edmond Dantes is jailed unjustly too, but he emerges to take an elaborate revenge. Victor Hugo's Les Miserables offers another view, in which mercy given to a convict transforms him. And Dostoevsky's House of the Dead, a fictionalized account of his own years in a Siberian prison camp, shows the same setting from a darker and more realistic angle. Tolstoy admired that book. More recently, tales such as Stephen King's Rita Hayworth and Shawshank Redemption return to the idea of an innocent man finding a measure of freedom behind bars, though with a very different spirit.

It matters today because the question at its center is still uncomfortable. When you have been wronged, and you can name the wrongdoer, what do you owe him? Tolstoy's answer is radical and not everyone will accept it, but the story does not lecture. It gives us a man, a long time, and a choice, and lets us decide what we think. In a few pages it also shows how a life that begins in lightness, with a guitar and a joke about gray hair, can end in a different kind of lightness, which is a rare thing for a short story to manage.

One more connection is with Tolstoy's own later fiction. The mercy that Aksyonov shows to Makar is close to the mercy in Tolstoy's late tales, such as Where Love Is, God Is, and echoes in the novel Resurrection, where a nobleman follows a wrongly condemned woman to Siberia and slowly changes his own life. In that sense this early story is a small rehearsal for a theme that Tolstoy would return to for the rest of his career, the idea that suffering unjustly borne can give a person a kind of moral authority that no court can take away.
