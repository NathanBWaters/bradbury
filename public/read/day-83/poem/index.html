Nightly Poem • Pastoral & Elegy

The Buried Life

Matthew Arnold (1852) • 6 min read

Beneath the light war of mocking words and daily social disguises, an underground river of authentic selfhood flows unseen until a beloved hand unlocks the heart.

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Buried Life is one of Matthew Arnold's most thoughtful poems, published in 1852 in a collection called Empedocles on Etna, and Other Poems. Arnold was a Victorian poet, critic and, for most of his working life, a school inspector, traveling Britain to examine classrooms. He lived in an age when science and industry were shaking religious certainty, and many thoughtful people felt cut off from each other and from themselves. This poem begins as a lovers' quarrel that turns sad, then grows into a large question: why is it so hard for people to say what they really feel, even to someone they love? Arnold's answer is an image of a hidden river running deep inside every person, mostly unnoticed. The poem argues that love, at rare moments, lets us hear that river, and that those moments are among the truest of our lives.

Light flows our war of mocking words, and yet,

Behold, with tears mine eyes are wet.

I feel a nameless sadness o’er me roll.

Yes, yes, we know that we can jest,

We know, we know that we can smile;

But there’s a something in this breast

To which thy light words bring no rest,

And thy gay smiles no anodyne.

Give me thy hand, and hush awhile,

And turn those limpid eyes on mine,

And let me read there, love, thy inmost soul.

Alas, is even Love too weak

To unlock the heart, and let it speak?

Are even lovers powerless to reveal

To one another what indeed they feel?

I knew the mass of men conceal’d

Their thoughts, for fear that if reveal’d

They would by other men be met

With blank indifference, or with blame reprov’d:

I knew they liv’d and mov’d

Trick’d in disguises, alien to the rest

Of men, and alien to themselves—and yet

The same heart beats in every human breast!

But we, my love—doth a like spell benumb

Our hearts—our voices?—must we too be dumb?

Ah, well for us, if even we,

Even for a moment, can get free

Our heart, and have our lips unchain’d:

For that which seals them hath been deep-ordain’d.

Fate, which foresaw

How frivolous a baby man would be,

By what distractions he would be possess’d,

How he would pour himself in every strife,

And well-nigh change his own identity;

That it might keep from his capricious play

His genuine self, and force him to obey,

Even in his own despite, his being’s law,

Bade through the deep recesses of our breast

The unregarded River of our Life

Pursue with indiscernible flow its way;

And that we should not see

The buried stream, and seem to be

Eddying about in blind uncertainty,

Though driving on with it eternally.

But often, in the world’s most crowded streets,

But often, in the din of strife,

There rises an unspeakable desire

After the knowledge of our buried life,

A thirst to spend our fire and restless force

In tracking out our true, original course;

A longing to inquire

Into the mystery of this heart that beats

So wild, so deep in us, to know

Whence our thoughts come and where they go.

And many a man in his own breast then delves,

But deep enough, alas, none ever mines:

And we have been on many thousand lines,

And we have shown on each talent and power,

But hardly have we, for one little hour,

Been on our own line, have we been ourselves;

Hardly had skill to utter one of all

The nameless feelings that course through our breast,

But they course on for ever unexpress’d.

And long we try in vain to speak and act

Our hidden self, and what we say and do

Is eloquent, is well—but ’tis not true:

And then we will no more be rack’d

With inward striving, and demand

Of all the thousand nothings of the hour

Their stupefying power;

Ah yes, and they benumb us at our call:

Yet still, from time to time, vague and forlorn,

From the soul’s subterranean depth upborne

As from an infinitely distant land,

Come airs, and floating echoes, and convey

A melancholy into all our day.

Only—but this is rare—

When a belovèd hand is laid in ours,

When, jaded with the rush and glare

Of the interminable hours,

Our eyes can in another’s eyes read clear,

When our world-deafen’d ear

Is by the tones of a lov’d voice caress’d—

A bolt is shot back somewhere in our breast

And a lost pulse of feeling stirs again:

The eye sinks inward, and the heart lies plain,

And what we mean, we say, and what we would, we know.

A man becomes aware of his life’s flow,

And hears its winding murmur, and he sees

The meadows where it glides, the sun, the breeze.

And there arrives a lull in the hot race

Wherein he doth for ever chase

That flying and elusive shadow, Rest.

An air of coolness plays upon his face,

And an unwonted calm pervades his breast.

And then he thinks he knows

The Hills where his life rose,

And the Sea where it goes.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem opens in a small, painful moment between two people. The speaker and his beloved have been trading light, joking, mocking words, yet he finds his eyes wet with tears and feels "a nameless sadness." Something in his chest does not respond to her smiles. He asks her to take his hand, be quiet, and look at him so he can read "thy inmost soul." Then comes the real question: "Alas, is even Love too weak to unlock the heart, and let it speak?"

From there the poem widens. The speaker says that most people hide their thoughts because they fear that, if they were revealed, they would meet "blank indifference" or blame. So people live in disguises, strangers to others and even to themselves. And yet, he adds, "the same heart beats in every human breast." This is a central idea: we are all alike underneath, and all pretending to be different on top.

Next comes a strange and rather beautiful explanation. Fate, the speaker says, knew how frivolous human beings would be, how easily we pour ourselves into every distraction and "well-nigh change [our] own identity." So to protect our genuine self, Fate hid it. Deep in our chest it set "the unregarded River of our Life," flowing quietly beneath everything, so that we cannot see it and seem to be "eddying about in blind uncertainty, though driving on with it eternally." That image is the poem's key. Your real life is a river moving under the surface of your daily activity. You are carried along by it even when you feel lost.

The middle of the poem describes the longing to know that hidden self. In crowded streets and the "din of strife," a desire rises "after the knowledge of our buried life." People dig into themselves but never deep enough. We take on "many thousand lines" of activity and show every talent, but "hardly have we, for one little hour, been on our own line," been ourselves. What we say is eloquent, but "'tis not true."

The final movement is the answer. Rarely, when a loved hand is laid in ours, when we can read another's eyes and hear a loved voice, "a bolt is shot back somewhere in our breast." The heart lies plain, and "what we mean, we say." Then a man hears his life's "winding murmur," sees the meadows it passes through, and feels a cool calm. The last three lines give the river a source and an ending: "The Hills where his life rose, and the Sea where it goes." Love does not solve life, but it lets us see where we come from and where we are headed, if only for a moment.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The great strength of The Buried Life is its central image. A person's true self as a hidden river is simple, memorable and flexible. It lets Arnold say several things at once: that the self is moving, not fixed; that it is deep rather than surface; that it is natural and powerful, and that we mostly cannot see it. The closing picture of hills and sea gives the metaphor a satisfying shape, a beginning and an end, so the poem feels completed rather than merely stopped.

The poem is also admirable for the honesty of its mood. It does not rush to comfort. It spends most of its length describing how hard it is to be known, and the sadness of masks and noise. The quiet turn at "Only, but this is rare," is skillful, because the word "rare" keeps the happy ending from being too easy. The passage in which a bolt "is shot back" and the heart "lies plain" has a real emotional force, and the plain monosyllables of "what we mean, we say, and what we would, we know" are among Arnold's best lines.

The form, irregular lines of varying length with rhymes that arrive unpredictably, suits the subject. The short and long lines make the poem sound like thought itself, running on, catching, doubling back. When the rhyme finally clicks, as in the lines about the river's "indiscernible flow," it feels earned.

Now the problems. The poem is abstract for long stretches. After the first few lines there is almost no concrete scene. We lose the specific lovers and enter a general lecture about "the mass of men." The idea that everyone hides behind disguises is asserted rather than shown, and the speaker's tone can feel somewhat lofty and explanatory. Arnold the critic and teacher is never far away.

The device of Fate, who "foresaw how frivolous a baby man would be," is awkward. The image of humanity as a frivolous baby is faintly patronizing, and the explanation that Fate hid the river to protect us from ourselves feels more like a clever idea than a felt truth. Some of the language is also dated and stiff, with contractions like "conceal'd" and "reprov'd" that make it sound older than it needs to, and a few rhymes that look forced.

Finally, the ending can seem slightly too tidy. After so much struggle, one clasped hand resolves everything. The poem's consolation is more emotional than rigorous, and a skeptical reader may feel that love is being asked to carry more weight than any single person can hold. Still, the poem's best moments are remarkably moving.

III. The Impact It Had

The Buried Life did not make a large public splash when it appeared in 1852. The book it came in, Empedocles on Etna, and Other Poems, was a small, rather somber volume published anonymously, with the signature "A," and Arnold himself withdrew the title poem soon afterward, uneasy about its gloom. The Buried Life survived and gradually became one of his most quoted works, valued by readers who found in it a language for inner isolation.

For Arnold's own career, the poem marks the high point of his emotional lyric period. In the early 1850s he was also taking up a new life, marrying and beginning work as an inspector of schools. Soon his energy would move toward criticism and social writing, such as Essays in Criticism and Culture and Anarchy, where he argued for the value of culture and thoughtful reading in a materialistic age. The Buried Life stands as the personal, wistful counterpart to those public essays, showing the same worry about modern life, the hurry and noise, expressed as private feeling.

Its idea of a hidden inner life has had a long influence. Readers have often seen it as an anticipation of the modern interest in the unconscious. It is a stretch to say Arnold foreshadowed Freud directly, and there is no evidence Freud read him, but the notion that our real selves lie below awareness and are hard to reach is plainly kin to later psychology. The poem also anticipates the preoccupation of twentieth-century poets with alienation and the failure of communication. Writers like T. S. Eliot, who admired and argued with Arnold, took up similar themes in a harsher key.

Critics in the twentieth century, including Lionel Trilling in his well-known study of Arnold, treated the poem as central to understanding the Victorian sense of divided selfhood. It is often paired with Dover Beach as a statement of Arnold's modern melancholy. In the classroom it is used to teach the Victorian crisis of faith and identity, and to introduce the extended metaphor.

The phrase itself, the buried life, has passed into ordinary speech and into book titles, essays and memoirs, as a shorthand for the secret inner self that never quite makes it into daily conversation.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Buried Life above all for naming something they recognize but rarely say. Most people have had the experience of being in a conversation, even a loving one, and feeling that the most important thing is going unsaid. The poem puts that feeling into words with an exactness that is a relief. It tells the reader they are not alone in feeling opaque to others, and that this is a general condition, not a personal failing.

The river image is often named as a favorite. It is a gentle, flowing metaphor that comforts as well as describes. The idea that beneath our hurry there is a steady current, carrying us toward a purpose we cannot see, appeals to many readers, religious and nonreligious alike. They find in it a way of thinking about their own lives that does not require either blind confidence or despair.

Many also love the emotional scene at the poem's core, when a lover's hand and voice release something in the speaker's chest. The idea that real intimacy is a kind of unlocking, a bolt sliding back, has stayed with generations of readers. Couples have quoted it, and writers have praised the way the poem links love not to romance in the usual sense but to being truly seen.

For other poets and critics, the poem is admired as a classic of Victorian sincerity. It combines serious thinking with real feeling, which was what Arnold himself demanded of poetry. He believed poetry should help us live, and this poem is an example of what he meant. Admirers have prized its calm, grave tone, which does not shout its distress.

Finally, the last three lines are widely loved. The short, shifting rhythm, "The Hills where his life rose, and the Sea where it goes," gives the poem a gentle landing, with an old-fashioned grandeur that suits the subject. Many readers can recall those lines long after forgetting the rest of the poem. They suggest that a life has a shape and a direction, and that glimpsing it, even briefly, is worth the long wait.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Buried Life has been admired, but it has not escaped criticism, some from Arnold's own time and some from later readers.

The most frequent complaint is that it is too abstract and too much like a lecture. Victorian reviewers of Arnold's early poetry often felt that he was thoughtful but cool, more at home in ideas than in sensation. Even sympathetic readers, then and later, have noted that the poem moves quickly from a vivid human moment to general reflection and stays there. Instead of watching particular people, we listen to a speaker explaining the condition of "the mass of men." That distance weakens the emotional force for some readers.

Another objection concerns the sentimental resolution. The poem suggests that love can open the buried life, and that is a pleasant and comforting idea, but it may be too easy. Critics have asked whether one moment of intimacy can really overcome a lifetime of disguise, and whether the poem is being fully honest about how difficult connection can be. A harder poem might have left the speaker alone with the silence.

The use of Fate and the picture of humanity as a "frivolous baby" has struck some readers as awkward and condescending. It also raises a puzzling problem: if Fate deliberately hid our true selves to keep us on course, then the silence between people is a design, not a defect, which sits uneasily with the poem's lament that we cannot speak. The logic, some readers argue, does not quite hold together.

There is also a historical criticism of Arnold's whole approach. Later modernist poets, who admired his seriousness, thought his verse was too polite and too tied to inherited forms. Eliot, for instance, praised Arnold as a critic while being less convinced by his poetry. His smooth, melancholy cadences seemed to belong to a cultured, comfortable class. A more political reading might note that the poem's isolation is that of an educated man in a crowded street, and that it pays little attention to the material conditions that divide people.

Finally, some readers feel the poem is overlong and its imagery repetitive, circling the same idea in several stanzas before the final resolution. These are fair objections, though most are matters of taste.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Buried Life sits in the Victorian tradition of doubt and inwardness. Many writers of the era, Tennyson in In Memoriam, Browning in his dramatic monologues, Arnold himself in Dover Beach, were trying to keep faith in human meaning after science and industry had weakened older certainties. Arnold's particular note was calm, reflective sadness, and The Buried Life is one of its purest expressions. It is a poem about modern loneliness written when the word "modern" was still new.

Its ancestors lie in the Romantic poets, especially Wordsworth, who also looked inward for the sources of meaning and used natural images, rivers among them, for the movement of the mind. Arnold admired Wordsworth deeply and edited a selection of his poems. But where Wordsworth finds comfort in nature, Arnold finds it only in human connection, and even that is rare. That is the difference between a Romantic and a Victorian: the hope is smaller and the loneliness larger.

Its descendants are the poems and novels of alienation in the twentieth century. The sense that people wear masks and cannot speak what they feel runs through Eliot's Prufrock, with its question of whether it would have been worth it to say what one meant, and through the novels of Virginia Woolf and others who explored the hidden stream of consciousness. The poem does not cause these works, but it prepares the ground for them.

A good companion is Arnold's own To Marguerite, with its famous line that we are like islands in a "sea of life," each one separate. The Buried Life offers a gentler, more hopeful version of the same thought, with the river below the surface joining everyone. It can also be set against the Romantic ideal of the self as a joyful, expressive force. Here the self is deep and silent, which feels more like a modern view.

Why read it today? Because in an age of constant messages and performed identities, the problem the poem describes has only grown. We are surrounded by talk and often more hidden than before. The poem offers a quiet reminder that there is a deeper current under the noise, that love and attention can reach it, and that a few unguarded moments may be worth more than a thousand witty exchanges.
