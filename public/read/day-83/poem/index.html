The Buried Life

Matthew Arnold (1852) • 6 min read

Beneath the light war of mocking words and daily social disguises, an underground river of authentic selfhood flows unseen until a beloved hand unlocks the heart.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Published in Matthew Arnold’s 1852 collection Empedocles on Etna, and Other Poems, 'The Buried Life' emerged as Arnold transitioned from his passionate youthful romance in Switzerland to his demanding career as an Inspector of Schools across industrializing Victorian Britain. Belonging to the Victorian Literature tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'The Buried Life' (1852) unfolds across 98 verbatim lines in 8 stanzas. Anticipating Sigmund Freud’s theory of the unconscious and T. S. Eliot’s Prufrock by more than half a century, 'The Buried Life' stands alongside 'Dover Beach' as Arnold’s deepest exploration of modern interiority. Through ninety-eight lines of irregular Pindaric-ode verse mingling iambic pentameter with meditative trimeter and tetrameter lines, Matthew Arnold fuses formal precision with emotional immediacy, making the poem an enduring cornerstone of the poetic canon.

Light flows our war of mocking words, and yet,

Behold, with tears mine eyes are wet.

I feel a nameless sadness o’er me roll.

Yes, yes, we know that we can jest,

We know, we know that we can smile;

But there’s a something in this breast

To which thy light words bring no rest,

And thy gay smiles no anodyne.

Give me thy hand, and hush awhile,

And turn those limpid eyes on mine,

And let me read there, love, thy inmost soul.

Alas, is even Love too weak

To unlock the heart, and let it speak?

Are even lovers powerless to reveal

To one another what indeed they feel?

I knew the mass of men conceal’d

Their thoughts, for fear that if reveal’d

They would by other men be met

With blank indifference, or with blame reprov’d:

I knew they liv’d and mov’d

Trick’d in disguises, alien to the rest

Of men, and alien to themselves—and yet

The same heart beats in every human breast!

But we, my love—doth a like spell benumb

Our hearts—our voices?—must we too be dumb?

Ah, well for us, if even we,

Even for a moment, can get free

Our heart, and have our lips unchain’d:

For that which seals them hath been deep-ordain’d.

Fate, which foresaw

How frivolous a baby man would be,

By what distractions he would be possess’d,

How he would pour himself in every strife,

And well-nigh change his own identity;

That it might keep from his capricious play

His genuine self, and force him to obey,

Even in his own despite, his being’s law,

Bade through the deep recesses of our breast

The unregarded River of our Life

Pursue with indiscernible flow its way;

And that we should not see

The buried stream, and seem to be

Eddying about in blind uncertainty,

Though driving on with it eternally.

But often, in the world’s most crowded streets,

But often, in the din of strife,

There rises an unspeakable desire

After the knowledge of our buried life,

A thirst to spend our fire and restless force

In tracking out our true, original course;

A longing to inquire

Into the mystery of this heart that beats

So wild, so deep in us, to know

Whence our thoughts come and where they go.

And many a man in his own breast then delves,

But deep enough, alas, none ever mines:

And we have been on many thousand lines,

And we have shown on each talent and power,

But hardly have we, for one little hour,

Been on our own line, have we been ourselves;

Hardly had skill to utter one of all

The nameless feelings that course through our breast,

But they course on for ever unexpress’d.

And long we try in vain to speak and act

Our hidden self, and what we say and do

Is eloquent, is well—but ’tis not true:

And then we will no more be rack’d

With inward striving, and demand

Of all the thousand nothings of the hour

Their stupefying power;

Ah yes, and they benumb us at our call:

Yet still, from time to time, vague and forlorn,

From the soul’s subterranean depth upborne

As from an infinitely distant land,

Come airs, and floating echoes, and convey

A melancholy into all our day.

Only—but this is rare—

When a belovèd hand is laid in ours,

When, jaded with the rush and glare

Of the interminable hours,

Our eyes can in another’s eyes read clear,

When our world-deafen’d ear

Is by the tones of a lov’d voice caress’d—

A bolt is shot back somewhere in our breast

And a lost pulse of feeling stirs again:

The eye sinks inward, and the heart lies plain,

And what we mean, we say, and what we would, we know.

A man becomes aware of his life’s flow,

And hears its winding murmur, and he sees

The meadows where it glides, the sun, the breeze.

And there arrives a lull in the hot race

Wherein he doth for ever chase

That flying and elusive shadow, Rest.

An air of coolness plays upon his face,

And an unwonted calm pervades his breast.

And then he thinks he knows

The Hills where his life rose,

And the Sea where it goes.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Published in Matthew Arnold’s 1852 collection Empedocles on Etna, and Other Poems, 'The Buried Life' emerged as Arnold transitioned from his passionate youthful romance in Switzerland to his demanding career as an Inspector of Schools across industrializing Victorian Britain. Within the broader trajectory of Victorian Literature, Matthew Arnold composed 'The Buried Life' (1852) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Light flows our war of mocking words, and yet,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Matthew Arnold grounded these 98 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'The Buried Life' lies the modern psychological alienation of the divided self: because people fear that if their innermost feelings were exposed they would encounter only 'blank indifference, or with blame reproved,' they hide behind a defensive 'mass of hiding disguises' in the 'hot race' of the world, yet still feel 'An unspeakable desire / After the knowledge of our buried life'—unlocked only in rare moments of hand-in-hand intimacy. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"In tracking out our true, original course;"—Matthew Arnold develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Matthew Arnold constructs 'The Buried Life' using ninety-eight lines of irregular Pindaric-ode verse mingling iambic pentameter with meditative trimeter and tetrameter lines, sustained by the central hydrographic metaphor of the 'unregarded river of our life' winding through 'the deep recesses of our breast' toward the distant sea. Spanning 98 lines across 8 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Matthew Arnold balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Light flows our war of mocking words, and yet," and "And the Sea where it goes." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Anticipating Sigmund Freud’s theory of the unconscious and T. S. Eliot’s Prufrock by more than half a century, 'The Buried Life' stands alongside 'Dover Beach' as Arnold’s deepest exploration of modern interiority. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'The Buried Life' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Matthew Arnold’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form. From nineteenth- and twentieth-century critical debates to contemporary anthologies, Matthew Arnold’s 'The Buried Life' retains all of its original freshness and authority.
