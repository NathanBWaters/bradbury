The 1898 Columbarium of San Francisco and the Great Cemetery Eviction (1900–1941)

San Francisco — How San Francisco became the only city in America to outlaw cemeteries and evict 150,000 graves to Colma—leaving behind B. J. S. Cahill's hidden copper-domed temple of memory (Victorian Necropolis (1898) to the Great Cemetery Removal (1914–1941)) • 8 min read

“The living need the land more than the dead.” — Real-estate developer lobby slogan during the San Francisco Cemetery Removal Campaigns (1914–1937)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Hidden at the end of a quiet cul-de-sac at 1 Loraine Court in the Inner Richmond District stands one of San Francisco's most breathtaking architectural secrets: the 1898 Neoclassical Columbarium designed by London-born architect Bernard J. S. Cahill for the Independent Order of Odd Fellows. Between 1900 and 1941, pressured by residential land speculators around Lone Mountain, San Francisco passed municipal ordinances banning all new burials and forcibly disinterring over 150,000 bodies from Laurel Hill, Calvary, Masonic, and Odd Fellows Cemeteries—shipping the dead by special funeral streetcars to Colma. Spared demolition behind apartment houses, Cahill's four-story stained-glass rotunda remains the only active non-denominational repository for human ashes inside the city limits.

Walk down Anza Street just west of Stanyan in the Inner Richmond District, turn north into the short residential cul-de-sac of Loraine Court, and you will experience a jolt of architectural surrealism: rising directly between ordinary 1930s stucco apartment flats stands a monumental 1898 Neoclassical white dome crowned with oxidized green copper and Corinthian porticoes. Why is a grand nineteenth-century funerary rotunda marooned in the middle of a residential neighborhood? Because in 1898, Loraine Court did not have a single house on it: this entire quarter of western San Francisco—stretching across 300 acres around the cross-topped peak of Lone Mountain—was the city's 'Big Four' Victorian Necropolis, comprising Laurel Hill, Calvary (Catholic), Masonic, and Odd Fellows Cemeteries.

Established in the 1850s and 1860s when Lone Mountain was considered so far outside the city limits that only horse-drawn hearses and steam dummy trains visited its sandy hills, the four cemeteries held over 150,000 graves of California's Gold Rush pioneers, U.S. Senators, Comstock Lode barons, and working families. In 1895, as the Independent Order of Odd Fellows expanded their 1885 crematorium on the western edge of their twenty-seven-acre cemetery, they commissioned twenty-nine-year-old English-born architect Bernard J. S. Cahill—who later invented the famous 'Butterfly' octahedral world map projection and co-designed the 1904 Civic Center plan—to build a permanent fireproof Columbarium ('dove-cote' for funerary urns).

Dedicated in January 1898 and modeled in part on the Roman Pantheon and Ostia Antica's columbaria, Cahill's San Francisco Columbarium was constructed of thick steel-reinforced concrete and brick around a soaring forty-five-foot central atrium ringed by three tiers of circular white-and-gold balconies and four radiating Grecian wings named after mythological constellations. Look straight up from the center of the ground-floor mosaic medallion (which depicts the three interlocking chain links of the Odd Fellows—Friendship, Love, and Truth): natural sunlight filters through a magnificent twenty-eight-foot opalescent stained-glass skylight dome depicting three trumpeting angels ascending through golden clouds, flanked around the drum by sixteen stained-glass windows fabricated by the California Art Glass Works.

Barely two years after Cahill's Columbarium opened its bronze doors, the political war against San Francisco's dead began: as electric streetcars pushed residential tracts out into the Richmond and Western Addition, real-estate developers led by the Richmond Promotion Association complained that three hundred acres of untaxed graveyards were blocking the city's westward expansion. On March 26, 1900, the San Francisco Board of Supervisors enacted an ordinance banning all further earth burials within the city and county limits effective August 1, 1901, followed in 1912–1914 by eviction notices ordering the cemeteries closed and their graves removed south across the county line to the newly incorporated railroad necropolis of Lawndale (today's Colma, where the dead outnumber the living thousand to one).

Notice the bitter legal battle that followed: for fifteen years across three statewide and municipal ballot propositions in the 1920s, preservationists and pioneer descendants defeated the real-estate lobby until voters finally approved the mandatory disinterment ordinances for Odd Fellows and Masonic Cemeteries in 1929 and Laurel Hill and Calvary in 1937. Between 1930 and 1941, San Francisco witnessed one of the strangest mass migrations in urban history: teams of gravediggers excavated more than 150,000 coffins and skeleton boxes, loaded them onto special green-painted Market Street Railway funeral streetcars and flatbed trucks, and reburied unclaimed pioneers in mass mounds at Greenlawn Memorial Park in Colma while dumping thousands of unclaimed granite headstones into the bay at Aquatic Park and the Marina Wave Organ jetty.

While bulldozers leveled the 1885 Odd Fellows Crematorium next door and developers subdivided the former cemetery lawns into Rossi Playground, USF buildings, and the stucco flats of Anza and Balboa streets, Cahill's 1898 Columbarium was spared the wrecking ball solely because several thousand families held perpetual property deeds to the marble and bronze niches inside its walls. However, because California law after 1937 banned the operation of an active crematorium next door to sell new niches, the Columbarium lost its maintenance revenue stream and slid into four decades of Gothic decay: between the 1940s and 1979, winter storms shattered panes in the stained-glass dome, rainwater cascaded down the balconies, ferns sprouted from cracked plaster, and vandals pried open bronze niche doors.

The rescue of the San Francisco Columbarium began in 1980 when a new cemetery owner hired a devoted, eccentric caretaker named Emmons Blake, who moved into the neighborhood and spent the next eighteen years painstakingly scrubbing eighty years of grime off the Corinthian columns, matching Victorian paint colors in aqua, coral, and gold leaf, and re-leading every broken stained-glass window. Following its 1996 designation as San Francisco City Landmark #211 and its restoration by the Neptune Society, the Columbarium's 8,500 niches became a living portrait gallery of San Francisco history—housing the ashes of Supervisor Harvey Milk (partially), architectural visionary Bernard J. S. Cahill himself, silver-era pioneers, and toy magnate F. A. O. Schwarz's descendants.

Walk along the curving second- and third-floor galleries today and you will see why the Columbarium feels less like a mausoleum than a museum of human affection: behind glass-fronted niches sit vintage baseball gloves, miniature Gibson guitars, matchbox cable cars, antique teacups, and handwritten love letters placed beside silver and blown-glass urns. Out of the 150,000 graves that once covered the hills of Lone Mountain, only Cahill's Neoclassical rotunda on Loraine Court remains above ground—a quiet, sunlit sanctuary reminding hurried modern San Franciscans that a city's character is measured by how tenderly it keeps faith with those who built it.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The 1900–1941 Cemetery Evictions: How Colma Became the City of Souls

Why does San Francisco have only two active cemeteries within its city limits today (the federal San Francisco National Cemetery in the Presidio and the tiny 1776 graveyard beside Mission Dolores), while tiny Colma just south of Daly City holds 1.5 million graves? Driven by real-estate developers hungry for the 300-acre Lone Mountain cemetery belt, San Francisco banned burials in 1900 and forcibly disinterred 150,000 bodies between 1914 and 1941—charging families $10 per grave to move monuments and dumping unclaimed tombstones as coastal riprap along the bay.

2. B. J. S. Cahill's Rotunda and Opalescent Stained Glass

Architect Bernard J. S. Cahill (1866–1944)—who devoted his career to sacred architecture, civic planning, and his 1909 octahedral 'Butterfly Map' of the globe—designed the 1898 Columbarium specifically to counter the gloom of Victorian graveyards. By pairing a fireproof concrete-and-steel shell with an opalescent stained-glass dome by California Art Glass Works and concentric white-and-gold balconies, he created a luminous Pantheon where natural light illuminates every tier of niches.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this hidden landmark with three details: first, the Odd Fellows Three-Link Floor Mosaic ('Friendship, Love, Truth') directly beneath the central stained-glass dome; second, caretaker Emmons Blake, whose eighteen-year single-handed restoration (1980–1998) rescued the rotunda from water damage and ruin; and third, the glass-fronted personal memory niches housing pioneer families, Supervisor Harvey Milk's memorial plaque, and architect B. J. S. Cahill's own urn.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk west along Geary Boulevard or Anza Street past Stanyan Street and turn north into 1 Loraine Court, where the copper-domed Columbarium is open free to respectful visitors during daytime hours. Step into the central rotunda to look up at the twenty-eight-foot stained-glass skylight of ascending angels, climb the spiral stairs to the second and third balconies, and then walk one block west to Rossi Playground—formerly the lawn of Odd Fellows Cemetery.
