Nightly Short Story • Satire & Wit

Don’t Get Technatal

Ray Bradbury (1939) • 5 min read

“For several moments Stern had eyed his typewriter ominously, contemplating whether he should utter the unutterable. Finally:”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Don't Get Technatal is a very short, very early piece of Ray Bradbury's writing. He published it in the summer of 1939 in Futuria Fantasia, a homemade science fiction fan magazine that he edited and typed himself when he was eighteen, with money from his friend and fellow fan Forrest Ackerman. It is not a polished story, and it was never meant to be one. It was a young fan's joke, written for other fans. The background is the Great Depression, when a movement called Technocracy argued that engineers, not politicians, should run society, handing out equal shares of energy credit to everyone. Newspapers talked about it constantly. Bradbury imagines that world in the year 1975 and drops into the apartment of a pulp writer who is miserable because a perfect society has taken away all his plots. It takes about three minutes to read, and its main interest today is as a window onto a teenage writer already thinking about how happiness, control and storytelling collide.

For several moments Stern had eyed his typewriter ominously, contemplating whether he should utter the unutterable. Finally:

“Damn!” he roared. “I can’t write any more! Look, look at that!” He tore the sheet out of the rollers and crumpled it in his fist. “If I’d known it would be this way,” he said, “I wouldn’t have voted for it! Technocracy is ruining everything!”

Bella Stern, preoccupied with her knitting, glanced up in horror. “What a temper,” she exclaimed. “Can’t you keep your voice down?” She fussed with her work. “There now,” she cried, “you made me drop a stitch!”

“I want to be a writer!” Samuel Stern lamented, turning with grim eyes to his wife. “And the Technate has spoiled my fun.”

“The way you talk, Samuel,” said his wife, “I actually believe you want to go back to that barbarism prevalent in the dark thirties!”

“It sounds like one damned good idea!” he said. “At least I’d have something decent, or indecent, to write about!”

“What can you mean?” she asked, tilting her head back and thinking. “Why can’t you write? There are just oodles of things I can think of that are readable.”

Something like a tear rolled down Samuel’s cheek. “No more gangsters, no more bank robberies, no more holdups, no more good, old-fashioned burglaries, no more vice gangs!” His voice grew lachrymose as he proceeded down an infinite line of “no mores.” “No more sadness,” he almost sobbed. “Everybody’s happy, contented. No more strife and hard work. Oh, for the days when a gangland massacre was headline scoop for me!”

“Tush!” sniffed Bella. “Have you been drinking again, Samuel?”

He hiccuped gently.

“I thought so,” she said.

“I had to do something,” he declared. “I’m going nuts for want of a plot.”

Bella Stern laid her knitting aside and walked to the balcony, looked meditatively down into the yawning canyon of the New York street fifty stories below. She turned back to Sam with a reminiscent smile.

“Why not write a love story?”

“What!” Stern shot out of his chair like a hooked eel.

“Why, yes,” she concluded. “A nice love story would be very enjoyable.”

“Love!” Stern’s voice was thick with sarcasm. “Why, we don’t even have decent love these days. A man can’t marry a woman for her money, and vice-versa. Everyone under Technocracy gets the same amount of credit. No more Reno, no more alimony, no more breach of promise, or lawsuits! Everything is cut and dried. The days of society weddings and coming out parties are gone⁠—cause everyone is equal. I can’t write political criticisms about graft in the government, about slums and terrible living conditions, about poor starving mothers and their babies. Everything is okay⁠—okay⁠—okay⁠—” his voice sobbed off into silence.

“Which should make you very happy,” countered his wife.

“Which makes me very sick,” growled Samuel Stern. “Look, Bell, all my life I wanted to be a writer. Okay. I’m writing for the pulp magazines for a coupla years. Right? Okay. Then I’m writing sea stories, gangsters, political views, first class-bump-offs. I’m happy.⁠ ⁠… I’m in my element. Then⁠—bingo!⁠—in comes Technocracy, makes everyone happy⁠—bump! out goes me! I just can’t stand writing the stuff the people read today. Everything is science and education.” He ruffled his thick black hair with his fingers and glared.

“You should be joyful that the population is at work doing what they want to do,” Bella beamed.

Sam continued muttering to himself. “They took all the sex magazines off the market first thing, all of the gangster, murder and detective publications. They been educating the children and making model citizens out of them.”

“Which is as it should be,” finished Bella.

“Do you realize,” he blazed, whipping his finger at her, “that for two years there hasn’t been more than a dozen murders in the city? Not one suicide or gang war⁠—or⁠—”

“Heavens!” sighed Bella. “Don’t be prehistoric, Sam. There hasn’t been anything really criminal for twenty years now. This is 1975 you know.” She came over and patted him gently on the shoulder. “Why don’t you write something science-fictional?”

“I don’t like science,” he spat.

“Then your only alternative is love,” she declared firmly.

He formed the despicable word with his lips, then: “No, I want something new and different.” He got up and strode to the window. In the penthouse below he saw half a dozen robots moving about speedily, working. His face lit up suddenly, like that of a tiger spying his prey. “Jumping Jigwheels!” he cried. “Why didn’t I think of it before! Robots! I’ll write a love story about two robots.”

Bella squelched him. “Be sensible,” she said.

“It might happen some day,” he argued. “Just think. Love oiled, welded, built of metal, wired for sound!” He laughed triumphantly, but it was a low laugh, a strange little sound. Bella expected him to beat his chest next. “Robots fall in love at first sight,” he announced, “and blow an audio tube!”

Bella smiled tolerantly. “You’re such a child, Sam. I sometimes wonder why I married you.”

Stern sank down, burning slowly, a crimson flush rising in his face. Only half a dozen murders in two years, he thought. No more politics, no more to write about. He had to have a story, just had to have one. He’d go crazy if something didn’t happen soon. His brain was clicking furiously. A calliope of thought was tooting in his subconscious. He had to have a story. He turned and looked at his wife, Bella, who stood watching the air traffic go by the window, bending over the sill, looking down into the street fifty floors below.⁠ ⁠…

… and then he reached slowly and quietly for his atomic gun.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story is a single conversation in a single room, and almost everything that matters is hidden in the jokes. Samuel Stern, a pulp magazine writer, sits at his typewriter in a fifty-story apartment in the New York of 1975. He is furious. He cannot write. His wife, Bella, who knits and cheerfully approves of everything, cannot understand why. The reason is that the Technate, the government of this future, has made life so smooth that there is nothing left for a writer of crime and adventure to write about. No gangsters, no holdups, no bank robberies, no scandals about graft, no starving mothers, no society weddings. Everyone gets the same credit, so no one marries for money. Only a dozen murders in two years. As Stern puts it, everything is okay, okay, okay.

The humor depends on the reversal. We are used to dystopias in which the horror is obvious: soldiers, prisons, suffering. Here the complaint is that the world is too good, and the person complaining is a hack who misses his raw material. Bella is the voice of the system, answering each of his grievances with a bright line about how this is as it should be and how he should be joyful that people are doing what they want. The comedy is that she is not wrong in any simple way, and Stern is not right in any noble way. He is not defending liberty. He is missing his job.

Underneath the gag there is a serious question that Bradbury would return to for the rest of his career: what happens to art, and to people, when conflict and pain are removed? Stern says everything is science and education now, and that he cannot stand writing the stuff people read today. The story hints that stories need trouble, and that a society that has banned trouble has also banned the thing that makes stories worth telling. Fahrenheit 451 would make a much more serious version of this argument, about a world that burns books to stay happy.

Then comes the last line, which is the real joke. Stern is desperate for a plot. His brain is clicking like a calliope. He looks at his wife, who is leaning out over the sill of a window fifty floors above the street. And then he reaches slowly and quietly for his atomic gun. The reader is left to supply the rest. He may be about to shoot her, or to shoot something else, or simply to stage a crime. The point is the leap: a man who cannot find a murder to write about is about to commit one. It is a dark punchline, in the manner of the pulp magazines Bradbury read, and it turns the whole tidy utopia into a place where the first murder in years might be committed by a bored author.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

It would be unfair to judge a teenager's fanzine sketch by the standards of a finished story, but it would also be dishonest to pretend it is better than it is. It is, frankly, a slight and uneven piece, and its main virtues are the ones you would expect from a clever eighteen-year-old: a good premise, a lively ear for patter, and a sharp final beat.

The premise is genuinely good. The idea of a writer made miserable by a perfect world is a witty inversion that gets the reader's attention in a couple of lines. It is also timely for its moment. Technocracy was a real movement of the early thirties, and the story's world, with its equal credits for everyone, comes straight out of the arguments people were having in newspapers. Bradbury uses it with a light touch, not trying to refute the movement, just imagining its consequences for one person. The pacing is well judged for a gag. The back-and-forth between Stern and Bella has the rhythm of a vaudeville routine, with Stern complaining and Bella supplying the straight lines, and the escalation, from crime to love to robots, builds toward the ending.

The ending itself is the best-crafted thing here. The last two paragraphs set the scene, with the balcony, the fifty floors and the wife bending over the sill, and then the final sentence arrives with a click. It is effective because it is abrupt. There is no explanation and no aftermath, just the reach for the gun. In a very short space Bradbury shows he understands how to end a story on an image that sticks.

The weaknesses are real, though. The characters are cartoons. Bella is an ideal housewife of the sitcom type, and Stern is a stage grouch. Much of the dialogue is broad slang of the period, with phrases like Jumping Jigwheels that will make a modern reader wince, and the humor sometimes gets in its own way. The setup is also rather talkative: Stern announces the rules of his world in speeches, explaining things the characters would both already know, which is the oldest trick for getting background across. The idea of a love story about two robots, with its joke about a blown audio tube, is also the weakest joke in the piece.

More importantly, the satire has no real bite. It lets the system off the hook because it never shows what the Technate costs anyone other than a hack writer, and Bradbury himself would do far better at that sort of thing later. As a piece of apprentice work, it shows promise. As a standalone story, it is a sketch.

III. The Impact It Had

The honest answer is that Don't Get Technatal had almost no impact outside the small world in which it appeared. It was published in Futuria Fantasia, a fan magazine produced in very small numbers, and it was not a story that critics reviewed or anthologies reprinted. Its significance is almost entirely biographical, and it is worth being clear about that and not inflating it.

What the story does reveal is how Bradbury got started. In the late thirties he was a teenager in Los Angeles, a member of the Los Angeles Science Fantasy Society, surrounded by other young fans who wrote letters, traded magazines and produced their own fanzines. Forrest Ackerman, an energetic fan and collector, was one of the people who encouraged him, and Futuria Fantasia, which Bradbury edited, was financed by Ackerman. The magazine ran only a handful of issues, and much of its content was written by Bradbury himself, sometimes under other names. It was a way to practice writing in public, to see his stories in print, and to find out what made readers laugh or shiver. Stories like this one are the early exercises of a writer who would go on to sell to professional magazines within a few years.

It also shows themes that would echo later. The worry about a society that has made itself too comfortable and too regulated would grow up into some of Bradbury's best-known work. The most obvious descendant is Fahrenheit 451, in which a government keeps its citizens content by banning books, and a minor but suggestive relative is the 1948 story Pillar of Fire, in which a future society has burned its ghost stories and horror writers. Here, in miniature and played for laughs, is the same fear: that a world without trouble is a world without stories, and that someone will pay for it.

In terms of culture at large, the story is a small footnote to the broader satire of technocratic planning that ran through twentieth-century fiction. It did not shape the conversation, and it is hard to find evidence that anyone cited it before Bradbury became famous. Today it is mostly read by completists and by those curious about how a major writer's voice formed. As teaching material, it is occasionally useful as an example of how early work can contain a writer's lifelong preoccupations in rough form, and as a reminder that every famous author began by sending something out into the void.

IV. What Readers Loved

For a story this obscure, there are no published reviews or documented fan letters to cite. Futuria Fantasia was a labor of love circulated among fans, and the surviving record of how it was received is thin. What can be said is what a reader might find appealing in the story, then and now.

The first appeal is the cleverness of the central reversal. Fans of science fiction in the thirties were used to stories that imagined the future as either a paradise or a nightmare. A story in which a paradise is a nightmare for one specific person, a hack who cannot sell his pulp stories anymore, would have made a pulp-reading audience laugh at itself. These readers knew exactly what a writer for the gangster and detective magazines did, and they knew how silly the dependence of those magazines on crime could look. The in-joke quality, a pulp writer complaining about running out of plots, is part of the charm.

The second is the sheer speed. The story takes only a few minutes to read, and nothing in it is padded. Stern's rant moves from crime to politics to love to robots with the tempo of a comedy routine, and there is real pleasure in the way each suggestion from Bella is shot down by an ever more ridiculous counterproposal. The robot love story, with its image of love oiled, welded and wired for sound, gives the reader a laugh and also a glimpse of a writer's mind grabbing at any idea in desperation.

The third is the snap ending, which readers of short fiction almost always enjoy. The last line, with Stern reaching for his atomic gun, turns the entire preceding conversation into a setup. A reader who has been smiling at a domestic squabble suddenly finds a murder in the air. The shock is cheerful, in the way pulp horror often is, and it leaves the reader with a grin and a small shiver.

Finally, readers today often value the story as a chance to see the beginning. People who love Bradbury's later work enjoy finding the earliest signs of the writer: the playfulness with language, the interest in what happens to imagination under pressure, the instinct for an image that lands. There is something touching about a young man at a typewriter writing a story about a man who cannot write. It is a small, funny self-portrait of the fan-writer as an anxious professional, composed by a teenager who had not yet sold a story to a paying magazine.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

There is no body of professional criticism on Don't Get Technatal that I can point to, so the objections here are the ones a careful reader would raise, not the record of a famous controversy.

The most obvious complaint is that it is minor. It runs to under a thousand words, its characters are thin, and its satire is a single joke stretched to a punchline. Readers who come to Bradbury through his mature work may be disappointed to find no trace of the lyricism, the sorrow or the small-town warmth that make his reputation. This is a fan magazine piece, and it reads like one.

A second complaint concerns the humor. The slang, the exaggerated reactions and the wisecracks belong to a particular kind of thirties comic writing, and they have aged poorly. Bella's cheerful obedience and her role as an endlessly patient wife make her a type, and the story's jokes at her expense, culminating in the implied threat to her, have a casual quality that modern readers may find uncomfortable. The ending can be read as a bit of black comedy, but it is also a joke in which a woman exists mainly to be a victim. Some readers will take it as a harmless gag in the pulp tradition, while others will see it as an example of how casually the genre treated women's lives.

A third point is intellectual. The story treats Technocracy as a convenient butt of satire and does not engage with what the movement actually argued. A reader who knows the history may feel that it flattens a complicated and short-lived political phenomenon into a cartoon of bland contentment. Similarly, the central assumption that a society without crime would have nothing to write about is itself questionable: a world of equal credits might have plenty of stories about envy, grief, love and ambition. Stern's lack of imagination is partly the point, but it also exposes how narrow his imagination is, and the story does not quite decide whether we are meant to sympathize with him or laugh at him.

Finally, a reader might fairly object to the way the story uses its ending to avoid resolving anything. The final line is clever, but it is also a way of stopping rather than concluding. These objections do not make the story bad. They make it a minor piece of juvenilia, which is exactly what it is.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Don't Get Technatal sits at the edge of two traditions. One is the fan-written story, the playful, in-group fiction that grew up around the science fiction magazines of the thirties, when readers wrote letters, started clubs and then began publishing their own fiction. The other is the dystopian satire, the story that imagines a future society organized around one idea and shows the human cost.

The fan tradition explains the tone. Science fiction in the 1930s was a small, tightly knit community. Fans wrote to the magazines, met in local clubs like the Los Angeles Science Fantasy Society, and swapped homemade publications. Many of the professionals of the next generation, writers and editors alike, came out of that world, and Bradbury was one of them. The jokes in this story, about pulp writers, about plot shortages and about robot romances, are the jokes of insiders who knew the genre's habits.

The dystopian tradition explains the subject. Stories about planned, regulated societies go back at least to Edward Bellamy's Looking Backward, and they include Yevgeny Zamyatin's We, Aldous Huxley's Brave New World and, later, George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four. Huxley's novel, from 1932, is the closest relative: a world engineered for happiness in which conflict and art have been removed, and in which people are conditioned from birth. Bradbury's Technate belongs in the same family, though Bradbury's treatment is comic where Huxley's is chilly. The story also looks forward. Fahrenheit 451, with its fireman who burns books and its society that uses entertainment to keep people from thinking, is the grown-up version of the same worry. Even the idea of a writer finding the future's contentment unbearable is a thread in Bradbury's later fiction.

It is worth being clear about what this piece is not. It is not a work of literature that stands beside those novels. It is, as far as the record shows, an obscure piece of juvenilia whose main importance is that Bradbury wrote it. Why read it now? Because it takes three minutes, because it is funny in an old-fashioned way, and because it lets you see where a great storyteller began. It shows a teenager testing out a favorite theme, the fear that comfort can kill imagination, and learning how to end a story with a snap. Good writers do not arrive fully formed, and this little story is a reminder that even the greatest begin with a joke told to friends.
