Key Context & Why It Matters

Throughout the five-century Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910), whenever the royal court conducted a major state event—a coronation, royal wedding, state funeral, fortress construction, or diplomatic banquet—a temporary superintendency (dogam) compiled an illustrated documentary manual called an Uigwe ('Exemplary Protocol'). The finest single-copy editions, brushed on pure mulberry paper framed in red silk and bound with engraved brass hinges specifically for the king's eyes (Eoram-yong), were stored in the Oegyujanggak fortress archive on Ganghwa Island. Looted by Admiral Pierre-Gustave Roze's French expeditionary fleet during the 1866 Byeongin Yangyo and misfiled as 'Chinese books' in Paris for a century, 297 volumes were rediscovered in 1975 by Korean historian Dr. Park Byeong-seon and finally returned to Korea in 2011.

1. Bookbinding & Material Science: Why 'Eoram-yong' Volumes Outshine All Others

In the Joseon Uigwe hierarchy, a strict material distinction separated the working Bunsang-yong copies from the King's single Eoram-yong (어람용) copy. While Bunsang-yong copies used coarse paper, black ink lines, and rough iron spine plates, the Eoram-yong copy used triple-pounded chodo-ji mulberry paper (which absorbs mineral pigments without bleeding), cinnabar-red margin lines, natural indigo and malachite illustrations, green silk brocade covers, and non-corroding brass (sinchu) hinges—making each volume a masterwork of royal book arts. 2. Dr. Park Byeong-seon (1923–2011): The Scholar Who Rescued Two UNESCO Treasures

Few historians in modern history have matched the archival detective work of Dr. Park Byeong-seon.
