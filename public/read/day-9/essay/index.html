Nightly Essay • Philosophical Treatise

The Value of Philosophy

Bertrand Russell (1912) • 10 min read

Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves—because these questions enlarge our conception of what is possible and enrich our intellectual imagination.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Bertrand Russell wrote this piece as the last chapter of a short book called The Problems of Philosophy, published in 1912. Russell was already one of the most formidable minds in Britain. He had just finished, with Alfred North Whitehead, the huge and difficult Principia Mathematica, an attempt to build all of mathematics on pure logic. Yet here he was writing a small paperback for general readers, part of a series meant to put serious ideas into ordinary hands. The chapter answers a blunt question that many people ask: what is the point of philosophy? Philosophers never seem to settle anything, and it bakes no bread. Russell does not dodge this. He admits philosophy has few firm answers, and then argues that its value lies elsewhere, in what the questions do to the person asking them. It frees the mind from habit and self-absorption and makes it larger. The meaning, in a sentence, is that thinking about big questions is worth doing even without final answers, because it makes us bigger people.

Having now come to the end of our brief and very incomplete review of the problems of philosophy, it will be well to consider, in conclusion, what is the value of philosophy and why it ought to be studied. It is the more necessary to consider this question, in view of the fact that many men, under the influence of science or of practical affairs, are inclined to doubt whether philosophy is anything better than innocent but useless trifling, hair-splitting distinctions, and controversies on matters concerning which knowledge is impossible.

This view of philosophy appears to result, partly from a wrong conception of the ends of life, partly from a wrong conception of the kind of goods which philosophy strives to achieve. Physical science, through the medium of inventions, is useful to innumerable people who are wholly ignorant of it; thus the study of physical science is to be recommended, not only, or primarily, because of the effect on the student, but rather because of the effect on mankind in general. Thus utility does not belong to philosophy. If the study of philosophy has any value at all for others than students of philosophy, it must be only indirectly, through its effects upon the lives of those who study it. It is in these effects, therefore, if anywhere, that the value of philosophy must be primarily sought.

But further, if we are not to fail in our endeavour to determine the value of philosophy, we must first free our minds from the prejudices of what are wrongly called 'practical' men. The 'practical' man, as this word is often used, is one who recognizes only material needs, who realizes that men must have food for the body, but is oblivious of the necessity of providing food for the mind. If all men were well off, if poverty and disease had been reduced to their lowest possible point, there would still remain much to be done to produce a valuable society; and even in the existing world the goods of the mind are at least as important as the goods of the body. It is exclusively among the goods of the mind that the value of philosophy is to be found; and only those who are not indifferent to these goods can be persuaded that the study of philosophy is not a waste of time.

Philosophy, like all other studies, aims primarily at knowledge. The knowledge it aims at is the kind of knowledge which gives unity and system to the body of the sciences, and the kind which results from a critical examination of the grounds of our convictions, prejudices, and beliefs. But it cannot be maintained that philosophy has had any very great measure of success in its attempts to provide definite answers to its questions. If you ask a mathematician, a mineralogist, a historian, or any other man of learning, what definite body of truths has been ascertained by his science, his answer will last as long as you are willing to listen. But if you put the same question to a philosopher, he will, if he is candid, have to confess that his study has not achieved positive results such as have been achieved by other sciences. It is true that this is partly accounted for by the fact that, as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science. The whole study of the heavens, which now belongs to astronomy, was once included in philosophy; Newton's great work was called 'the mathematical principles of natural philosophy'. Similarly, the study of the human mind, which was a part of philosophy, has now been separated from philosophy and has become the science of psychology. Thus, to a great extent, the uncertainty of philosophy is more apparent than real: those questions which are already capable of definite answers are placed in the sciences, while those only to which, at present, no definite answer can be given, remain to form the residue which is called philosophy.

This is, however, only a part of the truth concerning the uncertainty of philosophy. There are many questions—and among them those that are of the profoundest interest to our spiritual life—which, so far as we can see, must remain insoluble to the human intellect unless its powers become of quite a different order from what they are now. Has the universe any unity of plan or purpose, or is it a fortuitous concourse of atoms? Is consciousness a permanent part of the universe, giving hope of indefinite growth in wisdom, or is it a transitory accident on a small planet on which life must ultimately become impossible? Are good and evil of importance to the universe or only to man? Such questions are asked by philosophy, and variously answered by various philosophers. But it would seem that, whether answers be otherwise discoverable or not, the answers suggested by philosophy are none of them demonstrably true. Yet, however slight may be the hope of discovering an answer, it is part of the business of philosophy to continue the consideration of such questions, to make us aware of their importance, to examine all the approaches to them, and to keep alive that speculative interest in the universe which is apt to be killed by confining ourselves to definitely ascertainable knowledge.

Many philosophers, it is true, have held that philosophy could establish the truth of certain answers to such fundamental questions. They have supposed that what is of most importance in religious beliefs could be proved by strict demonstration to be true. In order to judge of such attempts, it is necessary to take a survey of human knowledge, and to form an opinion as to its methods and its limitations. On such a subject it would be unwise to pronounce dogmatically; but if the investigations of our previous chapters have not led us astray, we shall be compelled to renounce the hope of finding philosophical proofs of religious beliefs. We cannot, therefore, include as part of the value of philosophy any definite set of answers to such questions. Hence, once more, the value of philosophy must not depend upon any supposed body of definitely ascertainable knowledge to be acquired by those who study it.

The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty. The man who has no tincture of philosophy goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices derived from common sense, from the habitual beliefs of his age or his nation, and from convictions which have grown up in his mind without the co-operation or consent of his deliberate reason. To such a man the world tends to become definite, finite, obvious; common objects rouse no questions, and unfamiliar possibilities are contemptuously rejected. As soon as we begin to philosophize, on the contrary, we find, as we saw in our opening chapters, that even the most everyday things lead to problems to which only very incomplete answers can be given. Philosophy, though unable to tell us with certainty what is the true answer to the doubts which it raises, is able to suggest many possibilities which enlarge our thoughts and free them from the tyranny of custom. Thus, while diminishing our feeling of certainty as to what things are, it greatly increases our knowledge as to what they may be; it removes the somewhat arrogant dogmatism of those who have never travelled into the region of liberating doubt, and it keeps alive our sense of wonder by showing familiar things in an unfamiliar aspect.

Apart from its utility in showing unsuspected possibilities, philosophy has a value—perhaps its chief value—through the greatness of the objects which it contemplates, and the freedom from narrow and personal aims resulting from this contemplation. The life of the instinctive man is shut up within the circle of his private interests: family and friends may be included, but the outer world is not regarded except as it may help or hinder what comes within the circle of instinctive wishes. In such a life there is something feverish and confined, in comparison with which the philosophic life is calm and free. The private world of instinctive interests is a small one, set in the midst of a great and powerful world which must, sooner or later, lay our private world in ruins. Unless we can so enlarge our interests as to include the whole outer world, we remain like a garrison in a beleaguered fortress, knowing that the enemy prevents escape and that ultimate surrender is inevitable. In such a life there is no peace, but a constant strife between the insistence of desire and the powerlessness of will. In one way or another, if our life is to be great and free, we must escape this prison and this strife.

One way of escape is by philosophic contemplation. Philosophic contemplation does not, in its widest survey, divide the universe into two hostile camps—friends and foes, helpful and hostile, good and bad—it views the whole impartially. Philosophic contemplation, when it is unalloyed, does not aim at proving that the rest of the universe is akin to man. All acquisition of knowledge is an enlargement of the Self, but this enlargement is best attained when it is not directly sought. It is obtained when the desire for knowledge is alone operative, by a study which does not wish in advance that its objects should have this or that character, but adapts the Self to the characters which it finds in its objects. This enlargement of Self is not obtained when, taking the Self as it is, we try to show that the world is so similar to this Self that knowledge of it is possible without any admission of what seems alien. The desire to prove this is a form of self-assertion and, like all self-assertion, it is an obstacle to the growth of Self which it desires, and of which the Self knows that it is capable. Self-assertion, in philosophic speculation as elsewhere, views the world as a means to its own ends; thus it makes the world of less account than Self, and the Self sets bounds to the greatness of its goods. In contemplation, on the contrary, we start from the not-Self, and through its greatness the boundaries of Self are enlarged; through the infinity of the universe the mind which contemplates it achieves some share in infinity.

For this reason greatness of soul is not fostered by those philosophies which assimilate the universe to Man. Knowledge is a form of union of Self and not-Self; like all union, it is impaired by dominion, and therefore by any attempt to force the universe into conformity with what we find in ourselves. There is a widespread philosophical tendency towards the view which tells us that Man is the measure of all things, that truth is man-made, that space and time and the world of universals are properties of the mind, and that, if there be anything not created by the mind, it is unknowable and of no account for us. This view, if our previous discussions were correct, is untrue; but in addition to being untrue, it has the effect of robbing philosophic contemplation of all that gives it value, since it fetters contemplation to Self. What it calls knowledge is not a union with the not-Self, but a set of prejudices, habits, and desires, making an impenetrable veil between us and the world beyond. The man who finds pleasure in such a theory of knowledge is like the man who never leaves the domestic circle for fear his word might not be law.

The true philosophic contemplation, on the contrary, finds its satisfaction in every enlargement of the not-Self, in everything that magnifies the objects contemplated, and thereby the subject contemplating. Everything, in contemplation, that is personal or private, everything that depends upon habit, self-interest, or desire, distorts the object, and hence impairs the union which the intellect seeks. By thus making a barrier between subject and object, such personal and private things become a prison to the intellect. The free intellect will see as God might see, without a here and now, without hopes and fears, without the trammels of customary beliefs and traditional prejudices, calmly, dispassionately, in the sole and exclusive desire of knowledge—knowledge as impersonal, as purely contemplative, as it is possible for man to attain. Hence also the free intellect will value more the abstract and universal knowledge into which the accidents of private history do not enter, than the knowledge brought by the senses, and dependent, as such knowledge must be, upon an exclusive and personal point of view and a body whose sense-organs distort as much as they reveal.

The mind which has become accustomed to the freedom and impartiality of philosophic contemplation will preserve something of the same freedom and impartiality in the world of action and emotion. It will view its purposes and desires as parts of the whole, with the absence of insistence that results from seeing them as infinitesimal fragments in a world of which all the rest is unaffected by any one man's deeds. The impartiality which, in contemplation, is the unalloyed desire for truth, is the very same quality of mind which, in action, is justice, and in emotion is that universal love which can be given to all, and not only to those who are judged useful or admirable. Thus contemplation enlarges not only the objects of our thoughts, but also the objects of our actions and our affections: it makes us citizens of the universe, not only of one walled city at war with all the rest. In this citizenship of the universe consists man's true freedom, and his liberation from the thraldom of narrow hopes and fears.

Thus, to sum up our discussion of the value of philosophy; Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions since no definite answers can, as a rule, be known to be true, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves; because these questions enlarge our conception of what is possible, enrich our intellectual imagination and diminish the dogmatic assurance which closes the mind against speculation; but above all because, through the greatness of the universe which philosophy contemplates, the mind also is rendered great, and becomes capable of that union with the universe which constitutes its highest good.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Russell begins by facing the strongest objection. Many people, he says, influenced by science or by practical affairs, suspect that philosophy is innocent but useless trifling, full of hair-splitting distinctions and quarrels about matters where knowledge is impossible. He does not wave this away. In fact he concedes a good deal of it. Philosophy, he says, has not had very great success in giving definite answers. Ask a mathematician or a historian what has been established in their field and the answer will go on as long as you care to listen. Ask a philosopher and, if he is honest, he will have to admit that his study has not achieved positive results like theirs.

His trick is to explain why. Whenever a question becomes answerable by definite knowledge, he says, it stops being called philosophy and becomes a separate science. The study of the heavens, once part of philosophy, became astronomy. The study of the mind, he says, is becoming psychology. So philosophy is left holding the questions that nobody can yet answer, and it seems unsuccessful only because it hands its successes to others. That is a witty reversal, and a useful one to keep in mind while reading.

Then comes the central claim. The value of philosophy is not to be found in a body of answers but in the uncertainty it produces and the effects that has on the mind. The man who has no tincture of philosophy, Russell says, goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices of common sense and the beliefs of his age and nation. The world of such a man is finite and obvious. Philosophy opens that door. It does not necessarily tell us what to think, but it shows us that familiar things have unfamiliar sides, and that our certainty was often only habit. It diminishes what he calls dogmatic assurance, the kind of confidence that shuts the mind against new possibilities.

The deepest layer concerns the self. Russell argues that a life shut up in private hopes and fears is like living in a small fortress surrounded by a great and powerful world. Sooner or later, the fortress falls. The way out is to widen the interests of the mind until the self is not the center. Contemplating the vast universe, he suggests, makes the mind itself great, and allows it to unite with what it contemplates.

He ends with a striking political image. The mind trained in impartial thought, he says, brings the same impartiality into action, where it becomes justice, and into feeling, where it becomes a universal love not reserved for those judged useful or admirable. Such a person is a citizen of the universe, not just of one walled city at war with all the rest. A first-time reader might miss that this is a moral argument as much as an intellectual one. Russell believes that open-minded thinking makes us kinder, freer people, and that this is the real, practical payoff of an apparently impractical subject.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The chapter is an unusually good piece of persuasion, and its first merit is its honesty. Defenders of any subject are tempted to claim that it offers solid results. Russell begins by granting that philosophy has few. That concession buys him a great deal of credibility, so that when he turns to what philosophy does offer, the reader is already inclined to listen. His reply to the practical man, that goods of the mind are at least as important as goods of the body, is stated plainly without bluster.

The prose is also a model of clear explanation. Russell writes long, balanced sentences that never lose their grip, and his images are simple and strong: the prison of prejudice, the fortress of the self, the walled city at war with all the rest. He was a master of a certain kind of English, lucid, ironic and dignified, and this chapter shows it at a high pitch. The final summary sentence is nearly a prayer, and it rounds the book off with real emotional force.

Still, the argument has weaknesses that an honest reading should name. The claim that all answerable questions leave philosophy and become sciences is neat but debatable. It treats philosophy as a leftover bin, and it ignores that some areas, such as ethics and the study of meaning, have remained philosophical without being merely unanswered. Some philosophers would say that the subject has methods and results of its own, even if they are subtler than those of chemistry.

The ideal of the free intellect that sees as God might see, without a here and now, without hopes and fears, is also open to question. It is a lofty picture, and arguably an impossible one. Humans cannot step wholly outside their own perspective, and some of what Russell calls personal distortion may be a source of insight rather than just a barrier. His contrast between impersonal, contemplative knowledge and the supposedly distorting knowledge of the senses reflects a view of the mind that later philosophy has questioned.

There is also a faint note of intellectual elitism. The language of the practical man, to whom he is a bit dismissive, risks caricature, and the assumption that contemplation is the highest good may sound like the preference of someone who has the leisure for it. Even so, the essay's moral pull is strong. It works because it states, with great simplicity, a conviction most readers recognize from their own experience: that a mind with larger questions is larger, and that being sure of everything is a poor way to live. The weaknesses are real, but they are the weaknesses of a noble ambition, and the clarity of the writing keeps even the shakier passages honest and open to challenge.

III. The Impact It Had

The Problems of Philosophy became one of the most widely read introductions to the subject ever written. It appeared in 1912 in the Home University Library, a series of inexpensive books designed to bring learning to general readers, and it has stayed in print for more than a century. For many students, it is the first book of philosophy they open, and the final chapter is the part most often remembered and quoted. As a result, its argument about the value of philosophy has become something like a standard defense in the debate over why anyone should study the subject.

For Russell himself, the book marks a moment in a long, turbulent career. In the years after it, he went on to become a leading figure in the analytic tradition, a style of philosophy that stresses clarity, logic and careful argument. His student Ludwig Wittgenstein, who arrived in Cambridge in 1911, soon began to challenge and shake his views, and Russell later described how powerful that criticism was. Russell's public life also expanded dramatically. He opposed the First World War and was sent to prison for it in 1918, lost his Cambridge lectureship, campaigned for peace and nuclear disarmament, and wrote widely for the public. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. This little chapter, with its call to be a citizen of the universe, anticipates the broad humane spirit of his later public work.

The influence on teaching is lasting. Philosophy departments often cite the chapter when explaining the point of their discipline to skeptical students and administrators. When critics ask what the humanities are for, defenders frequently reach for Russell's reply that the value of such study lies in the way it shapes the mind, and not in a measurable product. It is a classic text in debates about liberal education.

It has also influenced popular thought. The idea that philosophy is valuable for the questions rather than the answers has become a commonplace in public discussions of the subject. Writers who introduce philosophy to general audiences, from Will Durant to Jostein Gaarder in Sophie's World to the many modern authors of accessible guides, work in the spirit Russell set out, offering a way of thinking rather than a catalog of conclusions.

Finally, its emphasis on intellectual humility has fed into broader cultural conversations about open-mindedness, critical thinking and the dangers of dogma. The line about diminishing dogmatic assurance is a favorite of those who want to defend doubt as a virtue, in science, politics and everyday life, though Russell would likely add that it should not collapse into the belief that nothing can be known.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this chapter first for the way it relieves a nagging worry. Many people who are drawn to big questions feel embarrassed about it, as though they were wasting time on something that will never pay. Russell tells them that the questions are worth asking and that the uncertainty is not a failure but a feature. For students who have just finished a course and wonder what it was for, the chapter reads like a graceful answer.

They also love the writing. Russell was among the best prose stylists of the twentieth century, and this chapter shows why. The sentences are clear, the images are memorable, and the tone is both authoritative and modest. He is able to explain a difficult idea without simplifying it into falsehood. Many readers have said that his clarity is the greatest gift of the book, since it makes a hard subject feel like an open invitation, not a locked room.

There is a moving quality to the vision of freedom at the end. The idea of becoming a citizen of the universe, instead of one walled city at war with all the rest, appeals to people who feel the claustrophobia of small worries and narrow loyalties. In the years of Russell's life that followed, years of world war and nuclear anxiety, that ideal of wide sympathy took on extra weight. Readers coming to the passage in difficult times often find it consoling and enlarging.

The essay also wins affection for its courage in saying that the greatest benefit of philosophy is indirect. It rejects the language of usefulness that dominates much of modern life, yet it does so without sneering at ordinary concerns. He grants that physical science is hugely useful and that the practical man is right about the importance of food and health. What he insists on is that this is not the whole story. Readers who feel the same insistence in themselves are glad to find it so well put.

Finally, many appreciate the sense of intellectual generosity. Russell does not pretend to have the final answers, and he does not require the reader to agree with him on any particular philosophical doctrine. He asks only for willingness to wonder. That invitation, offered at the end of a book that has surveyed some very hard problems, feels like a gift. People return to the final paragraphs again and again, as one returns to a favorite piece of music, because they give shape to a hope: that thought can make a person larger.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critics have raised several serious objections to Russell's argument, and it is worth stating them fairly. The first is that his picture of philosophy is too narrow. By describing it as the home of questions not yet answered by science, he makes it look like a waiting room. Later philosophers, including those influenced by Wittgenstein and by the ordinary language tradition, argued that many philosophical problems are not scientific problems in disguise, but puzzles about how our concepts and language work. On their view philosophy is a different kind of activity, not a failed forerunner of science.

A second objection concerns the ideal of impersonal contemplation. Russell's talk of seeing as God might see, without a here and now, strikes many modern readers as a fantasy of a view from nowhere. Feminist thinkers, phenomenologists and others have argued that knowledge always comes from somewhere, from a body and a history, and that pretending otherwise can hide the interests of the person who claims to speak for everyone. Russell's strong contrast between impersonal and personal knowledge, and his suspicion of the senses, reflects assumptions that have been challenged.

A third line of criticism points to the book's context. Russell wrote The Problems of Philosophy in 1911 and 1912, and some of his own views changed rapidly afterward. Under the pressure of Wittgenstein's objections to his theory of judgment, he abandoned parts of the book's philosophy of knowledge, and in later decades he came to regard its views with some distance. So a reader should not take the book as his final word.

Practical-minded critics, for their part, may say that Russell does not really answer the practical man. He dismisses the demand for usefulness by changing the subject to the goods of the mind, and some see that as a defensive move. If a society has limited resources, they ask, why should it pay for subjects whose value is so hard to measure? Russell's reply is eloquent, but it is addressed more to people already inclined to agree.

There is also the criticism of tone. The writing carries the assurance of a privileged Cambridge intellectual, and some readers find the contempt for custom and common sense a bit lofty. Common sense, after all, is not always prejudice. It often encodes hard-won experience. A fair reading is that Russell is right to demand that we question our habits, but that he may be too quick to treat tradition and ordinary belief as chains rather than as sources of wisdom.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This chapter belongs to the ancient tradition of the defense of philosophy, which runs back to Socrates, who said that the unexamined life is not worth living. Plato's image of prisoners in a cave who mistake shadows for reality is a distant ancestor of Russell's image of the person imprisoned in common-sense prejudice. The Stoics, who spoke of being citizens of the world, are the ancestors of his citizen of the universe. Russell, however, is a modern, writing in the age of science, and his task is to explain why contemplation still matters in a world impressed by machines.

Within modern philosophy, he stands at the beginning of the analytic movement. His earlier work with Whitehead on logic, and his collaboration and quarrels with G. E. Moore and later Wittgenstein, helped define a school that prized rigor and clarity. In his own time, he was reacting against the British idealism of thinkers like F. H. Bradley, who held that the world is a single spiritual whole. The Problems of Philosophy defends a more down-to-earth realism, while keeping the idealists' sense that philosophy should expand the mind.

The chapter also belongs to a broader literature of the humanities defending themselves. It sits beside earlier arguments for the idea of a university, such as John Henry Newman's, and anticipates more recent defenses of the liberal arts in an age of vocational training. Writers like Martha Nussbaum, in her arguments for the importance of humanistic education for democracy, carry the idea of the citizen of the world forward. Carl Sagan's way of making cosmic scale a source of human humility and wonder is another descendant.

Compared with essays like Huxley's lecture on a piece of chalk, which defends the value of scientific method, Russell's chapter defends the value of questions that science cannot yet settle. The two are friendly rivals, and both insist that education should open the mind and not merely train the hand. Compared with the more pessimistic strands of twentieth-century thought, Russell's essay is hopeful, believing that the free use of reason can enlarge life.

Why read it now? Because the pressure to justify every pursuit by its immediate payoff has hardly lessened, and because the temptation to be certain, in politics and in private life, is as strong as ever. Russell offers a calm alternative. He shows that we can live without final answers and be better for it. Even if you reject some of his claims, the chapter is a short, beautiful reminder that curiosity is not a luxury but one of the ways a human being grows up.
