The Piper

Ray Bradbury (1943) • 7 min read

“Lord! He’s there again! He’s there! Look!” the old man croaked, jabbing a calloused finger at the burial hill. “Old Piper again! As crazy as a loon! Every year that way!”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Piper appeared in the pulp magazine Thrilling Wonder Stories in early 1943, in the middle of the Second World War, when Ray Bradbury was twenty-two and still learning his trade by selling stories to magazines. Mars was already one of his favorite places to write about, years before The Martian Chronicles made it famous. Here it is a dying world, owned by Earth, dotted with a few mining towns, and haunted by a strange figure who stands on a burial hill and plays a pipe all night. The story borrows its mood from the old legend of the Pied Piper, who lured rats and then children away with music, and it quotes a line from the English poet William Blake. Behind its melodrama lies a real worry of the time: what happens when a powerful people takes what it wants from others, and what might those others do in return. It is a short fable about music, greed, and revenge.

“Lord! He’s there again! He’s there! Look!” the old man croaked, jabbing a calloused finger at the burial hill. “Old Piper again! As crazy as a loon! Every year that way!”

The Martian boy at the feet of the old man stirred his thin reddish feet in the soil and affixed his large green eyes upon the burial hill where the Piper stood. “Why does he do that?” asked the boy.

“Ah?” The old man’s leathery face rumpled into a maze of wrinkles. “He’s crazy, that’s what. Stands up there piping on his music from sunset until dawn.”

The thin piping sounds squealed in the dusk, echoed back from the low hills, were lost in melancholy silence, fading. Then louder, higher, insanely, crying with shrill voice.

The Piper was a tall, gaunt man, face as pale and wan as Martian moons, eyes electrical purple, standing against the soft of the dusking heaven, holding his pipe to his lips, playing. The Piper⁠—a silhouette⁠—a symbol⁠—a melody.

“Where did the Piper come from?” asked the Martian boy.

“From Venus.” The old man took out his pipe and filled it. “Oh, some twenty years ago or more, on the projectile with the Terrestrians. I arrived on the same ship, coming from Earth, we shared a double seat together.”

“What is his name?” Again the boyish, eager voice.

“I can’t remember. I don’t think I ever knew, really.”

A vague rustling sound came into existence. The Piper continued playing, paying no heed to it. From the darkness, across the star-jewelled horizon, came mysterious shapes, creeping, creeping.

“Mars is a dying world,” the old man said. “Nothing ever happens of much gravity. The Piper, I believe, is an exile.”

The stars trembled like reflections in water, dancing with the music.

“An exile.” The old man continued. “Something like a leper. They called him the brilliant. He was the epitome of all Venerian culture until the Earthmen came with their greedy incorporations and licentious harlots. The Earthlings outlawed him, sent him here to Mars to live out his days.”

“Mars is a dying world,” repeated the boy. “A dying world. How many Martians are there, sir?”

The old man chuckled. “I guess maybe you are the last pure Martian alive, boy. But there are millions of others.”

“Where do they live? I have never seen them.”

“You are young. You have much to see, much to learn.”

“Where do they live?”

“Out there, beyond the mountains, beyond the dead sea bottoms, over the horizon and to the north, in the caves, far back in the subterrane.”

“Why?”

“Why? Now that’s hard to say. They were a brilliant race once upon a time. But something happened to them, hybrided them. They are unintelligent creatures now, cruel beasts.”

“Does Earth own Mars?” The little boy’s eyes were riveted upon the glowing planet overhead, the green planet.

“Yes, all of Mars. Earth has three cities here, each containing one thousand people. The closest city is a mile from here, down the road, a group of small metal bubble-like buildings. The men from Earth move about among the buildings like ants enclosed in their space suits. They are miners. With their huge machines they rip open the bowels of our planet and dig out our precious lifeblood from the mineral arteries.”

“Is that all?”

“That is all.” The old man shook his head sadly. “No culture, no art, no purpose. Greedy, hopeless Earthlings.”

“And the other two cities⁠—where are they?”

“One is up the same cobbled road five miles, the third is further still by some five hundred miles.”

“I am glad I live here with you, alone.” The boy’s head nodded sleepily. “I do not like the men from Terra. They are despoilers.”

“They have always been. But someday,” said the old man, “they will meet their doom. They have blasphemed enough, have they. They cannot own planets as they have and expect nothing but greedy luxury for their sluggishly squat bodies. Someday⁠—!” His voice rose high, in tempo and pitch with the Piper’s wild music.

Wild music, insane music, stirring music. Music to stir the savage into life. Music to effect man’s destiny!

“Wild-eyed Piper on the hill, Crying out your rigadoons, Bring the savages to kill ’Neath the waning Martian moons!”

“What is that?” asked the boy.

“A poem,” said the old man. “A poem I have written in the last few days. I feel something is going to happen very soon. The Piper’s song is growing more insistent every night. At first, twenty years ago, he played on only a few nights of every year, but now, for the last three years he has played until dawn every night of every autumn when the planet is dying.”

“Bring the savages?” the boy sat up. “What savages?”

“There!”

Along the star-glimmered mountain tops a vast clustering herd of black, murmuring, advancing. The music screamed higher and higher.

“Piper, pipe that song again! So he piped, I wept to hear.”

“More of the poem?” asked the boy.

“Not my poem⁠—but a poem from Earth some seventy years ago. I learned it in school.”

“Music is strange.” The little boy’s eyes were scintillant with thought. “It warms me inside. This music makes me angry. Why?”

“Because it is music with a purpose.”

“What purpose?”

“We shall know by dawn.

“Music is the language of all things⁠—intelligent or not, savage or educated civilian. This Piper knows his music as a god knows his heaven. For twenty years he has composed his hymn of action and hate and finally, tonight perhaps, the finale will be reached. At first, many years ago, when he played, he received no answer from the subterrane, but the murmur of gibbering voices. Five years ago he lured the voices and the creatures from their caves to the mountain tops. Tonight, for the first time, the herd of black will spill over the trails toward our hovel, toward the road, toward the cities of man!”

Music screaming, higher, faster, insanely, sending shock after macabre shock through night air, loosening the stars from their riveted stations. The Piper stretched high, six feet or more, upon his hillock, swaying back and forth, his thin shape attired in brown-cloth. The black mass on the mountain came down like amoebic tentacles, met and coalesced, muttering and mumbling. “Go inside and hide,” said the old man. “You are young, you must live to propagate the new Mars. Tonight is the end of the old, tomorrow begins the new! It is death for the men of Earth!” Higher still and higher. “Death! They come to overrun the Earthlings, destroy their cities, take their projectiles. Then⁠—in the ships of man⁠—to Earth! Turnabout! Revolution and Revenge! A new civilization! When monsters usurp men and men’s greediness crumbles at his demise!” Shriller, faster, higher, insanely tempoed. “The Piper⁠—The Brilliant One⁠—He who has waited for years for this night. Back to Venus to reinstall the glory of his civilization! The return of Art to humanity!”

“But they are savages, these unpure Martians,” the boy cried.

“Men are savages. I am ashamed of being a man,” the old man said, tremblingly. “Yes, these creatures are savages, but they will learn⁠—these brutes⁠—with music. Music in many forms⁠—music for peace, music for love⁠—music for hate and music for death. The Piper and his brood will set up a new cosmos. He is immortal!” Now, hurrying, muttering up the road, the first cluster of black things reminiscent of men. A strange sharp odor in the air. The Piper, from his hillock, walking down the road, over the cobbles, to the city. “Piper, pipe that song again!” cried the old man. “Go and kill and live again! Bring us love and art again! Piper, pipe the song! I weep!” Then: “Hide, child, hide quickly! Before they come! Hurry!” And the child, crying, hurried to the small house and hid himself through the night.

Swirling, jumping, running, leaping, gamboling, crying⁠—the new humanity surged to man’s cities, his rockets, his mines. The Piper’s song! Stars shuddered. Winds stilled. Nightbirds sang no songs. Echoes murmured only the voices of the ones who advanced, bringing new understanding. The old man, caught in the whirlpool of ebon, was swept down, screaming. Then up the road, by the awful thousands, vomiting out of hills, sprawling from caves, curling, huge fingers of beasts, around and about and down to the Man Cities. Sighing, leaping up, voices and destruction!

Rockets across the sky!

Guns. Death.

And finally, in the pale advancement of dawn, the memory, the echoing of the old man’s voice. And the little boy arose to start afresh a new world with a new mate.

Echoing, the old man’s voice:

“Piper, pipe that song again! So he piped, I wept to hear!”

A new day dawned.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The Piper begins with a scene that could belong to a folk tale. An old man and a young Martian boy sit watching a tall, pale figure on a burial hill, playing a pipe from sunset until dawn. The old man calls him crazy, but as the conversation continues we learn that the Piper is a Venusian exile, once the glory of his culture, sent to Mars after Earthmen arrived with what the old man calls their greedy incorporations. Earth now owns Mars. Three small cities of a thousand people each dig minerals out of the ground with giant machines. They have, the old man says, no culture, no art, no purpose.

Everything in the story is about what the music is doing. The old man explains that the Piper has been playing for twenty years, and that he has slowly drawn the degraded Martian creatures out of their caves in the mountains. These creatures, we are told, were once a brilliant race but have been hybridized and made savage. The music grows more insistent every night, and by the end of the story the whole herd comes pouring down from the hills to destroy the Earth cities. The old man, who has been cheering this on, is himself swept under by the horde and killed. The boy, hidden in the house, survives to begin a new world with a new mate.

Several layers lie under this plot. The first is that the story is a fable about colonial exploitation. Earth has taken everything and given nothing, and the story imagines the colonized rising up. The second is that music is treated as a force that can reach beyond intelligence. The old man says that music is the language of all things, intelligent or not, and the boy notices that it makes him angry and asks why. Because, comes the answer, it is music with a purpose. Bradbury is playing with the idea that art can move people to action without argument, for good or for horror.

The third layer is the poetry. The old man recites a rhyme he has written, and then a line from an older poem, Piper, pipe that song again, which is taken from William Blake's Songs of Innocence, published in 1789, though the story says it is from Earth some seventy years ago. In Blake the line is the opening of a gentle book of songs, where a child asks a piper to play again and the piper plays, and the child weeps for joy. Bradbury twists that tenderness into a battle cry. The old man weeps too, but he weeps for a massacre.

What the story seems to be asking is whether art can be separated from power. The Piper is described as the one who will bring back the return of Art to humanity, yet he does it through slaughter. The ending, with its calm line about a new day dawning, leaves the reader to feel both the grandeur and the horror of that kind of rebirth.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Piper is a story of striking atmosphere and uneven execution. It has a few passages of real power, and many of the weaknesses that mark Bradbury's early pulp work.

The strongest thing in it is the image of the Piper himself, a gaunt silhouette on a hill with a face as pale as the Martian moons and eyes of electrical purple. The line that calls him a silhouette, a symbol, and a melody does a great deal in a few words. The music is rendered in sentences that quicken and rise, with repeated words such as higher and faster and insanely, so that the prose imitates the thing it describes. When the black herd comes down the hills like amoebic tentacles, and the stars tremble and the nightbirds fall silent, the story reaches a pitch of eerie lyricism that points directly to the later Martian stories.

But the story is held back by its structure. Almost everything is delivered through a long monologue by the old man, who explains the Piper's history, the Martians' decline, the greed of Earth, and what is about to happen. The boy is mostly a device for asking questions. This is information delivered by lecture, and it drains the tension out of the middle. There is also a confusing quality to the world. The old man says he came from Earth and shared a seat on the ship with the Piper, yet he speaks of Earthmen as despoilers and says he is ashamed of being a man. The Piper is from Venus, yet he leads the Martians. The boy is called the last pure Martian, while the creatures in the caves are called unpure. The cast and their loyalties are muddled, and the story relies on the reader to go along with a series of declarations.

The language can also be overheated. Phrases such as sluggishly squat bodies, licentious harlots, and brutes bring in a shrill, almost scolding tone. The old man's rhyme, with its rigadoons and waning Martian moons, is not good verse, and the stanza reads like a pastiche of Poe. A modern reader will wince at these moments, though perhaps the story intends the poem to be a bit crude, as the work of an amateur.

The most troubling craft problem concerns the moral center. The story wants us to feel the thrill of the uprising and the pity of the old man, but it also invites us to see the massacre as a kind of justice. The final scenes are told in a rush of fragments, such as Guns. Death. Rockets across the sky, and we never see anyone suffer in a personal way. It is more a tone poem than a story.

Still, as a piece of mood and ambition, it works in places. The first third, with the boy and the old man in the dusk, is genuinely evocative, and the idea of music as a slow weapon is a good one that lingers.

III. The Impact It Had

The Piper is not one of Bradbury's celebrated stories, and it would be wrong to say it had a wide cultural impact. Its value lies in what it shows about the development of his Mars, and in the way it prefigures themes that he would later handle with far greater subtlety.

In the early 1940s, Bradbury wrote a number of stories set on Mars for the pulps. In many of them the planet was a stage for adventure, with canals, ancient ruins, and strange creatures. The Piper is different in that Mars is already melancholy. The old man says it is a dying world where nothing of much gravity ever happens, which is very close to the mood of The Martian Chronicles. In that later book, published in 1950, Bradbury would turn Mars into a mirror in which Earth could see its own greed, loneliness, and nostalgia. The notion that Earthlings arrive as despoilers, rip open the planet for its minerals, and bring noise and commerce to a delicate civilization is already fully present in this story, though in a rough form.

The Piper also illustrates a recurring Bradbury move: the artist as a dangerous figure. In Fahrenheit 451, books are burned because they have the power to disturb people. In The Piper, music has that same power, and a single musician can summon an army. Bradbury, who believed deeply in the power of imagination, was also fascinated by its risks, and he returned to the idea that art can carry a kind of violence.

Beyond that, the story has had a quiet life among readers of Golden Age science fiction. It has been reprinted in retrospective collections of his early work, and it appears in discussions of his Martian writing as an example of how he moved from adventure toward elegy. It is rarely taught in classrooms, and no major adaptation exists as far as I am aware. The Pied Piper tradition it draws on, though, has been continually revived in science fiction, and the story is a small entry in that line.

For the field as a whole, the story is part of the long wave of fiction in which a colonized people turn the tables on their conquerors. In 1943, with the world at war and empires under strain, that idea carried a charge that is easy to underestimate. The story did not change the conversation, but it joined it, and it gave Bradbury an early chance to test a mood that would become his trademark.

IV. What Readers Loved

For an obscure early story, the record of public reaction is thin, so it is best to speak of what readers tend to respond to rather than to cite particular reviews. Those who have written about it in the context of Bradbury's early career point to a handful of pleasures.

The first is its sense of atmosphere. Even a reader unfamiliar with Bradbury will notice how quickly the opening scene creates a mood. The old man with his pipe, the boy with his large green eyes, the burial hill and the thin piping squealing in the dusk make a small stage lit by the stars. The writing has a tone of dream and prophecy that readers often recall long after the plot has faded. Bradbury has always been admired for his ability to make a landscape feel like a feeling, and this story gives an early hint of that gift.

The second is the idea of music as a weapon. Many people feel the force of music in their own lives, how a certain song can make them angry, tender, or restless without any words. The boy's remark that the music warms him inside and makes him angry, and does not know why, rings true. The reader recognizes that experience and enjoys seeing it carried to a fantastic extreme. The old tale of the Pied Piper has a deep hold on the imagination, and its use here as a story of revolution gives it a fresh and unsettling twist.

The third is the poetic ambition. The quotation from Blake adds a literary depth that sets the story apart from many pulp tales of the same period. Readers who know Blake's gentle verse feel the shock of seeing his words turned toward bloodshed. Even readers who do not know the source may sense that the refrain, with its mingled joy and weeping, belongs to an older and sadder world.

Finally, there is the bold shape of the ending. The story does not hedge. It lets the uprising happen, it lets the old man die in the flood of the creatures he has cheered on, and it leaves a child to begin again. The fable-like quality, with its sharp reversal and its sense of a closing cycle, has a stark appeal. Admirers of early Bradbury enjoy it as an experiment, a story where the author tries to be a poet and a prophet at once, and succeeds in a few unforgettable moments.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

As with most of Bradbury's early pulp work, there is no famous body of criticism devoted to The Piper. What follows are the objections that arise most naturally from the story itself, and that thoughtful readers have tended to raise about early work like it.

The most serious is its handling of race and savagery. The story depends on the idea that the Martians in the caves have been hybridized and have become unintelligent creatures, cruel beasts. The language of purity and impurity, the repeated word savages, and the casual cheering of a massacre come from a period when such vocabulary was common in adventure fiction, but they read very uncomfortably now. The story wants to criticize the savagery of Earthmen, and the old man says that men are savages, but it also presents a mass slaughter as a kind of liberation, and it does so with a thrilled and almost admiring tone. A reader may fairly feel that the story has not thought through the implications of its own imagery.

A second objection is that the moral logic is confused. The old man condemns Earth's greed and its lack of art, but he welcomes a murderous horde as the bringer of new understanding. The Piper is called brilliant and immortal, though he is leading a campaign of killing. The idea that music will teach the brutes, with music for peace and music for love and music for hate and music for death, is attractive, but the story never shows any peace or love. It shows only the hate. This leaves the reader unsure whether the tale is a celebration or a warning.

A third objection concerns the plotting. As noted, the story is mostly told as a monologue, and the characters are not individuals. The Piper never speaks, the Martian boy is a blank, and the old man is a mouthpiece. The thin world-building, with its three cities and its vague caves, feels more like a backdrop than a place.

Finally, some readers find the story overwrought. Its exclamations, its long lists of verbs, and its insistence on the power of the music can become tiresome. The style of the pulps rewarded intensity, and Bradbury at twenty-two had not yet learned the restraint that makes his later work so moving. It is a story that rises to a scream and stays there, and for some readers the scream is more fatiguing than frightening.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Piper stands at the junction of several older traditions, and seeing them helps explain both what it attempts and why it is only partly successful.

The first is the Pied Piper legend, the German tale of a stranger who pipes away the rats of Hamelin and, when the townspeople refuse to pay, pipes away their children. Robert Browning gave it a famous English form in 1842. The core of the legend is that music can enchant, and that a broken promise brings terrible payment. Bradbury's Piper has the same quality of exile and grievance, and the tale of the unpaid debt is echoed in the Earthmen's greedy taking.

The second is the Romantic tradition of the poet as prophet, represented here by the borrowed lines of William Blake. For Blake, the piper was an image of joyful inspiration, and the child who asks for the song again stands for innocent delight. Bradbury's reversal turns that innocence into something dangerous, and the contrast gives the story a bitter resonance.

The third is the science fiction tradition of the colonized world. H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds had imagined Earth invaded as Europeans had invaded others, and many later writers took up the idea of reversing the roles. Bradbury's Mars stories, from this one to The Martian Chronicles, belong to that line, and in the later book the theme is handled with far more compassion. Later writers such as Ursula Le Guin, in The Word for World Is Forest, and Kim Stanley Robinson, in his Mars trilogy, went further in imagining the moral costs of settling other planets. The Piper is a distant ancestor of those works.

It also anticipates the fear that runs through Bradbury's best-known novel. In Fahrenheit 451, the power of words and stories is feared by authorities. Here the power of music is feared and used. The thought that art is never harmless runs through his career.

Why read it today? Because it shows a young writer with a deep poetic instinct trying to fit it into the pulp mold. It is flawed, and some of its language is hard to accept, but it contains a real image, a lone figure on a hill whose song is slowly drawing the dispossessed down from the mountains. For a reader who wants to understand how the later, more humane Mars of The Martian Chronicles came to be, this is a useful and sometimes striking early draft.
