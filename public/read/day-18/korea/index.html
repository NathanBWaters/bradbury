Seonyudo Island Park: How a Concrete Water-Treatment Plant Became an Aquatic Lotus Ruin

Korea — On the Han River island once painted by Jeong Seon, landscape architect Jeong Young-sun turned 1978 chemical settling basins into self-cleaning botanical wetlands (Joseon True-View Era to Contemporary Seoul (1740s / 1978–2002)) • 9 min read

“Do not erase the scars of the twentieth century; let ivy climb the concrete columns and let lotus roots purify the water where chlorine once flowed.” — Jeong Young-sun, Seonyudo Design Notes (2001)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Anchored in the middle of the Han River between Mapo and Yeongdeungpo under the arch of Yanghwa Bridge, Seonyudo ('Island of the Immortals' Play') was once a picturesque granite peak celebrated by eighteenth-century Joseon landscape painter Jeong Seon (Gyeomjae). During the twentieth century, Japanese colonial engineers and post-war planners blasted the entire granite mountain into gravel to build flood levees and highways, then in 1978 covered the flattened island with a concrete chemical water-purification plant supplying southwestern Seoul. When the plant closed in 2000, pioneering landscape architect Jeong Young-sun and architect Joh Sung-yong refused to demolish the concrete tanks, transforming the industrial ruins into Korea's first environmental regeneration park in 2002.

Walk across the graceful wooden arch of the Seonyugyo pedestrian footbridge spanning the southern channel of the Han River in western Seoul, and step down onto an island that looks like a sunken Mayan temple overgrown by a river forest. Weathered gray reinforced-concrete columns rise out of shallow reflecting pools carpeted with water lilies; Virginia creeper and Boston ivy drape forty-foot concrete walls whose rebar scars are left open to the rain; and dragonflies hover over terraced concrete basins where reeds and lotus roots filter river water. This is Seonyudo Park, opened in April 2002 as South Korea’s first post-industrial ecological park.

Few places in Seoul compress three centuries of beauty, violence, and ecological redemption into twenty-seven acres as vividly as Seonyudo. During the Joseon Dynasty, Seonyudo was not a flat island at all, but Seonyubong —a dramatic forty-meter-high conical peak of pale granite rising out of the crystalline waters of the Han River alongside twin rocky cliffs known as rocks of the immortals. Poets and scholars sailed wooden ferries out to the island’s pavilion (Seonyujeong) to watch the sunset over the West Sea, and in the 1740s the greatest master of Joseon Jingyeong Sansuhwa (True-View Landscape Painting), Jeong Seon (pen name Gyeomjae, 1676–1759), immortalized the soaring granite crags and pine-shaded cliffs in his album Yangcheong Punggyeong.

The twentieth century systematically erased Jeong Seon’s mountain. After the catastrophic Eulchuk Han River flood of July 1925, Japanese colonial engineers turned Seonyubong into a rock quarry, dynamiting its granite cliffs to build riverside embankments and roadbeds for the 1930s Je-il Hanganggyo bridge. In the 1960s, as post-war Seoul paved highways along the riverbanks and constructed the Second Han River Bridge (Yanghwadaegyo) directly across the island’s spine in 1965, the last remnants of the granite peak were blasted flat. Then, between 1978 and 2000, the Seoul Metropolitan Government converted the entire flattened island into the Seonyu Water Purification Plant —an industrial fortress of massive concrete settling tanks, rapid sand filters, chemical coagulant dosing chambers, and underground treated-water reservoirs pumping 400,000 tons of tap water daily to households across southwestern Seoul.

When the city consolidated its water infrastructure in December 2000 and decommissioned the Seonyu plant, conventional municipal habit called for bringing in wrecking balls, pulverizing the concrete tanks into landfill, and planting a manicured lawn with imported flowerbeds. Instead, landscape architect Jeong Young-sun (b. 1941, founder of SeoAhn Total Landscape and the first Korean recipient of the IFLA Sir Geoffrey Jellicoe Award in 2023) and architect Joh Sung-yong proposed a radical act of architectural memory. Why pretend the twentieth century never happened? Jeong Young-sun argued that demolishing the 1978 water plant would waste embodied carbon, generate thousands of tons of toxic concrete dust, and repeat the exact erasure that had destroyed Seonyubong’s granite peak in the 1920s. Instead, she and Joh cut open the concrete lids of the industrial tanks like surgical archaeologists, leaving the raw concrete structural grids standing as modern ruins while turning their basins into living botanical laboratories.

Consider the Garden of Green Columns (Noksaek Gidung-ui Jeongwon). Originally an underground concrete reservoir that held treated drinking water in pitch darkness, the structure had its heavy concrete roof slab carefully sawed away, leaving behind thirty parallel concrete columns standing in two rows like a Roman forum. Jeong planted evergreen spindle vines (Euonymus fortunei) and deciduous ivy at the base of each column so the gray concrete pillars turned into living vertical columns of green in summer and crimson in autumn. Immediately adjacent lies the heart of the island’s ecological machinery: the Water Purification Garden (Sujil Jeonghwa-won) and the Aquatic Botanical Garden (Susaeng Singmul-won). What had once been the plant’s chemical coagulation and sedimentation basins—where aluminum sulfate and liquid chlorine were dumped into raw river water—was re-engineered into a chemical-free phytoremediation cascade.

Water pumped from the Han River enters the upper concrete troughs and flows slowly across stepped gravel beds planted with over 10,000 native aquatic plants: water dropwort (minari), sweet flag iris (changpo), common reeds (galdae), cattails (budeul), and floating lotus (yeonkkot). As the river water trickles past their submerged root rhizomes, symbiotic microbial biofilms living on the plant roots digest dissolved nitrates, phosphates, and heavy metals, discharging crystal-clear water into the lower play pools where children wade barefoot. In the Garden of Time (Sigan-ui Jeongwon), the former rapid-precipitation tanks were sliced into deep, sunken concrete canyons five meters below grade. Because the thick concrete walls block the wind, hold moisture, and create distinct vertical microclimates from the shady, damp floor to the sunlit upper rim, Jeong Young-sun planted 118 native Korean woodland species—ferns, mosses, bamboo groves, wild ginger, and hornbeams—that colonized the raw concrete cracks as if a mountain gorge had reclaimed a factory.

Notice how Joh Sung-yong and Jeong Young-sun handled the pathways: rather than pouring fresh asphalt over the island, they routed elevated wooden boardwalks and corroded steel grates across the top edges of the old settling walls so visitors walk along the exact catwalks where water-plant engineers once checked chemical gauges—looking down into birch groves and lily ponds instead of chlorinated sludge. Even the circular concrete concentrator tanks—originally used to thicken toxic chemical mud—were preserved intact: one became an open-air amphitheater wrapped in towering bamboo, while another became the Environment Classroom and playground where children climb among recycled concrete pipes.

When Seonyudo Park opened in 2002, it triggered a paradigm shift across Asian urban design. Along with Peter Latz’s Landschaftspark Duisburg-Nord in Germany’s Ruhr Valley, Seonyudo proved that a post-industrial city does not need to bulldoze its working-class engineering past to become green; by letting nature and industrial concrete enter into dialogue, both history and ecology become richer. Over the past two decades, ecologists monitoring Seonyudo have documented the return of Korean pond frogs, dragonflies, spot-billed ducks, little egrets, and migratory kestrels nesting in the ivy-draped concrete columns right beneath the roar of Yanghwa Bridge.

Walk out onto the cantilevered riverside pavilion at the northwestern tip of Seonyudo as the sun sinks into the West Sea and paints the Han River gold. Looking across the water toward the cliffs of Mangwon while water trickles through the concrete lotus basins behind you, you can see both Gyeomjae Jeong Seon’s eighteenth-century painting and twenty-first-century Seoul breathing together.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Biological Phytoremediation: Turning Chlorine Tanks into Root-Zone Filters

In the 1978 Seonyu Water Purification Plant, clarifying Han River water required heavy doses of chemical coagulants (alum) and chlorine gas inside concrete flocculation tanks. In 2002, Jeong Young-sun converted those exact concrete basins into a biological root-zone filtration system. As raw Han River water flows at a controlled hydraulic retention time through beds of volcanic scoria gravel planted with Phragmites reeds, Typha cattails, and Oenanthe javanica (water dropwort), rhizosphere bacteria oxidize ammonia into nitrate while plant roots uptake phosphorus, reducing biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and turbidity naturally.

2. Adaptive Reuse & Embodied Carbon: Why Leaving the Concrete Saved History

Rather than demolishing the reinforced-concrete water reservoirs and hauling 50,000 tons of rubble to landfills, architect Joh Sung-yong and landscape architect Jeong Young-sun selectively removed only the roof slabs and non-structural partitions. Leaving the rough board-formed concrete walls and 30 structural columns intact preserved the spatial memory of Seoul's 1970s industrialization ('Miracle on the Han') while creating sunken microclimatic courtyards 4°C cooler than the surrounding riverbanks in summer.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three concepts in mind: first, Jingyeong Sansuhwa (True-View Landscape Painting), the eighteenth-century movement led by Jeong Seon (Gyeomjae) that painted Seonyubong's original granite cliffs from life; second, Sujil Jeonghwa-won, the aquatic phytoremediation garden inside the old settling basins; and third, Seonyugyo, the 469-meter pedestrian arch bridge built in 2002 to link the island to the south bank without cars.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Take Seoul Subway Line 9 to Seonyudo Station and walk across the arched wooden Seonyugyo pedestrian bridge onto Seonyudo Park. Follow the elevated catwalk over the Aquatic Botanical Garden, descend into the sunken canyons of the Garden of Time, and pause among the ivy-wrapped pillars of the Garden of Green Columns before watching the sunset from the Seonyujeong pavilion.
