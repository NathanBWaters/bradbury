Seonyudo Island Park: How a Concrete Water-Treatment Plant Became an Aquatic Lotus Ruin

Korea — On the Han River island once painted by Jeong Seon, landscape architect Jeong Young-sun turned 1978 chemical settling basins into self-cleaning botanical wetlands (Joseon True-View Era to Contemporary Seoul (1740s / 1978–2002)) • 20 min read

“Do not erase the scars of the twentieth century; let ivy climb the concrete columns and let lotus roots purify the water where chlorine once flowed.” — Jeong Young-sun, Seonyudo Design Notes (2001)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Anchored in the middle of the Han River between Mapo and Yeongdeungpo under the arch of Yanghwa Bridge, Seonyudo ('Island of the Immortals' Play') was once a picturesque granite peak celebrated by eighteenth-century Joseon landscape painter Jeong Seon (Gyeomjae). During the twentieth century, Japanese colonial engineers and post-war planners blasted the entire granite mountain into gravel to build flood levees and highways, then in 1978 covered the flattened island with a concrete chemical water-purification plant supplying southwestern Seoul. When the plant closed in 2000, pioneering landscape architect Jeong Young-sun and architect Joh Sung-yong refused to demolish the concrete tanks, transforming the industrial ruins into Korea's first environmental regeneration park in 2002. Anchored at Seonyudo Island on the Han River, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

1. Biological Phytoremediation: Turning Chlorine Tanks into Root-Zone Filters

In the 1978 Seonyu Water Purification Plant, clarifying Han River water required heavy doses of chemical coagulants (alum) and chlorine gas inside concrete flocculation tanks. In 2002, Jeong Young-sun converted those exact concrete basins into a biological root-zone filtration system. 2. Adaptive Reuse & Embodied Carbon: Why Leaving the Concrete Saved History

Rather than demolishing the reinforced-concrete water reservoirs and hauling 50,000 tons of rubble to landfills, architect Joh Sung-yong and landscape architect Jeong Young-sun selectively removed only the roof slabs and non-structural partitions. Leaving the rough board-formed concrete walls and 30 structural columns intact preserved the spatial memory of Seoul's 1970s industrialization ('Miracle on the Han') while creating sunken microclimatic courtyards 4°C cooler than the surrounding riverbanks in summer.
