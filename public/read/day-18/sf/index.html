San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • Counterculture & Mutual Aid

The 1967 Haight-Ashbury Diggers, the Free Store, and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle

San Francisco — How veterans of the San Francisco Mime Troupe invented free daily stew, a 'Trip Without a Ticket' store without price tags, and a mock funeral for 'Hippie, devoted son of Mass Media' (Sixties Counterculture & Summer of Love (1965–1967)) • 8 min read

“It’s free because it’s yours.” — The San Francisco Diggers, The Digger Papers (1968)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Behind the tie-dyed media clichés of the 1967 'Summer of Love' at the corner of Haight and Ashbury streets operated one of the most radical experiments in urban mutual aid in American history: the San Francisco Diggers. Founded in autumn 1966 by actors Emmett Grogan, Peter Berg, Peter Coyote, and Billy Murcott out of R. G. Davis's guerrilla San Francisco Mime Troupe, the Diggers took their name from Gerrard Winstanley's seventeenth-century English agrarian communards. Every afternoon at 4:00 p.m. in the Golden Gate Park Panhandle, they ladled hot stew out of twenty-gallon milk cans to hundreds of runaways after asking each diner to step through a six-foot wooden 'Free Frame of Reference,' opened a 'Free Store' without money or cash registers, and staged the October 6, 1967 'Death of Hippie' funeral when corporate commercialization overtook the neighborhood.

Why did the 1960s counterculture crystallize specifically in the forty blocks of Queen Anne and Edwardian houses bordering the eastern Panhandle of Golden Gate Park? In the 1950s, California Highway Division engineers had proposed slicing the 'Panhandle Freeway' straight through Golden Gate Park, depressing property values until neighborhood activists killed the freeway plan in the 1966 Freeway Revolt—leaving grand fourteen-room Victorian flats available to students, poets, and musicians for $150 a month. While early psychedelic landmarks like the Red Dog Saloon, Michael Ferguson and Peggy Caserta's Mnasidika boutique (1510 Haight Street), Ron and Jay Thelin's Psychedelic Shop (1535 Haight Street), and the January 14, 1967 'Human Be-In' on the Polo Fields drew global media coverage, a fiercely pragmatic collective of street actors saw a looming humanitarian crisis.

In October 1966, shortly after San Francisco police killed sixteen-year-old Matthew 'Peanut' Johnson in Hunters Point and imposed a citywide curfew, actors Emmett Grogan, Billy Murcott, Peter Berg, and Peter Coyote broke away from the radical San Francisco Mime Troupe to form the San Francisco Diggers. Naming themselves after Gerrard Winstanley's 1649 English 'True Levellers' (who dug up the enclosed common lands of St. George's Hill so the poor could eat without landlords), the San Francisco Diggers argued that you could not preach freedom while selling beads, posters, and concert tickets: to imagine a post-monetary society, you had to act it out on the street right now.

Beginning in the autumn of 1966, every afternoon at 4:00 p.m. on the grass of the Golden Gate Park Panhandle between Oak and Fell streets at Ashbury, the Diggers unloaded steaming twenty-gallon stainless-steel milk cans filled with 'Free Food'—hearty turkey, beef, or vegetable stew cooked by Digger women from surplus produce donated or hustled from the Alemany Farmers Market and Chinatown wholesale butchers. Before ladling stew into a diner's bowl, Grogan and Coyote placed a six-foot-by-six-foot yellow wooden sculpture called the 'Free Frame of Reference' on the grass and invited every person in line to step physically through the empty square frame—a piece of participatory guerrilla theater meant to signify stepping out of capitalist consumer passivity into mutual responsibility.

A few blocks away at 1762 Page Street (and later 520 Frederick Street), the Diggers opened the 'Trip Without a Ticket' Free Store: a storefront with no prices, no cash register, and no paid staff where anyone could walk in, take clothes, boots, dishes, or furniture, or drop off surplus goods, and where a shoebox labeled 'Free Money' sat on the counter for anyone who needed bus fare. When a visiting merchant walked into the Free Store and asked suspiciously, 'Who's the manager here?' a Digger pointed straight back at the visitor and said, 'You are'—wait long enough in the Free Store, Peter Coyote recalled, and you witnessed the exact psychological moment when a visitor stopped hoarding and began straightening the racks for the next stranger.

By June 1967, as Scott McKenzie's hit song 'San Francisco (Be Sure to Wear Flowers in Your Hair)' played on every AM radio station in America and Time and Life magazines trumpeted the 'Summer of Love,' more than 100,000 teenagers and college students—many under sixteen and carrying nothing but a sleeping bag—flooded into the Haight-Ashbury. While City Hall and the Department of Public Health initially hoped to starve the runaways out of town by refusing emergency aid, two community institutions prevented a tragedy: the Diggers set up seven free communal crash pads, free bakeries baking whole-wheat bread in one-pound coffee cans, and the Communications Company ('ComCo'), which cranked out twice-daily free street news bulletins on Gestetner mimeograph machines.

Simultaneously, on June 7, 1967, twenty-eight-year-old toxicologist Dr. David E. Smith rented an old Victorian dental office at 558 Clayton Street (corner of Haight) and opened the Haight-Ashbury Free Medical Clinic around a simple four-word revolutionary manifesto: 'Health Care Is a Right, Not a Privilege'—treating 12,000 patients that first summer with volunteer doctors and nurses at zero cost. As Gray Line tour buses rolled down Haight Street with loudspeakers blaring 'Look at the hippies in their native habitat!' and corporate record labels and Mafia-linked amphetamine dealers flooded the avenue, the Diggers realized that the word 'hippie' had been co-opted into a mass-media consumer brand.

At sunrise on October 6, 1967—exactly one year after California outlawed LSD—the Diggers staged their final masterpiece of street theater down Haight Street to the Panhandle: the 'Death of Hippie, Devoted Son of Mass Media' funeral procession. Dressed in black robes with black armbands, eighty mourners carried a fifteen-foot cardboard coffin overflowing with beads, hair, peace medallions, and copies of the San Francisco Oracle down Haight Street to the Panhandle, where they set the coffin ablaze and handed out notices telling young people not to wait for a media label in the Haight, but to carry the 'Free City' ethic home to their own neighborhoods.

Though the original Haight street scene fragmented by 1968, the infrastructural DNA invented in those blocks between 1966 and 1967 reshaped modern society: Dr. David Smith's Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic became the prototype for over three hundred community free clinics across the United States and pioneered modern addiction medicine and concert Rock Medicine. Meanwhile, the Diggers' 'Free Food' network evolved directly into the Haight Ashbury Food Program, the network of Bay Area natural food cooperatives, and the open-source gift economy—all still visible whenever neighbors gather under the eucalyptus trees of the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury streets.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From the 1649 English Commons to 1966 Guerrilla Street Theater

Why did Emmett Grogan, Peter Berg, and Peter Coyote name their 1966 San Francisco collective 'The Diggers'? In April 1649, following the English Civil War, Gerrard Winstanley led landless workers onto St. George's Hill in Surrey to dig and plant vegetables on the common land without paying rent to aristocratic lords. Trained in Bertolt Brecht's epic theater at the San Francisco Mime Troupe, the 1966 Diggers translated Winstanley's commons into urban life: instead of staging plays about sharing on a theater stage, they turned the city sidewalk into the stage through 'Free Food' and the 'Free Store.'

2. 'Health Care Is a Right, Not a Privilege': The 1967 Free Clinic Model

When 100,000 young people arrived in the Haight in June 1967 and official city hospitals refused non-judgmental care for runaways or psychedelic crises, UCSF researcher Dr. David E. Smith opened the Haight-Ashbury Free Medical Clinic at 558 Clayton Street on June 7, 1967. By decoupling medical treatment from ability to pay, moral policing, or bureaucracy, Smith and his volunteers established the modern American free-clinic movement and created the field of event 'Rock Medicine' with promoter Bill Graham.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three cultural artifacts: first, the six-foot yellow 'Free Frame of Reference' through which diners walked at 4:00 p.m. in the Golden Gate Park Panhandle before receiving Digger stew; second, the Communications Company (ComCo), Chester Anderson and Claude Hayward's Gestetner mimeograph press that printed instant street journalism and Richard Brautigan's broadside poems for free; and third, the October 6, 1967 'Death of Hippie' parade that critiqued media commodification.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Start at the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at the intersection of Ashbury and Oak streets on the lawn where the 4:00 p.m. Digger stew cans were served each day. Walk two blocks south up Ashbury to the famous clock and street sign at Haight and Ashbury streets, pass 710 Ashbury Street (the 1966–1968 Grateful Dead house), and walk two blocks east to 558 Clayton Street to see the historic Victorian birthplace of the Haight-Ashbury Free Medical Clinic.
