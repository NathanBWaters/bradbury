The 1967 Haight-Ashbury Diggers, the Free Store, and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle

San Francisco — How veterans of the San Francisco Mime Troupe invented free daily stew, a 'Trip Without a Ticket' store without price tags, and a mock funeral for 'Hippie, devoted son of Mass Media' (Sixties Counterculture & Summer of Love (1965–1967)) • 20 min read

“It’s free because it’s yours.” — The San Francisco Diggers, The Digger Papers (1968)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Behind the tie-dyed media clichés of the 1967 'Summer of Love' at the corner of Haight and Ashbury streets (37.7700° N, 122.4469° W) operated one of the most radical experiments in urban mutual aid in American history: the San Francisco Diggers. Founded in autumn 1966 by actors Emmett Grogan, Peter Berg, Peter Coyote, and Billy Murcott out of R. G. Davis's guerrilla San Francisco Mime Troupe, the Diggers took their name from Gerrard Winstanley's seventeenth-century English agrarian communards. Every afternoon at 4:00 p.m. in the Golden Gate Park Panhandle, they ladled hot stew out of twenty-gallon milk cans to hundreds of runaways after asking each diner to step through a six-foot wooden 'Free Frame of Reference,' opened a 'Free Store' without money or cash registers, and staged the October 6, 1967 'Death of Hippie' funeral when corporate commercialization overtook the neighborhood.

Standing today at the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury in the heart of the Haight-Ashbury District (37.7700° N, 122.4469° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Sixties Counterculture & Summer of Love (1965–1967). Why did the 1960s counterculture crystallize specifically in the forty blocks of Queen Anne and Edwardian houses bordering the eastern Panhandle of Golden Gate Park? In the 1950s, California Highway Division engineers had proposed slicing the 'Panhandle Freeway' straight through Golden Gate Park, depressing property values until neighborhood activists killed the freeway plan in the 1966 Freeway Revolt—leaving grand fourteen-room Victorian flats available to students, poets, and musicians for $150 a month. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how Digger co-founders Emmett Grogan, Peter Coyote, Peter Berg, and Free Medical Clinic founder Dr. David E. Smith and a generation of San Franciscans confronted feeding, housing, and healing 100,000 young Summer of Love arrivals through autonomous non-monetary mutual aid while resisting commercial exploitation, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Golden Gate Park Panhandle and the Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic (558 Clayton Street) emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Haight-Ashbury District. While early psychedelic landmarks like the Red Dog Saloon, Michael Ferguson and Peggy Caserta's Mnasidika boutique (1510 Haight Street), Ron and Jay Thelin's Psychedelic Shop (1535 Haight Street), and the January 14, 1967 'Human Be-In' on the Polo Fields drew global media coverage, a fiercely pragmatic collective of street actors saw a looming humanitarian crisis. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during October 1966 (founding of the Diggers), January 14, 1967 (Human Be-In), and October 6, 1967 (Death of Hippie parade). In October 1966, shortly after San Francisco police killed sixteen-year-old Matthew 'Peanut' Johnson in Hunters Point and imposed a citywide curfew, actors Emmett Grogan, Billy Murcott, Peter Berg, and Peter Coyote broke away from the radical San Francisco Mime Troupe to form the San Francisco Diggers. Contemporary observers who walked past the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood Digger co-founders Emmett Grogan, Peter Coyote, Peter Berg, and Free Medical Clinic founder Dr. David E. Smith, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of Mime-Troupe guerrilla street theater, non-monetary 'Free Store' logistics, and community harm-reduction medicine proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Naming themselves after Gerrard Winstanley's 1649 English 'True Levellers' (who dug up the enclosed common lands of St. George's Hill so the poor could eat without landlords), the San Francisco Diggers argued that you could not preach freedom while selling beads, posters, and concert tickets: to imagine a post-monetary society, you had to *act it out* on the street right now. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of Mime-Troupe guerrilla street theater, non-monetary 'Free Store' logistics, and community harm-reduction medicine imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the Golden Gate Park Panhandle and the Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic (558 Clayton Street). Beginning in the autumn of 1966, every afternoon at 4:00 p.m. on the grass of the Golden Gate Park Panhandle between Oak and Fell streets at Ashbury, the Diggers unloaded steaming twenty-gallon stainless-steel milk cans filled with 'Free Food'—hearty turkey, beef, or vegetable stew cooked by Digger women from surplus produce donated or hustled from the Alemany Farmers Market and Chinatown wholesale butchers. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through October 1966 (founding of the Diggers), January 14, 1967 (Human Be-In), and October 6, 1967 (Death of Hippie parade), the surrounding streets of the Haight-Ashbury District became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. Before ladling stew into a diner's bowl, Grogan and Coyote placed a six-foot-by-six-foot yellow wooden sculpture called the 'Free Frame of Reference' on the grass and invited every person in line to step physically through the empty square frame—a piece of participatory guerrilla theater meant to signify stepping out of capitalist consumer passivity into mutual responsibility. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. A few blocks away at 1762 Page Street (and later 520 Frederick Street), the Diggers opened the 'Trip Without a Ticket' Free Store: a storefront with no prices, no cash register, and no paid staff where anyone could walk in, take clothes, boots, dishes, or furniture, or drop off surplus goods, and where a shoebox labeled 'Free Money' sat on the counter for anyone who needed bus fare. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the Golden Gate Park Panhandle and the Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic (558 Clayton Street) toward private speculation—forcing Digger co-founders Emmett Grogan, Peter Coyote, Peter Berg, and Free Medical Clinic founder Dr. David E. Smith and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury. When a visiting merchant walked into the Free Store and asked suspiciously, 'Who's the manager here?' a Digger pointed straight back at the visitor and said, 'You are'—wait long enough in the Free Store, Peter Coyote recalled, and you witnessed the exact psychological moment when a visitor stopped hoarding and began straightening the racks for the next stranger. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of Mime-Troupe guerrilla street theater, non-monetary 'Free Store' logistics, and community harm-reduction medicine and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

By June 1967, as Scott McKenzie's hit song 'San Francisco (Be Sure to Wear Flowers in Your Hair)' played on every AM radio station in America and *Time* and *Life* magazines trumpeted the 'Summer of Love,' more than 100,000 teenagers and college students—many under sixteen and carrying nothing but a sleeping bag—flooded into the Haight-Ashbury. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the Golden Gate Park Panhandle and the Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic (558 Clayton Street) was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of the intersection of Haight and Ashbury streets and the Golden Gate Park Panhandle at Oak and Ashbury. While City Hall and the Department of Public Health initially hoped to starve the runaways out of town by refusing emergency aid, two community institutions prevented a tragedy: the Diggers set up seven free communal crash pads, free bakeries baking whole-wheat bread in one-pound coffee cans, and the Communications Company ('ComCo'), which cranked out twice-daily free street news bulletins on Gestetner mimeograph machines. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in October 1966 (founding of the Diggers), January 14, 1967 (Human Be-In), and October 6, 1967 (Death of Hippie parade), the entire city turned its eyes toward the Golden Gate Park Panhandle and the Haight-Ashbury Free Clinic (558 Clayton Street) in the Haight-Ashbury District. Simultaneously, on June 7, 1967, twenty-eight-year-old toxicologist Dr. David E. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in Mime-Troupe guerrilla street theater, non-monetary 'Free Store' logistics, and community harm-reduction medicine. At sunrise on October 6, 1967—exactly one year after California outlawed LSD—the Diggers staged their final masterpiece of street theater down Haight Street to the Panhandle: the 'Death of Hippie, Devoted Son of Mass Media' funeral procession. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of Digger co-founders Emmett Grogan, Peter Coyote, Peter Berg, and Free Medical Clinic founder Dr. David E.
