Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

Fire and Ice

Robert Frost (1920) • 3 min read

Nine razor-sharp lines weigh whether human passion or cold hatred is better equipped to bring about the end of the world.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Fire and Ice is a nine-line poem by Robert Frost, published in Harper's Magazine in December 1920 and collected in his book New Hampshire, which won the Pulitzer Prize. It is one of the most quoted short poems in English, and it is easy to see why. In about forty seconds it asks the oldest question there is, how the world will end, and answers it in a voice so calm that the calm itself becomes unsettling. It was written in the shadow of the First World War and the influenza pandemic, when the idea of civilization collapsing did not seem far-fetched. Frost said that the poem grew partly out of a conversation about how the earth might end, and it also echoes Dante, in whose Inferno the sinners of passion burn and the worst traitors are frozen. The poem's real subject is not astronomy. It is human nature: desire and hatred, and which of them is the better destroyer.

Some say the world will end in fire,

Some say in ice.

From what I’ve tasted of desire

I hold with those who favor fire.

But if it had to perish twice,

I think I know enough of hate

To say that for destruction ice

Is also great

And would suffice.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem begins with a casual line, almost like a bit of small talk: some say the world will end in fire, some say in ice. This is a familiar kind of argument, the sort that people have over a drink. It could refer to the old religious picture of a world consumed by flame at the last judgment, or to the scientific picture, which was then being discussed, of a sun that burns out and leaves the earth frozen. Frost, by the way, was fond of telling a story that the poem came out of a talk with an astronomer. Whatever the exact origin, the first two lines set up a debate about the physical end of everything.

Then the poem turns, and it turns on the word "desire." The speaker says that from what he has tasted of desire, he holds with those who favor fire. The key phrase is "what I've tasted." This is a man speaking from experience, not from science. Desire is hot: it burns, it consumes, it makes people do reckless things. If the world had to end by one force, he would pick the one he knows from his own appetites and wants.

But then comes the surprise: "But if it had to perish twice." The world, the speaker suggests, might be destroyed two times, and for the second, he has another candidate. He knows enough of hate, he says, to claim that ice would do the job just as well. Hate is cold. It is the opposite of passion: patient, distant, unmoving, a refusal to feel. The poem has quietly moved from physics to psychology. The two ways the world ends are two ways people ruin each other.

The last three short lines are what make it a finished object. "Is also great / And would suffice." The rhyme sounds almost comic, and the understatement is chilling. "Suffice" is a word you use about a cup of tea. Applied to the end of the world, it makes destruction sound routine. This understatement is the poem's chief effect. The speaker does not shout or moralize. He weighs the options like a man choosing a mode of transport.

Readers sometimes miss that the poem does not actually say which will win. It gives fire a first preference and ice an equal rating, so that the final impression is not a choice but a pair. Human beings are capable of destroying themselves in both ways, through the heat of wanting too much and through the chill of caring too little. If there is a hint of a moral, it is that the world's end will not need a cosmic cause. We already carry both elements within us.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Fire and Ice is a miniature that works almost perfectly, and the way it works is worth looking at closely. Its first great strength is economy. There are nine lines, and only about a dozen different words carry the argument. There is not a spare syllable, and yet it has a beginning, a middle, a turn, and a twist at the end. Few poems this short feel so complete.

The second is the rhyme scheme, which is unusual. Frost uses only three sounds across nine lines, arranged as ABAABCBCB. The "ire" rhyme of fire and desire runs through the first half. The "ice" sound comes back again and again at the end, in ice, twice, ice, suffice. By the end of the poem the same sound has returned so many times that it feels like a door closing repeatedly. The sound of the poem itself shifts from open to shut as it moves from fire to ice. That is craft in the service of meaning.

Third, the line lengths do real work. The poem opens with lines of ordinary length and then shrinks. The final three lines, "Is also great / And would suffice," are very short, and the shortness delivers the cold. The reader almost falls off the end, and that sense of a sudden stop is part of the effect.

Now to be critical. The poem's slightness is both its glory and its limit. It makes a point and then stops, and a reader hungry for development may feel that it is more a clever remark than a full poem. It is also, in a way, a bit pat. The pairing of desire with fire and hate with ice is one of the oldest metaphors we have. Fire for passion and ice for coldness are almost proverbial, and Frost does not dramatically reinvent them. What he does is add the wry voice. Without it, the poem would be a greeting card.

There is also the question of the diction. "Some say" is a very casual opening, and the sudden turn to "I hold with those who favor fire" can seem a little glib. The poem's voice is sly, and some readers feel it is too pleased with itself. An audience that wants gravity at the edge of apocalypse might find the poem's lightness evasive.

And yet that is the real source of its power. The lightness is deliberate. The most frightening way to talk about the end of the world is in the tone of a man talking about the weather. By combining a huge subject with a tiny, tidy form, Frost makes the reader supply the horror. The smallness of the poem is the hole through which the large thing is seen. This is why the poem stays memorable. It is a trick, but it is a trick that works every time, because the reader is the one who finishes it.

III. The Impact It Had

Fire and Ice became one of the most famous poems of the twentieth century, and probably one of the most memorized. It appeared first in Harper's Magazine in December 1920 and then in New Hampshire, the 1923 collection that won Frost the first of his four Pulitzer Prizes for poetry. By then Frost was in the process of becoming America's most beloved poet, and short, quotable poems like this one were a large part of the reason. It gave him a reputation as a writer who could say something profound in a few words, which later made him a natural choice to recite at public events, most famously at the inauguration of John F. Kennedy in 1961.

The poem has also lived a second life outside poetry. The phrase has become shorthand for opposing destructive forces. It has been borrowed for the titles of novels, albums, films, and nonfiction books about everything from nuclear war to climate change. Scientists and journalists have quoted it when discussing whether the earth will end through warming or through freezing, and the image is so familiar that it is often used without attribution. George R. R. Martin's long fantasy series A Song of Ice and Fire takes its name from the same pairing of images, though he has not described it as a direct borrowing and the idea itself is older than Frost. The poem in a sense gave the phrase its modern cultural weight.

In education, Fire and Ice is a regular in school anthologies, often because it is short enough to analyze in a single class. Teachers use it to introduce rhyme, meter, metaphor, and the idea of tone. It is also a favorite for introducing the idea of the speaker, a distinct voice that is not the same as the poet himself. Students tend to enjoy the final lines, and many are surprised to learn that ice can stand in for emotion.

On other poets, the influence is more subtle. Frost showed that a poem could be formally strict, with careful rhyme and meter, and still sound like a thought spoken aloud. Poets who wanted to write short, ironic, conversational poems about enormous subjects had in Frost a clear example of how it could be done. The poem sits in the line of epigram, the brief witty form that goes back to ancient Greece, and it gave that form a modern American accent.

Finally, it affected how readers think of Frost. Before poems like this, he might have been seen as a regional poet of New England farm life. Fire and Ice showed that he could also be a poet of cosmic and philosophical questions, and that the cosmic could be handled with a farmer's understatement. That doubleness became the signature of his reputation.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love Fire and Ice, first of all, because it is memorable in a way that most poems are not. A reader can learn it by heart in a minute and recite it for the rest of their life. This is a rare gift. Many people who do not read poetry can still say the opening lines, and many who read a great deal of poetry still keep it close, because there is satisfaction in being able to hold a whole poem in the mind at once.

They also love the wit. Behind the dark subject there is a sense of humor, which is one of the things people most prize in Frost. The speaker takes the largest conceivable question, the end of the world, and treats it with something like a shrug. The phrase "from what I've tasted of desire" is faintly comic, hinting at a rueful, personal history, and the closing "would suffice" has the dry quality of a man who has seen a lot and is not easily impressed. People like being in the company of a voice like that, and it makes the poem feel human, not grand.

Readers appreciate its ambiguity. It gives you a choice, but then immediately complicates it. People often come to the poem wanting to take a side, fire or ice, and then find they are being asked a different question. Is hatred more dangerous than passion? Which have you encountered more often in your own life? That invitation to reflect is a large part of why people go back to it, and a short poem that opens so many doors rewards repeated reading.

Fellow writers have admired its balance of form and content. The rhyme scheme, with its tight repetition, has the cold clarity of the thing it describes. Students and teachers note how the last lines literally narrow down, as if the poem itself were freezing. This sense that the poem is acting out its subject is a pleasure that many readers find hard to forget.

There is also an emotional appeal that is less often mentioned. In a century of world wars, nuclear fear, and climate anxiety, the idea of the end of the world is not abstract to most people. A poem that deals with that fear in a few quiet lines offers a strange kind of comfort. It does not tell us that everything will be all right. It tells us instead that human beings have always wondered about this, and that the answer, whichever it is, lies at least partly in our own hearts. For many readers, being able to look at the worst thing with a steady eye is the closest thing to consolation that a poem can give.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Fire and Ice has been praised more than it has been attacked, but a number of objections come up regularly, and they are worth taking seriously. The most common is that the poem is too slight. Critics who prize Frost's longer, more dramatic work, such as Home Burial or Mending Wall, sometimes treat Fire and Ice as a clever trifle, a good party piece that does not demand much of the reader. They point out that it makes an observation rather than exploring a situation, and that once you have understood the trick of the ending, there is not much more to dig out.

A second objection concerns the central metaphor. Fire for passion and ice for coldness is, as noted, a very old pairing, and some critics think the poem simply rearranges the familiar. They suggest that the speaker's choice between desire and hate is a little too neat. Real human destructiveness, the argument goes, is messier than a two-way split, and the poem's confident categories flatten it. Hatred, for instance, is not always cold. It can burn as hot as any desire. The poem seems to ignore this, perhaps deliberately, perhaps not.

Some readers have also complained about the tone. The offhand voice can seem flippant in the face of its subject, and it is a little disquieting to find the end of the world treated as a matter of personal preference. A reader who has lived through war or disaster might find the poem's cool detachment off-putting. Frost's detractors have sometimes said that his famous irony is a way of avoiding feeling, and this poem would be a good place to make that case.

There is also a more technical dispute about interpretation. Because the poem is so short, it can be read in several ways, and critics disagree about how seriously to take it. Some read it as a straightforward, if wry, moral observation about human emotions. Others read it as a satire on the endless human habit of arguing about apocalypse. Still others see it as a deliberately empty gesture. The disagreement shows both the poem's richness and its slipperiness, and no single reading has settled the matter.

It is worth being honest that the poem has also suffered from its own popularity. It is quoted so often, and so casually, that many people have stopped hearing it. When a poem becomes a slogan, its subtlety tends to be lost. Frost himself was famously cagey about explaining his work, and the habit of treating Fire and Ice as a neat riddle with a clean answer would probably have amused him. The best criticism of the poem is therefore not that it fails, but that its fame can make it harder to read freshly.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Fire and Ice belongs to the large family of poems about the end of the world, which is one of the oldest subjects in literature. The Norse myths imagine a final battle called Ragnarok that ends in fire and flood. The Bible's Book of Revelation imagines cosmic fire. And in the early twentieth century, as science began to describe the death of the sun and the heat death of the universe, poets absorbed these new pictures of an ending. Frost's poem sits at the point where the mythic and the scientific meet, and he handles both with the same wry shrug.

It also echoes Dante. In the Inferno, the sinners of passion are blown about in a storm, and the sinners of fraud and betrayal are locked in the frozen lake at the very bottom of hell. The coldest sin is the worst. Frost's judgment, that ice is also great for destruction, is in tune with that old moral order. Readers who know Dante may feel that Frost has compressed a medieval hierarchy of sin into nine lines of New England talk.

In terms of movement, the poem is hard to place. It was written during the great age of modernism, when poets like T. S. Eliot were writing sprawling, allusive works. Eliot's Hollow Men, from 1925, famously ends with the world ending not with a bang but a whimper, which makes an instructive comparison. Where Eliot is solemn and fragmented, Frost is compact and colloquial. Both poems imagine the end as something sadly small. Frost also differs from the imagists, who prized sharp images, because his poem is built on argument and voice, not a single picture.

In the American tradition, the poem descends from Emily Dickinson's short, startling lyrics and from the epigrams of Ben Jonson and the Greek Anthology before her. What descends from it is the great modern habit of the short, ironic poem, running through poets like Dorothy Parker, Wendy Cope, and many others who can say a dark thing lightly. It also feeds the apocalyptic imagination of modern fiction and film.

Why read it now? Because the question it asks has not gone away. We still wonder whether we will end through burning or freezing, through too much wanting or too little caring, and we have more reasons than Frost's readers did to take the question seriously. But the poem's best lesson may be its tone. It shows how to look at something terrifying without panic and without denial. It takes less than a minute to read, and it may take the rest of a life to finish thinking about.
