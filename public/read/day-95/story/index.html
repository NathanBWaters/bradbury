The Lumber Room

Saki (H. H. Munro) (1914) • 11 min read

“How she did howl,” said Nicholas cheerfully, as the party drove off without any of the elation of high spirits that should have characterised it.

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Lumber Room is a short comic story about a small boy who outwits a large, self-satisfied aunt, and it is one of the best-loved pieces by the writer known as Saki. His real name was Hector Hugh Munro. He was born in Burma in 1870, lost his mother when he was very young, and was raised in Devon by strict, unimaginative aunts, a background that fuels nearly all of his stories about children. As an adult he became a journalist and a sharp-tongued satirist in Edwardian London. This story appeared in a collection called Beasts and Super-Beasts in 1914, the year the First World War began, and Munro himself was killed in France in 1916. Though it is funny, it is also a story about power: who gets to decide what is forbidden, and what happens when a child understands the rules better than the adult who made them. Listen for how Nicholas wins by being exactly as obedient as he is told to be.

The children were to be driven, as a special treat, to the sands at Jagborough. Nicholas was not to be of the party; he was in disgrace. Only that morning he had refused to eat his wholesome bread-and-milk on the seemingly frivolous ground that there was a frog in it. Older and wiser and better people had told him that there could not possibly be a frog in his bread-and-milk and that he was not to talk nonsense; he continued, nevertheless, to talk what seemed the veriest nonsense, and described with much detail the colouration and markings of the alleged frog. The dramatic part of the incident was that there really was a frog in Nicholas’ basin of bread-and-milk; he had put it there himself, so he felt entitled to know something about it. The sin of taking a frog from the garden and putting it into a bowl of wholesome bread-and-milk was enlarged on at great length, but the fact that stood out clearest in the whole affair, as it presented itself to the mind of Nicholas, was that the older, wiser, and better people had been proved to be profoundly in error in matters about which they had expressed the utmost assurance.

“You said there couldn’t possibly be a frog in my bread-and-milk; there was a frog in my bread-and-milk,” he repeated, with the insistence of a skilled tactician who does not intend to shift from favourable ground.

So his boy-cousin and girl-cousin and his quite uninteresting younger brother were to be taken to Jagborough sands that afternoon and he was to stay at home. His cousins’ aunt, who insisted, by an unwarranted stretch of imagination, in styling herself his aunt also, had hastily invented the Jagborough expedition in order to impress on Nicholas the delights that he had justly forfeited by his disgraceful conduct at the breakfast-table. It was her habit, whenever one of the children fell from grace, to improvise something of a festival nature from which the offender would be rigorously debarred; if all the children sinned collectively they were suddenly informed of a circus in a neighbouring town, a circus of unrivalled merit and uncounted elephants, to which, but for their depravity, they would have been taken that very day.

A few decent tears were looked for on the part of Nicholas when the moment for the departure of the expedition arrived. As a matter of fact, however, all the crying was done by his girl-cousin, who scraped her knee rather painfully against the step of the carriage as she was scrambling in.

“How she did howl,” said Nicholas cheerfully, as the party drove off without any of the elation of high spirits that should have characterised it.

“She’ll soon get over that,” said the soi-disant aunt; “it will be a glorious afternoon for racing about over those beautiful sands. How they will enjoy themselves!”

“Bobby won’t enjoy himself much, and he won’t race much either,” said Nicholas with a grim chuckle; “his boots are hurting him. They’re too tight.”

“Why didn’t he tell me they were hurting?” asked the aunt with some asperity.

“He told you twice, but you weren’t listening. You often don’t listen when we tell you important things.”

“You are not to go into the gooseberry garden,” said the aunt, changing the subject.

“Why not?” demanded Nicholas.

“Because you are in disgrace,” said the aunt loftily.

Nicholas did not admit the flawlessness of the reasoning; he felt perfectly capable of being in disgrace and in a gooseberry garden at the same moment. His face took on an expression of considerable obstinacy. It was clear to his aunt that he was determined to get into the gooseberry garden, “only,” as she remarked to herself, “because I have told him he is not to.”

Now the gooseberry garden had two doors by which it might be entered, and once a small person like Nicholas could slip in there he could effectually disappear from view amid the masking growth of artichokes, raspberry canes, and fruit bushes. The aunt had many other things to do that afternoon, but she spent an hour or two in trivial gardening operations among flower beds and shrubberies, whence she could keep a watchful eye on the two doors that led to the forbidden paradise. She was a woman of few ideas, with immense powers of concentration.

Nicholas made one or two sorties into the front garden, wriggling his way with obvious stealth of purpose towards one or other of the doors, but never able for a moment to evade the aunt’s watchful eye. As a matter of fact, he had no intention of trying to get into the gooseberry garden, but it was extremely convenient for him that his aunt should believe that he had; it was a belief that would keep her on self-imposed sentry-duty for the greater part of the afternoon. Having thoroughly confirmed and fortified her suspicions Nicholas slipped back into the house and rapidly put into execution a plan of action that had long germinated in his brain. By standing on a chair in the library one could reach a shelf on which reposed a fat, important-looking key. The key was as important as it looked; it was the instrument which kept the mysteries of the lumber-room secure from unauthorised intrusion, which opened a way only for aunts and suchlike privileged persons. Nicholas had not had much experience of the art of fitting keys into keyholes and turning locks, but for some days past he had practised with the key of the schoolroom door; he did not believe in trusting too much to luck and accident. The key turned stiffly in the lock, but it turned. The door opened, and Nicholas was in an unknown land, compared with which the gooseberry garden was a stale delight, a mere material pleasure.

Often and often Nicholas had pictured to himself what the lumber-room might be like, that region that was so carefully sealed from youthful eyes and concerning which no questions were ever answered. It came up to his expectations. In the first place it was large and dimly lit, one high window opening on to the forbidden garden being its only source of illumination. In the second place it was a storehouse of unimagined treasures. The aunt-by-assertion was one of those people who think that things spoil by use and consign them to dust and damp by way of preserving them. Such parts of the house as Nicholas knew best were rather bare and cheerless, but here there were wonderful things for the eye to feast on. First and foremost there was a piece of framed tapestry that was evidently meant to be a fire-screen. To Nicholas it was a living, breathing story; he sat down on a roll of Indian hangings, glowing in wonderful colours beneath a layer of dust, and took in all the details of the tapestry picture. A man, dressed in the hunting costume of some remote period, had just transfixed a stag with an arrow; it could not have been a difficult shot because the stag was only one or two paces away from him; in the thickly-growing vegetation that the picture suggested it would not have been difficult to creep up to a feeding stag, and the two spotted dogs that were springing forward to join in the chase had evidently been trained to keep to heel till the arrow was discharged. That part of the picture was simple, if interesting, but did the huntsman see, what Nicholas saw, that four galloping wolves were coming in his direction through the wood? There might be more than four of them hidden behind the trees, and in any case would the man and his dogs be able to cope with the four wolves if they made an attack? The man had only two arrows left in his quiver, and he might miss with one or both of them; all one knew about his skill in shooting was that he could hit a large stag at a ridiculously short range. Nicholas sat for many golden minutes revolving the possibilities of the scene; he was inclined to think that there were more than four wolves and that the man and his dogs were in a tight corner.

But there were other objects of delight and interest claiming his instant attention: there were quaint twisted candlesticks in the shape of snakes, and a teapot fashioned like a china duck, out of whose open beak the tea was supposed to come. How dull and shapeless the nursery teapot seemed in comparison! And there was a carved sandalwood box packed tight with aromatic cotton-wool, and between the layers of cotton-wool were little brass figures, hump-necked bulls, and peacocks and goblins, delightful to see and to handle. Less promising in appearance was a large square book with plain black covers; Nicholas peeped into it, and, behold, it was full of coloured pictures of birds. And such birds! In the garden, and in the lanes when he went for a walk, Nicholas came across a few birds, of which the largest were an occasional magpie or wood pigeon; here were herons and bustards, kites, toucans, tiger-bitterns, brush turkeys, ibises, golden pheasants, a whole portrait gallery of undreamed-of creatures. And as he was admiring the colouring of the mandarin duck and assigning a life-history to it, the voice of his aunt in shrill vociferation of his name came from the gooseberry garden without. She had grown suspicious at his long disappearance, and had leapt to the conclusion that he had climbed over the wall behind the sheltering screen of the lilac bushes; she was now engaged in energetic and rather hopeless search for him among the artichokes and raspberry canes.

“Nicholas, Nicholas!” she screamed, “you are to come out of this at once. It’s no use trying to hide there; I can see you all the time.”

It was probably the first time for twenty years that anyone had smiled in that lumber-room.

Presently the angry repetitions of Nicholas’ name gave way to a shriek, and a cry for somebody to come quickly. Nicholas shut the book, restored it carefully to its place in a corner, and shook some dust from a neighbouring pile of newspapers over it. Then he crept from the room, locked the door, and replaced the key exactly where he had found it. His aunt was still calling his name when he sauntered into the front garden.

“Who’s calling?” he asked.

“Me,” came the answer from the other side of the wall; “didn’t you hear me? I’ve been looking for you in the gooseberry garden, and I’ve slipped into the rainwater tank. Luckily there’s no water in it, but the sides are slippery and I can’t get out. Fetch the little ladder from under the cherry tree⁠—”

“I was told I wasn’t to go into the gooseberry garden,” said Nicholas promptly.

“I told you not to, and now I tell you that you may,” came the voice from the rainwater tank, rather impatiently.

“Your voice doesn’t sound like aunt’s,” objected Nicholas; “you may be the Evil One tempting me to be disobedient. Aunt often tells me that the Evil One tempts me and that I always yield. This time I’m not going to yield.”

“Don’t talk nonsense,” said the prisoner in the tank; “go and fetch the ladder.”

“Will there be strawberry jam for tea?” asked Nicholas innocently.

“Certainly there will be,” said the aunt, privately resolving that Nicholas should have none of it.

“Now I know that you are the Evil One and not aunt,” shouted Nicholas gleefully; “when we asked aunt for strawberry jam yesterday she said there wasn’t any. I know there are four jars of it in the store cupboard, because I looked, and of course you know it’s there, but she doesn’t, because she said there wasn’t any. Oh, Devil, you have sold yourself!”

There was an unusual sense of luxury in being able to talk to an aunt as though one was talking to the Evil One, but Nicholas knew, with childish discernment, that such luxuries were not to be overindulged in. He walked noisily away, and it was a kitchenmaid, in search of parsley, who eventually rescued the aunt from the rainwater tank.

Tea that evening was partaken of in a fearsome silence. The tide had been at its highest when the children had arrived at Jagborough Cove, so there had been no sands to play on⁠—a circumstance that the aunt had overlooked in the haste of organising her punitive expedition. The tightness of Bobby’s boots had had disastrous effect on his temper the whole of the afternoon, and altogether the children could not have been said to have enjoyed themselves. The aunt maintained the frozen muteness of one who has suffered undignified and unmerited detention in a rainwater tank for thirty-five minutes. As for Nicholas, he, too, was silent, in the absorption of one who has much to think about; it was just possible, he considered, that the huntsman would escape with his hounds while the wolves feasted on the stricken stag.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story begins with a quarrel over a frog. Young Nicholas has refused to eat his bread and milk because, he says, there is a frog in it. Adults who are older, wiser and better, we are told, have assured him that there cannot possibly be a frog in his bread and milk. But there is one. Nicholas put it there himself, which gives him a strong claim to know something about it. When he is proved right, the grown-ups are furious, and as a punishment he is not allowed to go on the treat that has hastily been invented for the other children: a trip to the sands at Jagborough.

The aunt, who calls herself aunt to all the children even though she is only a cousin's aunt, has a habit of inventing a treat whenever one of the children falls from grace, so that the guilty one can be left behind to feel the loss. Nicholas is made to stay at home, and the aunt, aware that he is up to something, settles in to guard the gooseberry garden, which she has forbidden him to enter. He is happy to let her think that is his plan. He makes a few loud, obvious attempts to creep toward the garden, which keep her on sentry duty through the afternoon, and meanwhile slips into the house.

His real goal is the lumber room, a locked storeroom that has been sealed from the children's curiosity, about which no questions are ever answered. He has long dreamed of seeing inside it. The key, he has discovered, sits on a shelf in the library. He climbs on a chair, takes it, and opens the door to find a world of wonders: a tapestry of a hunter, a stag and wolves, snake-shaped candlesticks, a teapot like a china duck, brass figures in a box of cotton-wool and a huge book of colored pictures of birds. To Nicholas it is the greatest treasure house he can imagine, and the aunt, who thinks that things are spoiled by use and prefers to leave them in dust and damp, has no idea of what she has locked away.

Then comes the comic climax. The aunt, seeking him in the gooseberry garden, slips into a rainwater tank and cannot climb out. She calls his name, and he, hearing her, answers politely and refuses to help. He reminds her that she told him never to go into the gooseberry garden. When she tells him he may, he says her voice does not sound like his aunt's and that she must be the Evil One tempting him to disobey. He tests her by asking about strawberry jam, and when she says there is some, he declares that she has proved she is the devil, since the real aunt told him there was none. He strolls away, and it is a kitchenmaid who eventually rescues her.

At its simplest, the story is a delight about a child who beats a grown-up at her own game. But there is a deeper meaning in the way it shows how adult rules can be turned against those who make them. Nicholas never breaks a rule. He obeys the aunt's own words with perfect, merciless literalness. Beneath the comedy is a serious point: the aunt's authority is arbitrary, and her treats, punishments and prohibitions are ways of controlling the children. The lumber room itself is a symbol of the imagination that grown-ups lock away, and Nicholas's afternoon is a small, glorious act of liberation.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a very well-made comic story, and the pleasure of it lies in precision. The first section, with the frog, establishes Nicholas as a master tactician, and the narrator's mock-solemn tone, in which he is described as a skilled tactician who does not intend to shift from favorable ground, sets up the comedy. We learn that the story belongs to him, and that he will win through intelligence.

The second section, in which Nicholas lures the aunt into guarding the wrong place, is a small masterpiece of misdirection. He does not need to be tricky in some elaborate way. He merely lets her assumptions do the work. The long central scene in the lumber room is a surprising change of tone. After the brisk comedy of the beginning, the story slows down and lets itself linger over objects and images. The tapestry, with its huntsman, the stag he has just shot, his two spotted dogs and four galloping wolves, is given a vivid, almost reverent description, and for a moment the story becomes something close to a hymn to the imagination. It is a touch of real beauty in a comic tale.

The final scene, with its beautifully logical refusal, is among the funniest pieces of dialogue Saki wrote. The Evil One gag is perfectly constructed. The strawberry jam detail, which shows the aunt lying to the children about the contents of the larder, gives Nicholas a final blow that is also an act of moral accounting. The coda, with the silent, sullen tea, the boots that pinch and the tide that was high so that there were no sands to play on, completes the portrait of an adult whose cleverness has failed her.

There are weaknesses, though. The aunt is a caricature, a woman made up almost wholly of pomposity and stupidity, and some readers may wish for more complexity in her. The story is also one-sided. We see the world only from Nicholas's point of view, and the other children, Bobby and the cousins, are barely sketched. The ending is sharp, but it does not follow the consequences. We never learn whether Nicholas is punished later, and we may suspect that a real aunt would find a way to get her revenge. The cruelty of leaving a woman in a tank for thirty-five minutes, even if she is a tyrant, is something the story lightly passes over. Yet the comic spirit is strong, and the tale moves so fast that these quibbles seldom trouble us while we read. What lingers is its energy.

One more thing is worth noticing about the craft. The ending of the day, with its tea in a fearsome silence, does not simply repeat the joke. It adds a final image: Nicholas, absorbed in thought, wondering whether the huntsman in the tapestry will escape with his dogs while the wolves feast on the stricken stag. That echo of the picture quietly suggests that Nicholas has been thinking about danger, victory and how things might turn out, and it gives the comedy an odd, lingering shadow. A lesser writer would have stopped at the aunt's humiliation. Saki ends instead on the boy's private world, which is where the story has always belonged.

III. The Impact It Had

The Lumber Room has had a long and healthy career as a classroom favorite. It appears in anthologies for young readers and in textbooks in Britain and the Commonwealth, and it is often used to teach irony, characterization and point of view. Because it is short, funny and clear, it is an ideal introduction to Saki.

It also belongs to a long line of stories in which a clever child defeats a stupid or cruel adult. This tradition has been enormously productive in children's literature. Roald Dahl, who wrote Matilda and many other stories in which children triumph over awful grown-ups, is often compared to Saki, and while it is hard to prove direct influence, the family resemblance is plain. Richmal Crompton's William books, which began in the early 1920s, feature a similar mischievous boy, and countless modern comedies about children outsmarting parents and teachers draw on the same pattern.

The story has also helped shape how readers think about Saki as a writer of children. He is one of the few authors of his time who took a child's side without sentimentality. His children are not sweet little angels. They are crafty, ruthless and clever, and they are often right. That unsentimental sympathy was unusual in the age of Edwardian children's fiction, and it helped open the door for later writers who treated childhood as a world with its own logic.

In the broader culture, the story has become a kind of touchstone for the pleasure of seeing a pompous authority figure confounded by her own rules. It is still anthologized more than a century after it was written. Much of this staying power comes from its biographical backdrop. Many readers know that Saki spent his own childhood under the rule of aunts, and they read Nicholas's triumph as a kind of wish-fulfillment, a fantasy of revenge enjoyed by a boy who never got to win.

It is also taught as a lesson in how a writer builds a joke. Students are often asked to trace how each early detail, the frog, the forbidden garden, the key on the shelf, the jam that the aunt says does not exist, comes back later with a new meaning. That tight, interlocking design is part of why the story is so often used in classrooms: it shows plainly, in a few pages, how setup and payoff work. Some teachers also use it to start conversations about fairness, authority and honesty, since the story invites readers to decide for themselves whether Nicholas has been clever, cruel, or both.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love this story, first of all, because Nicholas wins. There is a deep pleasure in watching a child who has been bossed and scolded and dismissed turn the tables with nothing but wit and logic. Many readers remember their own childhood frustrations with adults who invented arbitrary rules, and they enjoy the story as a kind of revenge fantasy.

They also love the language. Saki's sentences are polished and wickedly funny, full of understatement and mock-seriousness. The description of the aunt as one who insisted, by an unwarranted stretch of imagination, on styling herself his aunt also is typical of his deadpan wit. The narrator, who seems to be on the side of the grown-ups but is in fact secretly on Nicholas's side, creates a sly comic tension that gives every page a spark.

Another pleasure is the lumber room itself. After the sharp comedy of the beginning, the story opens into a lovely, dreamy interlude, in which a boy who has been starved of beauty finds himself surrounded by marvels. Readers often cite this scene as the emotional center of the story. The tapestry, in which a huntsman who has just shot a stag does not seem to notice four wolves creeping toward him, becomes a story that Nicholas can live inside, and the birds in the great book are things he has never seen. The passage reminds us that imagination is a form of freedom.

Finally, readers value the story's confidence. It does not apologize for Nicholas. It does not make him learn a lesson or punish him for his cleverness. It simply lets him win, and it lets the adult look foolish. That refusal to moralize is part of its charm, and it is the reason the tale feels fresh and subversive even today. It is a story that trusts children to be smart, and trusts readers to enjoy it.

Readers also delight in how much the story seems to know about being a child. The slow, unfair rhythm of a day in disgrace, the way adults make rules that change with their moods, the strange, charged feeling of being in a place you are forbidden to enter, all ring true. Someone who has stood outside a locked door wondering what was inside will recognize the thrill of Nicholas turning that key. And the story honors that thrill. It does not hurry the scene, and it treats the boy's wonder as something worth the reader's full attention.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critical reaction to The Lumber Room has been mostly warm, and because it is a minor comic story in a collection of pieces, extensive contemporary reviews are hard to find. Most reviewers of Saki's collections praised his wit while sometimes noting what they saw as a lack of warmth. What follows is therefore mostly a description of the objections that readers have raised over time.

The most common complaint is about the aunt. She is drawn without any depth, and some readers feel she is a straw figure, easy to knock down. A fairer story might have shown her as a person with her own anxieties and limits, instead of a mass of smugness. Others feel that the story is too cozy in its cruelty, that Nicholas's refusal to help a woman who may be injured is more disturbing than funny. The aunt is trapped in a tank for thirty-five minutes, and while the narrator tells us there is no water, a reader who thinks about it may feel a twinge of discomfort.

A second objection is to the story's moral coldness. Saki's humor often depends on cruelty, and critics have noted that his stories tend to side with the clever and the ruthless. Nicholas wins by lying, by manipulating, and by exploiting an adult's weakness. Some readers see this as a weakness, a lack of moral seriousness. Others say that is precisely the point: the children of Saki's stories are driven to cunning by the bullying of adults, and the stories do not pretend that the world is fair.

Finally, some readers have found the story slight. It is a sketch, not a deep exploration of childhood, and it lacks the darker resonance of Sredni Vashtar or the sharp social satire of The Open Window. The Edwardian setting, with its aunts, governesses and seaside treats, is distant from modern life, and a few of the story's period details, such as the aunt's fear of the Evil One, may need a word of explanation. A still more modern objection concerns the story's picture of the child as hero and the adult as fool, which some feel is a simple inversion and not a real insight. Yet most critics agree that the story's lightness is a virtue, not a flaw, and that it achieves what it sets out to do.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Where does The Lumber Room belong in the larger world of literature? It is a comedy of manners, in the tradition of English satire that mocks the pretensions of polite society, and it also belongs to the long line of stories about children and adults in which the child's point of view subverts adult authority. Its ancestors include Dickens's novels, in which children suffer at the hands of cruel guardians, and Mark Twain's Tom Sawyer, in which a clever boy turns the rules of a puzzled aunt to his own advantage. Saki's Nicholas is closer to Tom than to Oliver Twist, since he does not suffer but triumphs.

It also descends from the Victorian tradition of the unhappy childhood, from Rudyard Kipling's story Baa Baa, Black Sheep, which drew on his own miserable years in a foster home, to the harsh institutions in Charlotte Bronte's Jane Eyre. Saki's twist is to turn that tradition into comedy. He takes the same cold aunts and joyless rules and shows a child who fights back with cunning and wins. The result is lighter in tone than Kipling or Bronte but just as sharp in its criticism of adult tyranny.

Its descendants are many. Dahl's tales of rebellious children, Crompton's William, and in a sense the mischievous protagonists of modern children's comedy owe a debt to this tradition. In the wider world of short fiction, Saki stands beside O. Henry and Oscar Wilde as a master of the polished, ironic tale, and his dry, deadpan manner is often mentioned alongside later comic writers such as P. G. Wodehouse and Roald Dahl, though how much they owed him is a matter of opinion. The idea that a child can be the cleverest person in the room has become a staple of popular culture.

Why read it now? Because it is short, funny, and still true. Anyone who has ever been told to do something for no good reason, or has ever been punished for telling an inconvenient truth, will recognize Nicholas. The story also offers a quiet lesson about imagination: the lumber room is a symbol of everything that adults lock away, and Nicholas's visit is a reminder that wonder is worth stealing a key for. It is a perfect small story, read in minutes, that leaves you smiling.
