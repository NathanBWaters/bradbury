Nam June Paik: The Shaman of Cathode Rays Who Invented the 'Electronic Superhighway'

Korea — How a Seoul-born composer attacked television tubes with horseshoe magnets, seated a bronze Buddha before a closed-circuit camera, and foresaw the internet in 1974 (Contemporary Art & Digital Era (1932–2006)) • 20 min read

“Skin has become inadequate in interfacing with reality. Technology has become the body’s new membrane of existence.” — Nam June Paik, Media Art Manifesto

Key Context & Why It Matters

Born in Seoul in 1932 to a prominent textile manufacturing family in Changsin-dong and forced to flee south at age eighteen when the Korean War erupted in June 1950, Nam June Paik (Baek Nam-jun, 1932–2006) single-handedly founded the global medium of video art. Trained in Arnold Schoenberg's twelve-tone music in Tokyo and Munich before joining George Maciunas's Fluxus movement alongside John Cage and Joseph Beuys, Paik realized in 1963 at Galerie Parnass in Wuppertal that the television set did not have to be a one-way instrument of corporate broadcast passivity. By placing powerful horseshoe magnets onto cathode-ray tubes, building the Paik-Abe Video Synthesizer, seating an eighteenth-century Buddha before a live closed-circuit screen, and coining the term 'Electronic Superhighway' in 1974, Paik prophesied the networked digital age.

Inside the gallery sat thirteen secondhand black-and-white television sets strewn across the floor on their backs and sides inside a tangle of soldering irons, foot pedals, and stripped copper wires. Titled *Exposition of Music – Electronic Television*, that evening marked the birth of video art. 1. Electromagnetic Physics: How 'Magnet TV' Hacked the Cathode-Ray Tube

To appreciate why Nam June Paik's 1963–1965 modified televisions stunned engineers, look inside a cathode-ray tube (CRT). A conventional broadcast receiver scans 525 or 625 horizontal lines thirty times per second using precisely timed electromagnetic coils around the neck of the vacuum tube. By placing a permanent neodymium or alnico horseshoe magnet against the glass envelope or rewiring the internal vertical/horizontal deflection yokes to respond to audio frequencies from a microphone, Paik hijacked the Lorentz force acting on the electron beam, turning deterministic raster scanning into non-linear electronic calligraphy.
