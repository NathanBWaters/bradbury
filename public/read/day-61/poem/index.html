Sonnet 73: That time of year thou mayst in me behold

William Shakespeare (1609) • 3 min read

Across three narrowing metaphors—late autumn boughs, twilight fading into black night, and a dying fire glowing on the ashes of its youth—mortality deepens love.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Sonnet 73 is one of the best-loved poems Shakespeare ever wrote, and one of the most quoted pictures of growing old in the English language. It comes from his collection of 154 sonnets, printed in 1609, though many were probably written in the 1590s, when sonnets were the fashionable way for an ambitious poet to show off. Most of the first hundred and twenty-six poems in the sequence are addressed to a younger man whom the speaker adores, and here the speaker, now aging, turns to look at himself through that young person's eyes. Shakespeare was living in an England that had recently torn down its monasteries, so when he mentions ruined church choirs, his first audience could actually see them standing empty in the countryside. In fourteen short lines, the poem moves through three pictures: autumn, dusk, and a dying fire. Its overall meaning is a quiet and surprising one. Knowing that something is passing does not weaken love. It sharpens it.

That time of year thou mayst in me behold

When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang

Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,

Bare ruin’d choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.

In me thou see’st the twilight of such day

As after sunset fadeth in the west,

Which by and by black night doth take away,

Death’s second self, that seals up all in rest.

In me thou see’st the glowing of such fire

That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,

As the death-bed whereon it must expire

Consumed with that which it was nourish’d by.

This thou perceivest, which makes thy love more strong,

To love that well which thou must leave ere long.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, Sonnet 73 is a man telling someone he loves that he is getting old. But the way Shakespeare says it is the whole point. He never uses the word old. Instead he gives three small pictures, one in each of the first three groups of four lines, and asks the person listening to look at them and see him.

The first picture is a tree late in autumn. The speaker says that you can look at him and see a time of year when yellow leaves hang on the branches, "or none, or few." He is already past the beautiful gold stage. The branches shake in the cold, and then comes the line that readers remember most: "Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang." A choir is the part of a church where singers stand, and it is also, of course, a group of singers. So the empty branches are an empty church, and the missing birds are missing voices. The speaker's youth is the singing that has gone. Notice also the word "late," which here means "lately," so the singing stopped not long ago and the silence is still fresh.

The second picture is twilight, the light that lingers in the west after the sun goes down. It will soon be taken away by night, which Shakespeare calls "Death's second self, that seals up all in rest." Sleep is a small rehearsal of death, and night seals everything up the way a coffin or a letter is sealed. The mood is a little darker now, because the fading light is quicker and more final than the fading year.

The third picture is a fire, burned down to glowing coals lying on the ashes of what it once was. Here Shakespeare does something clever. He says the fire lies on "the ashes of his youth," as if the ashes were the fire's own younger self, and then he adds that the fire is dying on that same bed, "Consumed with that which it was nourish'd by." The very wood that fed the flame is what finishes it. Life, in other words, is the thing that uses life up.

Then the final two lines turn everything over. "This thou perceivest, which makes thy love more strong, / To love that well which thou must leave ere long." The listener sees all this decay, and the poem says that seeing it deepens love, because you cherish what you know you will lose. What a first-time reader may miss is that the poem never quite says this love exists. It is a hope, even a gentle request. The speaker is nudging the young person toward loving him harder, and he does it not by complaining, but by making his own fading beautiful enough to deserve it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Sonnet 73 is a very nearly perfect machine, and it is worth looking at how it is built, because the engineering is part of the beauty. The three quatrains, the four-line groups, follow a deliberate downward slope. A year, then a day, then a fire. Each one shrinks the timescale, from months to hours to minutes, so the poem feels like it is closing in on its subject. The images also move from the outdoors to the sky to a hearth in a room, as though the speaker were being drawn closer and closer to the person he is talking to. Then the final couplet, the two rhyming lines, snaps shut like a lid.

The language is wonderfully economical. The line about the choirs is the famous triumph, because it works on three levels at once: bare branches, a ruined church, and lost songs. The English critic William Empson used it in his book Seven Types of Ambiguity as a prime example of how a single phrase can hold several meanings that all feel right together. Part of what makes it work is that Shakespeare never explains the comparison. He lets the reader do the joining.

The sound is just as careful. The poem moves in plain, short, mostly one-syllable words, which suits a man speaking quietly about something painful. Listen to how the repeated "In me thou see'st" at the start of the second and third groups of lines acts almost like a drumbeat, insisting that the listener keep looking.

Now for the weaker side. First, the poem is a little bit manipulative, and honest readers feel that. Its closing argument, that you should love me more because I am dying, is emotionally clever but not very logical. It is the kind of thing a person says when he is afraid of being left, and the poem's calm surface slightly hides that fear. Some readers find this one of its charms. Others find the self-pity faintly stagey.

Second, the three images are not all equally strong. The autumn picture is unforgettable and the fire is intricate, but the twilight quatrain is the most conventional, leaning on the familiar link between sunset and ending. It does its job, yet it sounds more like a literary habit than a discovery.

Third, there is a small oddity in the logic. The speaker keeps saying "in me thou see'st," as if age is a sight anyone can plainly observe, yet the poem is really about his anxiety, not about what is visible. The whole thing is a performance of being resigned, and sometimes you can hear the performance.

Still, these are small complaints against a poem that does so much in so little room. It is the kind of work where the more you slow down, the more you find, and where the apparent simplicity turns out to be hard-won. As a piece of craft, it deserves its reputation.

III. The Impact It Had

It is worth being honest at the start of this section. Sonnet 73 did not have a famous, single dramatic impact the way a political pamphlet might. Its influence is the quieter kind that comes from being read, memorized and borrowed for four hundred years. Still, that influence is large.

Within Shakespeare's own life, the poem belongs to a sequence that was only printed in 1609, long after the sonnet craze of the 1590s had peaked, and the 1609 volume does not seem to have been a bestseller. For a long time the sonnets were treated as a side dish to the plays. In the eighteenth century editors often handled them awkwardly, and in the nineteenth the Romantic poets, especially Wordsworth and Keats, began taking them seriously as personal poetry. Keats, in particular, was deeply devoted to Shakespeare. The idea that Shakespeare was confessing something real in these poems, whether or not that is true, helped turn them into a central part of the English canon.

Within the language, the poem is a source of phrases and pictures that have spread into everyday speech. The phrase "bare ruined choirs" has become a standard shorthand for a place or a life that once held music and no longer does. Writers have used it as a title or as a quote when describing abandoned churches, empty theaters, and the loss of youth. Poets writing about aging, from the Victorians to the present day, often quietly follow the Shakespearean path: do not say you are old, show the leaves and the light.

The poem has also become a constant in classrooms. It is among the sonnets most often assigned to students, partly because its structure is so clean. A teacher can point to the three quatrains and the couplet and say, here is exactly how a Shakespearean sonnet is supposed to move: three developing ideas and then a turn. Because the sequence of images is easy to follow and the vocabulary is mostly simple, students can see the machinery working.

Beyond schools, the poem has been recited on stage and screen and set to music by composers of songs. Actors who are elderly often choose it as an audition or a tribute piece, since it gives them a chance to speak about aging with grace instead of self-pity. It is frequently read at funerals and memorial gatherings.

Finally, its impact on ideas should be mentioned. It is one of the clearest statements in English of a thought that many people feel but few put so well: that awareness of loss is not the enemy of love but part of its fuel. That idea shows up again and again in later poetry, in novels, and in everyday talk about cherishing time. Shakespeare did not invent it, but he gave it one of its most durable forms.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about Sonnet 73? The short answer is that it tells the truth about getting older without shouting about it. Many people come to this poem later in life, or at a hard moment, and find that it says exactly what they felt and could not put into words. There is no bitterness in it and no false cheerfulness. It simply looks, and invites you to look with it.

One thing people admire is the way the poem keeps its dignity. The speaker could have complained, or begged, or tried to joke his way out of the subject. Instead he speaks in a steady, level voice. The calm tone gives the sadness more weight, the way a person who speaks quietly at a funeral can move a room more than someone who sobs.

Readers also love the pictures. There is something almost physical about the shivering boughs and the empty choirs. People who have never studied poetry still remember that line about the birds, because it is both simple to see and strange to think about. A bare tree is something everyone has looked at on a gray November day, and Shakespeare shows them that they have also been looking at a church, and a song, and a life. That sort of sudden doubling is one of the real joys of poetry, and this poem delivers it early.

Another source of love is the final couplet. Most of us have been taught that love is supposed to be a thing that gets easier when things are going well. This poem says the opposite. It says that the knowledge of ending makes love stronger. Many readers hear in that something deeply true about parents and children, about old friends, and about marriages. We tend to hold a person tighter when we remember they will not always be here.

The poem has also been admired by other writers. Poets of very different kinds have returned to it. Some admire its technical neatness, others its emotional restraint. It is the sort of poem that a young writer can study to learn how to build a sonnet, and an older writer can read to learn how to face the subject of time.

Finally, many readers love it simply because it is musical. Even if you do not follow every word, the rhythm of the lines carries you along, with a slow, sinking quality that suits the subject. When read aloud, as many people hear it, the poem sounds like someone setting down a heavy thing carefully. That gentle sound, together with the clear images and the surprise of the ending, explains why this sonnet has kept a place in people's hearts for more than four centuries.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Sonnet 73 is so universally admired that real pushback is mild, and it is worth being honest about that. There was no great scandal around it and no famous review attacking it. But thoughtful readers have raised several questions over the years, and they are worth hearing.

The first concerns the poem's emotional tactics. Some critics and general readers have felt that the closing couplet, which asks the listener to love the speaker more because he is dying, is a form of emotional pressure. If you read the poem as a plea, it can look like a clever way of saying, please do not leave me, dressed in the costume of calm wisdom. Those who feel this way are not accusing Shakespeare of bad writing, only of letting the speaker be a little self-regarding. Others reply that this is exactly the poem's honesty: the speaker is not perfect, and the poem lets us see that.

The second concerns the question of who is speaking and who is listening. Because the poem sits inside the group of sonnets addressed to a young man, readers have long debated what kind of relationship is being described and whether it is friendship, patronage, or desire. Scholars in the nineteenth century were uneasy with the possibility of a romantic reading, and some tried to explain it away. Modern readers are generally more open about the poem's intimacy, yet the underlying facts remain unknown. Nobody can say who the young man was, or whether he existed, and it is fair to say that any confident biography built on these poems is guesswork.

A third complaint is about the imagery. Some critics have said that the three pictures in the poem feel a little too neatly arranged, and that the careful stacking of autumn, dusk and ash makes the poem feel more constructed than felt. This is a version of an old complaint about Elizabethan sonnets in general, that they can be more about showing skill than showing life. It is a fair worry, though many readers feel the skill here is in the service of the feeling.

There is also the matter of the poem's age. Like many old poems, it can be hard for beginners. Words like "choirs," "late," "expire," and "perceivest" have shifted in sense or fallen out of use, and without a note a student can easily miss the double meaning of the church and the singing. Some teachers argue that this makes the poem less welcoming than it seems.

Finally, a few modern readers have noted that the poem's gentle resignation offers little resistance to death. For a reader who wants anger or protest, it can seem too accepting. That is a difference of taste more than a flaw, but it is a real reaction, and it explains why some people prefer other poems about dying.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Sonnet 73 belongs to the great tradition of the English sonnet, a fourteen-line poem with a specific pattern. The form came to England from Italy, where the poet Petrarch had made it famous by writing sonnets to an idealized woman named Laura. English poets of the sixteenth century, such as Thomas Wyatt, Henry Howard, Philip Sidney, and Edmund Spenser, adapted it. Shakespeare's version, with three groups of four lines and a closing pair, gave English a flexible way to build an argument and then flip it.

What makes Shakespeare's sequence unusual is that it breaks the usual rules. Most sonnet sequences were about a man adoring an unreachable woman. Shakespeare's first group is addressed to a young man, and its subject is not only desire but time, aging, and the fear of being forgotten. Sonnet 73 sits at the heart of that theme. It is closely related to other Shakespeare poems on the same subject, such as Sonnet 60, which compares minutes to waves, and Sonnet 64, which sees the ruin of towers and the sea eating into the land. Among these poems about time, 73 may be the most personal, because it is spoken about the speaker's own body.

The poem also looks back to a very old idea in literature: that human life follows the cycle of the seasons. The Bible, classical poetry and medieval writing are full of leaves falling and years turning. What Shakespeare does with it is to attach a living voice and a surprising purpose. The cycle is not just a fact; it is an argument for love.

Looking forward, the poem is an ancestor of a whole line of reflective poems about age and loss. You can hear its echo in the autumnal poems of the Romantics, in Tennyson's "Ulysses," in Dylan Thomas's famous insistence that one should rage against the dying of the light, which takes the opposite view, and in more recent poems that treat old age with plain tenderness. The choice between Shakespeare's acceptance and Thomas's defiance is one of the great conversations in English poetry.

It is also instructive to compare 73 with Sonnet 116, in which love is imagined as a fixed star that never changes. In 73, love is shaped by change. The two poems pull in different directions, and together they show how wide Shakespeare's range was inside a single sequence.

Why read it today? Because it is short, it can be absorbed in a minute, and yet it grows each time you return to it. It is a lesson in how to say a big thing quietly. It is also a reminder that people four hundred years ago were afraid of the same things we are, and found ways to speak about them with beauty. If you read it at twenty and again at sixty, it will be a different poem, and that is about the best praise a poem can earn.
