On Intuitive Knowledge

Bertrand Russell (1912) • 11 min read

All our knowledge of truths depends upon our intuitive knowledge; it is therefore important to consider the nature and scope of such knowledge.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This short chapter comes from Bertrand Russell's little book The Problems of Philosophy, published in 1912 and still one of the most widely read introductions to the subject. Russell was around forty, a leading logician who had just been working on the enormous Principia Mathematica with Alfred North Whitehead, and he wrote the book quickly, for ordinary readers rather than specialists. The chapter takes up a question every thinking person eventually bumps into. We feel we should be able to give reasons for what we believe. But reasons rest on other reasons, so where does the chain stop? Russell's answer is that it stops in things we simply see to be so: basic logical truths, simple arithmetic, what our senses show us right now, and our clear memories. He calls these self-evident, and then makes the interesting move of admitting that self-evidence comes in degrees and can sometimes mislead us. It is a quiet, careful chapter about what our knowledge is built on.

There is a common impression that everything that we believe ought to be capable of proof, or at least of being shown to be highly probable. It is felt by many that a belief for which no reason can be given is an unreasonable belief. In the main, this view is just. Almost all our common beliefs are either inferred, or capable of being inferred, from other beliefs which may be regarded as giving the reason for them. As a rule, the reason has been forgotten, or has even never been consciously present to our minds. Few of us ever ask ourselves, for example, what reason there is to suppose the food we are just going to eat will not turn out to be poison. Yet we feel, when challenged, that a perfectly good reason could be found, even if we are not ready with it at the moment. And in this belief we are usually justified.

But let us imagine some insistent Socrates, who, whatever reason we give him, continues to demand a reason for the reason. We must sooner or later, and probably before very long, be driven to a point where we cannot find any further reason, and where it becomes almost certain that no further reason is even theoretically discoverable. Starting with the common beliefs of daily life, we can be driven back from point to point, until we come to some general principle, or some instance of a general principle, which seems luminously evident, and is not itself capable of being deduced from anything more evident. In most questions of daily life, such as whether our food is likely to be nourishing and not poisonous, we shall be driven back to the inductive principle, which we discussed in Chapter VI. But beyond that, there seems to be no further regress. The principle itself is constantly used in our reasoning, sometimes consciously, sometimes unconsciously; but there is no reasoning which, starting from some simpler self-evident principle, leads us to the principle of induction as its conclusion. And the same holds for other logical principles. Their truth is evident to us, and we employ them in constructing demonstrations; but they themselves, or at least some of them, are incapable of demonstration.

Self-evidence, however, is not confined to those among general principles which are incapable of proof. When a certain number of logical principles have been admitted, the rest can be deduced from them; but the propositions deduced are often just as self-evident as those that were assumed without proof. All arithmetic, moreover, can be deduced from the general principles of logic, yet the simple propositions of arithmetic, such as “two and two are four,” are just as self-evident as the principles of logic.

It would seem, also, though this is more disputable, that there are some self-evident ethical principles, such as “we ought to pursue what is good.”

It should be observed that, in all cases of general principles, particular instances, dealing with familiar things, are more evident than the general principle. For example, the law of contradiction states that nothing can both have a certain property and not have it. This is evident as soon as it is understood, but it is not so evident as that a particular rose which we see cannot be both red and not red. (It is of course possible that parts of the rose may be red and parts not red, or that the rose may be of a shade of pink which we hardly know whether to call red or not; but in the former case it is plain that the rose as a whole is not red, while in the latter case the answer is theoretically definite as soon as we have decided on a precise definition of “red.”) It is usually through particular instances that we come to be able to see the general principle. Only those who are practised in dealing with abstractions can readily grasp a general principle without the help of instances.

In addition to general principles, the other kind of self-evident truths are those immediately derived from sensation. We will call such truths “truths of perception,” and the judgements expressing them we will call “judgements of perception.” But here a certain amount of care is required in getting at the precise nature of the truths that are self-evident. The actual sense-data are neither true nor false. A particular patch of colour which I see, for example, simply exists: it is not the sort of thing that is true or false. It is true that there is such a patch, true that it has a certain shape and degree of brightness, true that it is surrounded by certain other colours. But the patch itself, like everything else in the world of sense, is of a radically different kind from the things that are true or false, and therefore cannot properly be said to be true. Thus whatever self-evident truths may be obtained from our senses must be different from the sense-data from which they are obtained.

It would seem that there are two kinds of self-evident truths of perception, though perhaps in the last analysis the two kinds may coalesce. First, there is the kind which simply asserts the existence of the sense-datum, without in any way analysing it. We see a patch of red, and we judge “there is such-and-such a patch of red,” or more strictly “there is that”; this is one kind of intuitive judgement of perception. The other kind arises when the object of sense is complex, and we subject it to some degree of analysis. If, for instance, we see a “round” patch of red, we may judge “that patch of red is round.” This is again a judgement of perception, but it differs from our previous kind. In our present kind we have a single sense-datum which has both colour and shape: the colour is red and the shape is round. Our judgement analyses the datum into colour and shape, and then recombines them by stating that the red colour is round in shape. Another example of this kind of judgement is “this is to the right of that,” where “this” and “that” are seen simultaneously. In this kind of judgement the sense-datum contains constituents which have some relation to each other, and the judgement asserts that these constituents have this relation.

Another class of intuitive judgements, analogous to those of sense and yet quite distinct from them, are judgements of “memory.” There is some danger of confusion as to the nature of memory, owing to the fact that memory of an object is apt to be accompanied by an image of the object, and yet the image cannot be what constitutes memory. This is easily seen by merely noticing that the image is in the present, whereas what is remembered is known to be in the past. Moreover, we are certainly able to some extent to compare our image with the object remembered, so that we often know, within somewhat wide limits, how far our image is accurate; but this would be impossible, unless the object, as opposed to the image, were in some way before the mind. Thus the essence of memory is not constituted by the image, but by having immediately before the mind an object which is recognized as past. But for the fact of memory in this sense, we should not know that there ever was a past at all, nor should we be able to understand the word “past,” any more than a man born blind can understand the word “light.” Thus there must be intuitive judgements of memory, and it is upon them, ultimately, that all our knowledge of the past depends.

The case of memory, however, raises a difficulty, for it is notoriously fallacious, and thus throws doubt on the trustworthiness of intuitive judgements in general. This difficulty is no light one. But let us first narrow its scope as far as possible. Broadly speaking, memory is trustworthy in proportion to the vividness of the experience and to its nearness in time. If the house next door was struck by lightning half a minute ago, my memory of what I saw and heard will be so reliable that it would be preposterous to doubt whether there had been a flash at all. And the same applies to less vivid experiences, so long as they are recent. I am absolutely certain that half a minute ago I was sitting in the same chair in which I am sitting now. Going backward over the day, I find things of which I am quite certain, other things of which I am almost certain, other things of which I can become certain by thought and by calling up attendant circumstances, and some things of which I am by no means certain. I am quite certain that I ate my breakfast this morning, but if I were as indifferent to my breakfast as a philosopher should be, I should be doubtful. As to the conversation at breakfast, I can recall some of it easily, some with an effort, some only with a large element of doubt, and some not at all. Thus there is a continual gradation in the degree of self-evidence of what I remember, and a corresponding gradation in the trustworthiness of my memory.

Thus the first answer to the difficulty of fallacious memory is to say that memory has degrees of self-evidence, and that these correspond to the degrees of its trustworthiness, reaching a limit of perfect self-evidence and perfect trustworthiness in our memory of events which are recent and vivid.

It would seem, however, that there are cases of very firm belief in a memory which is wholly false. It is probable that, in these cases, what is really remembered, in the sense of being immediately before the mind, is something other than what is falsely believed in, though something generally associated with it. George IV is said to have at last believed that he was at the battle of Waterloo, because he had so often said that he was. In this case, what was immediately remembered was his repeated assertion; the belief in what he was asserting (if it existed) would be produced by association with the remembered assertion, and would therefore not be a genuine case of memory. It would seem that cases of fallacious memory can probably all be dealt with in this way, i.e. they can be shown to be not cases of memory in the strict sense at all.

One important point about self-evidence is made clear by the case of memory, and that is, that self-evidence has degrees: it is not a quality which is simply present or absent, but a quality which may be more or less present, in gradations ranging from absolute certainty down to an almost imperceptible faintness. Truths of perception and some of the principles of logic have the very highest degree of self-evidence; truths of immediate memory have an almost equally high degree. The inductive principle has less self-evidence than some of the other principles of logic, such as “what follows from a true premise must be true.” Memories have a diminishing self-evidence as they become remoter and fainter; the truths of logic and mathematics have (broadly speaking) less self-evidence as they become more complicated. Judgements of intrinsic ethical or aesthetic value are apt to have some self-evidence, but not much.

Degrees of self-evidence are important in the theory of knowledge, since, if propositions may (as seems likely) have some degree of self-evidence without being true, it will not be necessary to abandon all connection between self-evidence and truth, but merely to say that, where there is a conflict, the more self-evident proposition is to be retained and the less self-evident rejected.

It seems, however, highly probable that two different notions are combined in “self-evidence” as above explained; that one of them, which corresponds to the highest degree of self-evidence, is really an infallible guarantee of truth, while the other, which corresponds to all the other degrees, does not give an infallible guarantee, but only a greater or less presumption. This, however, is only a suggestion, which we cannot as yet develop further. After we have dealt with the nature of truth, we shall return to the subject of self-evidence, in connection with the distinction between knowledge and error.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Start with the problem Russell is really facing. Most of us assume that a belief we cannot defend is a belief we should not hold. He begins by granting that this is mostly right. Nearly everything we believe could, in principle, be traced back to some other belief that supports it. His example is wonderfully ordinary: hardly any of us ask what reason we have for thinking the food in front of us is not poison, yet we feel that a good reason could be dug up if someone pressed us.

Then he introduces what he calls an insistent Socrates, a questioner who keeps asking for the reason behind the reason. This is the heart of the chapter, and it is something every child does to every parent. Why? Because. Why? Because. Russell's point is that the chain cannot go on forever. Sooner or later you reach a belief for which no further reason can be given, and not because you are lazy, but because there is nothing more basic to appeal to. In everyday life, he says, we are usually driven back to the principle of induction, the assumption that the future will resemble the past, which he had discussed earlier in the book. Nothing simpler stands behind it. We use it constantly, and we cannot prove it.

So what holds the whole structure up? Russell's answer is that some truths are self-evident. He means truths that, once we understand them, we simply see to be so. The first group is the basic principles of logic, such as the law of contradiction, which says that nothing can both have a property and lack it. The second group includes simple arithmetic: two and two are four. He adds, a little cautiously, that there may be self-evident ethical principles too, such as that we ought to pursue what is good, though he calls that more disputable.

The chapter then widens beyond abstract principles. He notes that particular cases are often more evident than the general rule. We are more certain that this particular rose cannot be both red and not red than we are of the abstract law that covers it. Next come what he calls truths of perception. Here he draws a careful line. The raw patch of colour you are looking at is neither true nor false; it simply exists. What can be true is your judgement about it, such as that there is a patch of red, or that it is round.

Finally he turns to memory, the shakiest of the foundations. His memorable example is George IV, who was said to have come to believe he had fought at Waterloo because he had told the story so often. Russell's explanation is subtle. What the king really remembered was his own repeated telling, and the false belief grew out of that. The lesson he draws is the chapter's most important idea: self-evidence is not an on-off switch. It runs from blazing certainty down to a faint glimmer, and when two beliefs conflict, we keep the more self-evident one. At its top level, he suggests, self-evidence may truly guarantee truth. Below that, it gives only a presumption, and that is a sober, honest place to stop.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The great strength of this chapter is its honesty about where argument runs out. Many philosophers before Russell tried to prove everything from a single indisputable starting point. Russell simply says that you cannot prove everything, that you never could, and that this is no disaster. That is a mature and useful position. He also writes with a clarity that most philosophers never manage. The poisoned-food example, the insistent Socrates, the round patch of red and George IV at Waterloo each do real work. They make an abstract point feel like something you could check against your own mind.

His move toward degrees of self-evidence is the most original and defensible part. It rescues his theory from an obvious objection. If self-evidence guaranteed truth, then every false memory would refute the theory. By saying that self-evidence comes in grades, he can admit that we sometimes feel sure and are wrong, while still insisting that feeling sure is the best evidence we have. That is closer to how knowledge actually works for human beings than any all-or-nothing rule would be.

But the chapter has real weaknesses, and a fair reader should name them. The central difficulty is that self-evidence is a feeling, and feelings are not a very solid foundation. History is full of things that seemed self-evident to careful minds and turned out to be false. Euclid's geometry seemed beyond doubt until other geometries were found, and Russell himself discusses this in a neighbouring chapter. His own logic suffered a crushing blow when he discovered the paradox that bears his name, which showed that an apparently obvious principle about collections led to contradiction. A theory resting on what looks luminously evident has an awkward history to explain.

He half sees this, which is why he suggests, only as a tentative idea, that two different things may be hiding inside the word. One is a perfect guarantee, the other a mere presumption. But he does not say how we tell which one we are experiencing at any given moment, and that is exactly the question a critic would ask. If I cannot tell the infallible kind from the fallible kind from the inside, the distinction does me little good.

There is also a slightly dated feel in the vocabulary. Terms like sense-data and the whole picture of a mind confronting patches of colour belong to a particular moment in early analytic philosophy, and few philosophers today would describe perception that way. The ethical remark, that we simply see that we ought to pursue the good, is brief and hardly argued, and Russell himself later moved away from believing in objective ethical truths. Even so, as an introduction the chapter does what it sets out to do. It makes a hard problem vivid, it does not pretend to more than it can deliver, and it leaves the reader thinking.

III. The Impact It Had

It is hard to point to one dramatic event caused by this particular chapter, because its influence is bound up with the book that contains it. The Problems of Philosophy was published in 1912 in the Home University Library, a series meant to bring serious learning to general readers, and it became one of the best-selling and longest-lived introductions to philosophy ever written. Generations of students, including many who never took another philosophy course, have met epistemology, the study of knowledge, through its pages. This chapter is a standard stop on that tour.

Within philosophy, the chapter belongs to a family of ideas about foundations. The picture it sketches, in which our knowledge rests on a small base of directly evident truths and everything else is built upward by inference, is known as foundationalism. It has been the dominant way of thinking about knowledge for much of the history of Western philosophy, from Descartes onward, and Russell gave it one of its clearest twentieth-century statements. Later philosophers spent decades arguing about whether such a base exists, whether it can be described without circularity, and what to do about the fallibility Russell admits. Much of the discussion of knowledge in the following century can be read as a conversation with, or a rebellion against, the picture found here.

The chapter also helped fix a way of talking. Russell's term sense-data, his distinction between the raw datum and the judgement we make about it, and his careful separation of what is merely there from what can be true or false, were absorbed into the working vocabulary of English-language philosophy for a good while. His student Ludwig Wittgenstein, and later figures such as A. J. Ayer, worked within or against these ideas. Ayer's famous book Language, Truth and Logic is unthinkable without this tradition.

On Russell's own career, the book was a bridge. He was already known among specialists as a logician. This little volume, written quickly and aimed at everyone, was the first step toward the public figure he later became, the author of popular books and essays, the campaigner, and the winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. The habit of explaining hard things plainly, which this chapter shows so well, became his trademark.

As for how it is taught today, the chapter is often used as a gentle entry to the regress problem, the puzzle of how justification can ever come to rest. Teachers pair it with Descartes and with Hume, and then with modern critics of foundationalism, so that students can see both the attraction and the trouble of the idea.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved about Russell's book, and this chapter in particular, begins with the voice. He writes like a very clever friend who wants you to understand, not to be impressed. There is no fog of technical language. When a technical word appears, he stops to explain it. A reader who has never opened a philosophy book can follow the whole argument of this chapter from the first sentence to the last, and that is a rarer achievement than it sounds.

People also respond to how recognisable the problem is. Almost everyone has had the experience of being asked why they believe something and realizing, halfway through the answer, that they are not quite sure. Russell turns that uncomfortable moment into a topic. Readers often report a small shock of recognition when they meet the insistent Socrates, because they have been that questioner, or been questioned by one. The chapter takes an everyday feeling and shows that it points to a deep question.

Another source of affection is the tone of intellectual modesty. Russell was a formidable logician, and it would have been easy to write with swagger. Instead he repeatedly says that he is making a suggestion, that a point is disputable, or that something cannot yet be developed further. That candour builds trust. The reader feels they are being treated as an adult who can handle an unfinished answer, which is exactly what philosophy usually offers.

Fellow thinkers have admired the book as well. It is often named by working philosophers as the book that first drew them into the subject. Part of its charm is its confidence that these questions matter to everyone, not just to professors. Russell's belief that philosophy is for the general reader shines through, and later writers who tried to explain difficult ideas to a wide audience, in science as much as in philosophy, have often followed his example.

There is also real pleasure in the small, vivid touches. George IV convincing himself he had been at Waterloo is funny, and it is also a little chilling, because it shows how a mind can manufacture a false memory out of its own repeated story. The image of the red patch, the rose that cannot be both red and not red, the house next door struck by lightning half a minute ago: these examples stay in the mind long after the argument has faded. Readers remember the picture, and the picture carries the idea with it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most serious objection, raised in various forms ever since, is that self-evidence is too weak to carry the weight Russell puts on it. Critics point out that whether something seems obvious depends on the person, the culture and the time. Claims that once seemed beyond question have collapsed. A theory that grounds knowledge in what looks luminously clear has trouble explaining why we should trust that look, and it risks turning a psychological fact, a feeling of certainty, into a guarantee about the world.

A second line of attack comes from the broader movement against foundationalism. In the middle of the twentieth century, philosophers such as W. V. O. Quine argued that knowledge does not rest on a fixed base at all. It is more like a web or a boat that must be repaired at sea, plank by plank, with no dry dock to step into. Wilfrid Sellars attacked what he called the myth of the given, the idea that raw experience can simply hand us truths without any interpretation. Russell's patch of colour that is neither true nor false but simply there is close to exactly the kind of thing these critics had in mind.

Third, Russell's account of perception has been challenged. The idea that we are directly aware of private sense-data, and only infer the existence of ordinary objects from them, strikes many philosophers as a mistaken picture. It seems to leave us trapped behind a screen of our own sensations. Later thinkers, including J. L. Austin, mocked this way of speaking, arguing that we normally see tables and roses, not intermediary patches.

Fourth, there is a complaint about the ethical remark. Russell offers, almost in passing, that "we ought to pursue what is good" might be self-evident. Critics note that this is hardly informative, since the hard questions begin with what is good. And Russell himself later gave up the idea that ethics could be objectively known in this way, which makes the passage feel like a position its author did not hold for long.

Finally, some readers simply find the chapter inconclusive. Russell raises the problem of fallible memory and then answers it with a hedge: degrees of self-evidence, plus a hunch about two different notions. A reader hoping for a firm solution may feel the chapter ends where the hardest part begins. Defenders reply that this is precisely its virtue, that it refuses to cover over a real difficulty. It is fair to say both reactions are common, and neither is unreasonable.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place this chapter, picture a long argument in Western philosophy about where knowledge comes from. One side, the rationalists, including Descartes and Leibniz, held that reason can reach certain truths on its own. The other, the empiricists, including Locke, Berkeley and Hume, held that all knowledge comes from experience. Russell's chapter sits deliberately between them. He grants the empiricists that our knowledge of what exists comes through experience, and he grants the rationalists that some truths, the principles of logic and mathematics, are known by a kind of direct seeing that is independent of experience. Later chapters of his book make this partnership explicit.

It descends most directly from Descartes's quest for certainty and from Hume's discovery that the inductive principle, on which all our scientific reasoning depends, cannot itself be proved. Russell takes Hume's puzzle seriously and then chooses not to despair. He says, in effect, that some things must be accepted without proof, and that this is just what it means for reasoning to have a starting point. It is also shaped by the work of G. E. Moore, to whom the book is dedicated, who encouraged Russell and his generation to break with the grand idealist systems and ask plain questions in plain language.

What descends from it is, first, the long argument about foundationalism already mentioned, but also a wide family of later work on justification, the basing of belief on evidence, and the nature of intuition. When philosophers today talk about intuitions as evidence, in ethics or in the study of language, they are, knowingly or not, continuing a conversation Russell helped to frame. Interestingly, the debate about whether our firmest convictions can be trusted has returned in a new form in psychology, where studies of memory and bias confirm Russell's worry that vivid feelings of certainty can be false.

Compare him with other writers on the same problem. Descartes searched for a single unshakable point and built upward. Hume reached the edge of the regress and shrugged. Wittgenstein, later, suggested in On Certainty that doubting has to rest on things we do not doubt, a hinge on which questions turn. Russell stands between Descartes's ambition and Wittgenstein's quietism, offering a modest, structured answer.

Why read it now? Because the question is not going away. In a world where people are constantly asked to justify claims and where false confidence spreads easily, a short, clear meditation on where reasons stop is oddly timely. It teaches that you can be careful without being paralysed, and that admitting a limit is different from giving up. That is a habit of mind worth catching, and Russell offers it in a few clean pages.
