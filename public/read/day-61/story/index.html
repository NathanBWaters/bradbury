The Cask of Amontillado

Edgar Allan Poe (1846) • 12 min read

“I said to him: “My dear Fortunato, you are luckily met. How remarkably well you are looking today! But I have received a pipe of what passes for Amontillado, and I have my doubts.””

Key Context & Why It Matters

Edgar Allan Poe published "The Cask of Amontillado" in Godey's Lady's Book in November 1846, near the end of a hard, short life. Poe was an orphan, a working magazine editor and a fierce critic who made enemies easily, and he was writing at the time of bitter literary quarrels, money trouble and his wife Virginia's long illness. He had also argued that a short story should be built to deliver one single effect, with nothing wasted. This tale may be the cleanest proof of that idea. It is short, and you can read it in ten minutes. A man named Montresor tells how he took revenge on a friend named Fortunato, and he tells it with a calm that should disturb you. It matters because it is a perfect machine of dread, and because its narrator is one of fiction's great unreliable voices. Listen for what Montresor never explains, and for who he might be talking to.

The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could; but when he ventured upon insult, I vowed revenge. You, who so well know the nature of my soul, will not suppose, however, that I gave utterance to a threat. At length I would be avenged; this was a point definitively settled⁠—but the very definitiveness with which it was resolved, precluded the idea of risk. I must not only punish, but punish with impunity. A wrong is unredressed when retribution overtakes its redresser. It is equally unredressed when the avenger fails to make himself felt as such to him who has done the wrong.

It must be understood, that neither by word nor deed had I given Fortunato cause to doubt my good will. I continued, as was my wont, to smile in his face, and he did not perceive that my smile now was at the thought of his immolation.

He had a weak point⁠—this Fortunato⁠—although in other regards he was a man to be respected and even feared. He prided himself on his connoisseurship in wine. Few Italians have the true virtuoso spirit. For the most part their enthusiasm is adopted to suit the time and opportunity⁠—to practise imposture upon the British and Austrian millionaires. In painting and gemmary, Fortunato, like his countrymen, was a quack⁠—but in the matter of old wines he was sincere. In this respect I did not differ from him materially: I was skilful in the Italian vintages myself, and bought largely whenever I could.

It was about dusk, one evening during the supreme madness of the carnival season, that I encountered my friend. He accosted me with excessive warmth, for he had been drinking much. The man wore motley. He had on a tight-fitting parti-striped dress, and his head was surmounted by the conical cap and bells. I was so pleased to see him, that I thought I should never have done wringing his hand.

I said to him: “My dear Fortunato, you are luckily met. How remarkably well you are looking today! But I have received a pipe of what passes for Amontillado, and I have my doubts.”

“How?” said he. “Amontillado? A pipe? Impossible! And in the middle of the carnival!”

“I have my doubts,” I replied; “and I was silly enough to pay the full Amontillado price without consulting you in the matter. You were not to be found, and I was fearful of losing a bargain.”

“Amontillado!”

“I have my doubts.”

“Amontillado!”

“And I must satisfy them.”

“Amontillado!”

“As you are engaged, I am on my way to Luchesi. If anyone has a critical turn, it is he. He will tell me⁠—”

“Luchesi cannot tell Amontillado from Sherry.”

“And yet some fools will have it that his taste is a match for your own.”

“Come, let us go.”

“Whither?”

“To your vaults.”

“My friend, no; I will not impose upon your good nature. I perceive you have an engagement. Luchesi⁠—”

“I have no engagement;⁠—come.”

“My friend, no. It is not the engagement, but the severe cold with which I perceive you are afflicted. The vaults are insufferably damp. They are encrusted with nitre.”

“Let us go, nevertheless. The cold is merely nothing. Amontillado! You have been imposed upon. And as for Luchesi, he cannot distinguish Sherry from Amontillado.”

Thus speaking, Fortunato possessed himself of my arm. Putting on a mask of black silk, and drawing a roquelaire closely about my person, I suffered him to hurry me to my palazzo.

There were no attendants at home; they had absconded to make merry in honor of the time. I had told them that I should not return until the morning, and had given them explicit orders not to stir from the house. These orders were sufficient, I well knew, to insure their immediate disappearance, one and all, as soon as my back was turned.

I took from their sconces two flambeaux, and giving one to Fortunato, bowed him through several suites of rooms to the archway that led into the vaults. I passed down a long and winding staircase, requesting him to be cautious as he followed. We came at length to the foot of the descent, and stood together on the damp ground of the catacombs of the Montresors.

The gait of my friend was unsteady, and the bells upon his cap jingled as he strode.

“The pipe,” said he.

“It is farther on,” said I; “but observe the white web-work which gleams from these cavern walls.”

He turned towards me, and looked into my eyes with two filmy orbs that distilled the rheum of intoxication.

“Nitre?” he asked, at length.

“Nitre,” I replied. “How long have you had that cough?”

“Ugh! ugh! ugh!⁠—ugh! ugh! ugh!⁠—ugh! ugh! ugh!⁠—ugh! ugh! ugh!⁠—ugh! ugh! ugh!”

My poor friend found it impossible to reply for many minutes.

“It is nothing,” he said, at last.

“Come,” I said, with decision, “we will go back; your health is precious. You are rich, respected, admired, beloved; you are happy, as once I was. You are a man to be missed. For me it is no matter. We will go back; you will be ill, and I cannot be responsible. Besides, there is Luchesi⁠—”

“Enough,” he said; “the cough is a mere nothing; it will not kill me. I shall not die of a cough.”

“True⁠—true,” I replied; “and, indeed, I had no intention of alarming you unnecessarily⁠—but you should use all proper caution. A draught of this Medoc will defend us from the damps.”

Here I knocked off the neck of a bottle which I drew from a long row of its fellows that lay upon the mould.

“Drink,” I said, presenting him the wine.

He raised it to his lips with a leer. He paused and nodded to me familiarly, while his bells jingled.

“I drink,” he said, “to the buried that repose around us.”

“And I to your long life.”

He again took my arm, and we proceeded.

“These vaults,” he said, “are extensive.”

“The Montresors,” I replied, “were a great and numerous family.”

“I forget your arms.”

“A huge human foot d’or, in a field azure; the foot crushes a serpent rampant whose fangs are imbedded in the heel.”

“And the motto?”

“Nemo me impune lacessit.”

“Good!” he said.

The wine sparkled in his eyes and the bells jingled. My own fancy grew warm with the Medoc. We had passed through walls of piled bones, with casks and puncheons intermingling, into the inmost recesses of the catacombs. I paused again, and this time I made bold to seize Fortunato by an arm above the elbow.

“The nitre!” I said: “see, it increases. It hangs like moss upon the vaults. We are below the river’s bed. The drops of moisture trickle among the bones. Come, we will go back ere it is too late. Your cough⁠—”

“It is nothing,” he said; “let us go on. But first, another draught of the Medoc.”

I broke and reached him a flagon of De Grâve. He emptied it at a breath. His eyes flashed with a fierce light. He laughed and threw the bottle upwards with a gesticulation I did not understand.

I looked at him in surprise. He repeated the movement⁠—a grotesque one.

“You do not comprehend?” he said.

“Not I,” I replied.

“Then you are not of the brotherhood.”

“How?”

“You are not of the masons.”

“Yes, yes,” I said, “yes, yes.”

“You? Impossible! A mason?”

“A mason,” I replied.

“A sign,” he said.

“It is this,” I answered, producing a trowel from beneath the folds of my roquelaire.

“You jest,” he exclaimed, recoiling a few paces. “But let us proceed to the Amontillado.”

“Be it so,” I said, replacing the tool beneath the cloak, and again offering him my arm. He leaned upon it heavily. We continued our route in search of the Amontillado. We passed through a range of low arches, descended, passed on, and descending again, arrived at a deep crypt, in which the foulness of the air caused our flambeaux rather to glow than flame.

At the most remote end of the crypt there appeared another less spacious. Its walls had been lined with human remains, piled to the vault overhead, in the fashion of the great catacombs of Paris. Three sides of this interior crypt were still ornamented in this manner. From the fourth the bones had been thrown down, and lay promiscuously upon the earth, forming at one point a mound of some size. Within the wall thus exposed by the displacing of the bones, we perceived a still interior recess, in depth about four feet, in width three, in height six or seven. It seemed to have been constructed for no especial use in itself, but formed merely the interval between two of the colossal supports of the roof of the catacombs, and was backed by one of their circumscribing walls of solid granite.

It was in vain that Fortunato, uplifting his dull torch, endeavored to pry into the depths of the recess. Its termination the feeble light did not enable us to see.

“Proceed,” I said; “herein is the Amontillado. As for Luchesi⁠—”

“He is an ignoramus,” interrupted my friend, as he stepped unsteadily forward, while I followed immediately at his heels. In an instant he had reached the extremity of the niche, and finding his progress arrested by the rock, stood stupidly bewildered. A moment more and I had fettered him to the granite. In its surface were two iron staples, distant from each other about two feet, horizontally. From one of these depended a short chain, from the other a padlock. Throwing the links about his waist, it was but the work of a few seconds to secure it. He was too much astounded to resist. Withdrawing the key I stepped back from the recess.

“Pass your hand,” I said, “over the wall; you cannot help feeling the nitre. Indeed it is very damp. Once more let me implore you to return. No? Then I must positively leave you. But I must first render you all the little attentions in my power.”

“The Amontillado!” ejaculated my friend, not yet recovered from his astonishment.

“True,” I replied; “the Amontillado.”

As I said these words I busied myself among the pile of bones of which I have before spoken. Throwing them aside, I soon uncovered a quantity of building stone and mortar. With these materials and with the aid of my trowel, I began vigorously to wall up the entrance of the niche.

I had scarcely laid the first tier of my masonry when I discovered that the intoxication of Fortunato had in a great measure worn off. The earliest indication I had of this was a low moaning cry from the depth of the recess. It was not the cry of a drunken man. There was then a long and obstinate silence. I laid the second tier, and the third, and the fourth; and then I heard the furious vibrations of the chain. The noise lasted for several minutes, during which, that I might hearken to it with the more satisfaction, I ceased my labors and sat down upon the bones. When at last the clanking subsided, I resumed the trowel, and finished without interruption the fifth, the sixth, and the seventh tier. The wall was now nearly upon a level with my breast. I again paused, and holding the flambeaux over the mason-work, threw a few feeble rays upon the figure within.

A succession of loud and shrill screams, bursting suddenly from the throat of the chained form, seemed to thrust me violently back. For a brief moment I hesitated⁠—I trembled. Unsheathing my rapier, I began to grope with it about the recess; but the thought of an instant reassured me. I placed my hand upon the solid fabric of the catacombs, and felt satisfied. I reapproached the wall. I replied to the yells of him who clamored. I reechoed⁠—I aided⁠—I surpassed them in volume and in strength. I did this, and the clamorer grew still.

It was now midnight, and my task was drawing to a close. I had completed the eighth, the ninth, and the tenth tier. I had finished a portion of the last and the eleventh; there remained but a single stone to be fitted and plastered in. I struggled with its weight; I placed it partially in its destined position. But now there came from out the niche a low laugh that erected the hairs upon my head. It was succeeded by a sad voice, which I had difficulty in recognising as that of the noble Fortunato. The voice said⁠—

“Ha! ha! ha!⁠—he! he!⁠—a very good joke indeed⁠—an excellent jest. We will have many a rich laugh about it at the palazzo⁠—he! he! he!⁠—over our wine⁠—he! he! he!”

“The Amontillado!” I said.

“He! he! he!⁠—he! he! he!⁠—yes, the Amontillado. But is it not getting late? Will not they be awaiting us at the palazzo, the Lady Fortunato and the rest? Let us be gone.”

“Yes,” I said, “let us be gone.”

“For the love of God, Montressor!”

“Yes,” I said, “for the love of God!”

But to these words I hearkened in vain for a reply. I grew impatient. I called aloud:

“Fortunato!”

No answer. I called again:

“Fortunato!”

No answer still. I thrust a torch through the remaining aperture and let it fall within. There came forth in return only a jingling of the bells. My heart grew sick⁠—on account of the dampness of the catacombs. I hastened to make an end of my labor. I forced the last stone into its position; I plastered it up. Against the new masonry I re-erected the old rampart of bones. For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them. In pace requiescat!

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface this is a simple revenge story. Montresor, an Italian nobleman, says that Fortunato has injured him a thousand times and then insulted him. We never learn what the insult was. That missing detail is the first thing to notice. Montresor wants us to accept his cause as obviously just, but he never proves it, and the less he proves, the more we suspect that his grievance is partly in his own head. He opens by telling us that he must not only punish but "punish with impunity." He wants a crime with no consequences, and a victim who understands exactly who is punishing him.

The plot is a slow trap baited with vanity. It is carnival season, when the whole city is drunk and masked, so nobody will notice two men slipping away. Fortunato is already drunk and dressed as a jester, in motley with a cap and bells. Montresor knows that Fortunato's weak point is his pride as a judge of wine, so he mentions that he has bought a cask of Amontillado, a fine sherry, and that he is unsure whether it is genuine. He then pretends to consult another expert, Luchesi, which is exactly the right way to goad a proud man into insisting that he come himself. Montresor's politeness throughout is the horror of the story. He keeps urging Fortunato to turn back for the sake of his cough, knowing that each warning makes Fortunato more determined to go on.

The setting does a great deal of the work. They descend into the family catacombs, a damp underground burial place, where white niter, a mineral crust, clings to the walls and bones are piled in heaps. As they go deeper, the descent feels like a trip into a mind. The jester costume turns into a bitter joke, because the fool is led by his own foolishness into a hole. Montresor's family arms show a foot crushing a snake whose fangs are in the heel, with the motto "Nemo me impune lacessit," which means no one attacks me and gets away with it. That is Montresor describing himself, and notice that he is both the foot and the snake, the crusher and the one who strikes back.

The gesture with the trowel is a small joke, too. Fortunato makes a secret sign of the Masons, and Montresor says he is a mason as well, and produces a trowel from under his cloak. He means the bricklayer's kind, and the pun is cruelly apt. Then comes the walling up. Fortunato's drunken laughter fades into silence, then into a few cries, and finally into a jingling of bells. Montresor says his heart grew sick, "on account of the dampness of the catacombs," which is his own explanation for any pang of conscience. He finishes the wall and, as the last words say, no one has disturbed the bones for fifty years. "In pace requiescat," he adds, rest in peace.

That last phrase is where the deepest layer lies. The story opens as if addressed to a listener who knows Montresor well, and it ends fifty years later. Many readers imagine a deathbed confession, possibly to a priest. If that is right, the calm voice has not been at peace for half a century. A man who truly felt justified would not need to tell the story, and would not need to tell us his heart grew sick. Even the most controlled criminal in the tale seems to keep the murder close.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, "The Cask of Amontillado" is close to flawless, and that is worth saying with some care, because Poe has plenty of flaws elsewhere. Here the engineering is exact. The story gives us a first-person voice that is courteous, patient and chillingly rational. Every line of dialogue is double-edged. When Fortunato says he will not die of a cough, Montresor answers, "True, true," and we realize he is agreeing because the cough is not what will kill him. The irony is built into almost every exchange, and it never feels like Poe winking at us over the narrator's shoulder. It feels like the narrator's own private amusement.

The pacing is what makes it so effective. The opening paragraphs lay out the plan, the middle takes us through the descent in a series of small, escalating steps, and the end is quick and almost brutally plain. Poe resists the temptation to linger over the death. We hear the chain, the hammer of the trowel, the trembling cry, the jingle of bells. A longer scene would be less frightening. Instead of a villain who gloats, we get a man who is satisfied and slightly queasy, and that mix is very hard to forget.

The weaknesses are real, but small. The story depends on some conveniences. It is hard to believe that nobody in a crowded household would notice the master leading a drunk guest away, and Montresor tells us he gave the servants orders that guaranteed they would leave. The line is almost comic, and it reads more like a shortcut than a subtle piece of characterization. The plot also asks us to accept that a clever man like Fortunato would fall for something so obvious. Poe covers this by making Fortunato drunk and vain, and mostly it works, but a skeptical reader can feel the strings.

There is also a certain flatness in the characters. Fortunato is a victim without much depth, and the story gives us little reason to think of him as more than a fool who gets what we are invited to think he deserves. You could argue that this is the point, since we see him only through Montresor's contempt. But the effect is that the story is more interesting as a study of Montresor than as a drama between two men. If you want psychological depth in both figures, you will not find it here.

Finally, there is the question of Poe's love of ornament. Compared with the lush and overwrought prose of "The Fall of the House of Usher," this story is restrained. The word choices are formal, a bit antique, and sometimes stagey. A few phrases, like "supreme madness of the carnival season," come close to melodrama. Yet because the narrator himself is theatrical, the style fits him. The real strength of the writing is the gap between the elegant tone and the monstrous act. A reader who tries to find a better solution to the problem Poe set himself will have a hard time, and that is the best compliment a critic can give.

III. The Impact It Had

The immediate impact was modest. The story appeared in a popular women's magazine in 1846, and Poe died in 1849, so he never saw it become what it is now. In his lifetime his reputation rested more on "The Raven," on his detective stories and on his reviews. Over the following century, though, "The Cask of Amontillado" slowly rose to the top of his short fiction in the classroom, and today it is among the most frequently taught stories in American schools. Part of the reason is simple. It is short, it has a clean plot and it rewards close reading, so teachers can use it to teach irony, point of view and unreliable narration all at once.

Its influence on crime fiction is large. Poe is often credited with helping to invent the modern detective story, and in this tale he also helped invent the murderer's-eye view of crime. Later writers who tell a story from inside a killer's head, from the polite psychopath of later pulp to the quiet voices of modern thrillers, are working in a tradition that this story helped to define. The idea that a perfect crime is not the point, and that the real drama lies in the criminal's need to confess, runs through "The Tell-Tale Heart," "The Black Cat" and many later novels about guilt.

The story has also spread into popular culture. It was adapted for the stage, for television and for film many times, including the 1960s horror anthology films that made Poe a staple of the era's low-budget gothic cinema. It has been parodied, quoted and borrowed in countless places, and the image of a man being bricked into a wall has become shorthand for a certain kind of horror. Writers have often turned to it when they want a villain who is polite. Even people who have never read Poe usually know the outline of the plot.

It is also part of how Poe's whole career is understood. In 1846, the same year as this story, Poe published his essay "The Philosophy of Composition," which describes how he supposedly wrote "The Raven" by calculating each effect. Whatever one thinks of that essay's honesty, it set out a view of writing as deliberate construction rather than inspiration. "The Cask of Amontillado" is often presented as the short story that most completely lives up to the theory, a piece where every sentence moves toward one result. That has made it a model for later teachers of creative writing who emphasize economy.

Finally, the story has had a quiet impact on how readers think about the relationship between narrator and audience. By making the narrator address someone directly, and by hinting at a long delay between crime and telling, Poe gave later writers a strategy to imitate, in which the way a story is told reveals as much as what happens. That technique shows up in confessional novels and in modern first-person fiction of every kind. It is a small story, but its fingerprints are on a lot of larger ones.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love this story first for its sheer clarity. Many people meet Poe through longer, more tangled tales, with their elaborate sentences and strange landscapes, and find them hard going. This one is quick, and the story is easy to follow on a first reading. It begins with a promise of revenge, it moves steadily toward the burial, and it never loses you. Younger readers in particular often say that it is the story that made them see why anyone reads Poe, because it delivers suspense without needing a lot of background.

They also admire the voice. Montresor is charming in a terrible way. He is educated, witty and attentive, and he treats his victim with the courtesy of a good host. Readers enjoy the dark comedy of this, the way he calls Fortunato "my friend" and fusses over his health while leading him to his death. There is a pleasure in watching the trap close, and the story lets us feel that pleasure uneasily. We know we should not be amused, and we are, and Poe knows we are. That small moral discomfort is part of what makes the story stay in the mind.

Another reason it resonates is the setting. The carnival, with its masks and bells and wine, contrasts perfectly with the cold stone of the catacombs. A celebration above and a tomb below is an easy image to hold, and readers remember it long after they have forgotten other details. The jester's bells, which ring at the very end, are among the most haunting touches in the story. Many people recall that last sound more clearly than anything else, the little jingling that is all that is left of a living man.

Fellow writers have long admired the story as a lesson in economy. It does so much with so few words. It sets up a world, two characters, a grievance and a murder in about two thousand words, and it leaves a question open that readers still argue about. Writers who are tempted to explain everything can learn from how little Poe explains. He never tells us the insult, never tells us who is listening and never tells us whether Montresor repents.

Finally, readers respond to the ambiguity. Because the insult is never described, you can read Montresor as an avenger with real cause, as a snob with a wounded ego or as a man whose mind has slipped. Because the last line hints at remorse or at least memory, you can read the tale as a confession or as a boast. Readers return to it because it does not settle. The best moments in any great short story are the ones that make you want to turn back to the beginning, and this one has several.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

For all its fame, the story has drawn its share of objections. The earliest reviewers of Poe's work often praised his ingenuity but worried about his taste, and some nineteenth-century critics thought his stories were morbid or merely clever. Because this particular tale was not widely discussed when it appeared, much of the documented criticism comes later. In general, the complaints are the same ones that have followed Poe throughout his career: that he is more interested in effect than in meaning, that his characters are puppets, and that his gloom is a performance.

A more specific objection is that the story is a cold exercise. Some readers find that it has no moral center. There is no one to sympathize with, since Fortunato is a drunken bore and Montresor is a murderer, and the story never invites us to judge either of them in a way that feels fully earned. For these critics, the tale is a clever machine but not much more. They would rather have fiction that engages with the consequences of violence than one that polishes it. Others respond that the cold tone is itself a moral stance, since it forces the reader to notice how seductive a rational voice can be.

There is also a long argument about whether the story is truly a tragedy of revenge or a mere crime. Some scholars have pointed out that Montresor never shows evidence that Fortunato wronged him, and so the story may be read as the delusion of a paranoid. That is more an interpretation than an objection, but some readers find it frustrating. If the wrong is imaginary, they say, then the story loses its charge, because we are just watching a madman. Others find it far richer for that reason. The disagreement shows how openly the story invites competing readings.

Another set of complaints concerns the portrayal of Italy and Italians. The narrator remarks that few Italians have the true virtuoso spirit, and that most of them practice imposture on wealthy foreign visitors. This is the narrator's snobbery, not necessarily the author's, but some readers feel that Poe leans on stereotypes of the exotic, scheming south that were popular in English-language Gothic writing. It is a minor point, and most modern readers treat it as a feature of Montresor's character, but it appears in discussions of the story's background.

Lastly, a few critics have tried to read the story through Poe's biography, suggesting it reflects his quarrels with rivals in the literary world. That reading is popular but unprovable, and scholars caution against treating a tale as a diary entry. The reasonable position is that Poe was angry and combative in these years and that the story may have drawn some energy from that, but that its power does not depend on any real feud. Good critics resist the temptation to explain the story away by turning it into gossip, and the story survives all such attempts.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"The Cask of Amontillado" belongs to the American Gothic tradition, and more specifically to the dark strain of Romanticism in which writers turned from the sunny optimism of their age toward guilt, madness and death. Poe's contemporaries Nathaniel Hawthorne and Herman Melville explored sin and obsession in their own ways, but Poe was the most extreme in using the first-person confessional voice of someone who is morally damaged. He drew on the older European Gothic of writers like Ann Radcliffe and E. T. A. Hoffmann, with its crypts, castles and doomed aristocrats, and narrowed it down to something intimate and psychological.

Within Poe's own work, this story sits beside the other tales of murderers who narrate their crimes, especially "The Tell-Tale Heart" and "The Black Cat." What distinguishes it is its coolness. The narrators of those two tales are visibly unraveling, whereas Montresor holds himself together to the end. That makes him a distant ancestor of the controlled, intelligent killers who appear in later crime fiction. Think of the genteel monster, the man whose manners are a mask. You can trace that figure through Robert Browning's dramatic monologue "My Last Duchess," which Poe's story resembles in spirit, and forward to novels where the horror comes from a seductively reasonable voice.

The story also fits into the history of the short story as an art form. Poe argued in his reviews that a short tale should be readable in a single sitting and aimed at a single effect. This story proves the claim. It looks forward to the tightly compressed work of writers like Ambrose Bierce, Saki and, later, Shirley Jackson, whose "The Lottery" similarly makes a quiet voice carry horror. Ray Bradbury himself, who loved Poe from childhood, gave Poe's tale a direct tribute in "Usher II," a chapter of The Martian Chronicles in which a man is walled up with Amontillado in a replica of Poe's world, and much of his own short fiction owes something to this kind of tight, ironic dread.

Beyond fiction, the tale has become part of the shared cultural vocabulary. A reference to a man being walled up alive is understood without explanation. The phrase "for the love of God" gains an extra bitterness once you know Fortunato's last words and Montresor's cruel echo. The story's central trick of using friendship as a weapon has been borrowed countless times in thrillers.

It matters today for a simple reason. It is short enough to read in one sitting, and good enough that you will want to read it again. It also teaches something that still seems urgent: that a calm, articulate, reasonable voice can describe something monstrous, and that we should always ask what the speaker is leaving out. In an age full of persuasive voices, that is a useful lesson to learn from a story about a cask of wine that does not exist.
