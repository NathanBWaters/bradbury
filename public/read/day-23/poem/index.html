Nightly Poem • Pastoral & Elegy

The Lake Isle of Innisfree

W. B. Yeats (1890) • 3 min read

Standing on grey London pavement, a homesick young Irish poet hears lake water lapping in his deep heart’s core and vows to build a cabin of clay and wattles.

Key Context & Why It Matters

W. B. Yeats wrote this poem in 1888, when he was twenty-three and living in London, far from the Irish countryside where he had spent his boyhood summers. One day, walking down Fleet Street, he noticed a little fountain in a shop window, a thin jet of water balancing a toy ball. The sound and sight brought back Lough Gill in County Sligo, and a small island on it called Innisfree. He had also been reading Thoreau's Walden, about living simply in a hut by a pond. The poem appeared in 1890, and it became Yeats's most popular work, though he came to feel a little ambivalent about it. It matters as a founding document of the Irish literary revival, a movement to build a modern Irish literature out of Irish places, speech and legend. Its meaning is a longing. The speaker dreams of peace in a simple cabin, and then admits that the real place for that peace is inside him, wherever he happens to be standing.

I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,

And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made:

Nine bean rows will I have there, a hive for the honey bee,

And live alone in the bee-loud glade.

And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,

Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;

There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,

And evening full of the linnet’s wings.

I will arise and go now, for always night and day

I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;

While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey

I hear it in the deep heart’s core.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, this is a very simple poem. A man says he will get up and go to an island, build a small cabin, plant beans, keep bees, and live in peace. That is the whole plot. But what the poem is really doing becomes clear only when you notice where the speaker is standing, and that he never actually leaves.

Look at the first stanza. "I will arise and go now" has the sound of a vow, almost a line from scripture. In fact the phrasing echoes the biblical story of the prodigal son, who says he will arise and go to his father. The cabin he plans is "of clay and wattles made," meaning mud and woven sticks, the humblest building material there is. Nine rows of beans and a hive of bees are all he wants. Notice the modesty of those details. The dream is not of luxury or glory. It is of self-sufficiency and quiet. "The bee-loud glade" is a wonderful phrase, because it makes silence itself buzz. The peace he imagines is not empty. It is full of small living sounds.

The second stanza gives the heart of the vision. Peace, he says, "comes dropping slow," and it drops "from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings." Peace is described as something that falls like dew, gently, from the morning mist down to the ground. The following lines compress a whole day into three colors of light. Midnight is "all a glimmer," noon is "a purple glow," and evening is "full of the linnet's wings." The island has a light of its own, as if each hour had its own soft color.

Then comes the third stanza, and the poem changes direction. He repeats "I will arise and go now," but now he gives the reason, and the reason is painful. "I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey." The word "grey" is the only dull color in the whole poem, and it describes the city. Meanwhile, "always night and day / I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore." Importantly, he hears it "in the deep heart's core."

That is the secret of the poem. The island is not truly a place on a map. It exists in his memory and imagination, and it is calling to him from inside. The poem is therefore less a plan than an ache. The speaker is a homesick man on a London street who carries a lake inside his chest, and who knows that no cabin in Sligo could fully answer the longing.

A first-time reader might miss how the music mirrors this. The long lines in the first two stanzas move slowly, like water, while the final stanza's last line is the quietest. The poem does not end with the moment he reaches the island. It ends with the sound he cannot stop hearing. In that sense it is a poem about how a place can live in a person who has left it, and how the inner place may be truer than the outer one.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The first thing to say, in fairness, is that the poem's best qualities are sound qualities. It is one of the most musical short poems in English. The long, swaying lines, the repeated "I will arise," and the soft liquid consonants of "lake water lapping with low sounds" create a spell that you feel before you understand it. Yeats himself noted that this was the first poem in which he had found a rhythm of his own, one that came from his own speech. Reading it aloud, the rhythm does feel natural and unforced, closer to the cadence of someone talking to himself than to the stiff verse of the period.

The structure is tight. In three stanzas, Yeats gives an announcement, a picture, and a confession, and the third stanza reframes the first two. That quiet turn, from imagined future to present ache, is a real piece of craft. It means the poem repays rereading, because once you know where the speaker stands, the dream of the island takes on a sadder light.

The imagery is wonderfully concrete in places. The nine bean rows are a perfect detail, precise and homely, and the bees give the poem its quiet hum. The idea of peace "dropping slow" is a small stroke of genius.

But a critic has to be honest about what has aged less well. The poem is a young man's poem, and parts of it have the slightly dreamy, decorative feel of the 1890s. Words like "glade" and "linnet's wings" and the inverted "I will arise" belong to the vocabulary of late Victorian verse, in which nature is pretty and a little unreal. Today the island can feel like a postcard. It is a gentle fantasy of simplicity, written by someone who had never actually had to dig bean rows for a living.

There is also some tension between the poem's pose and its reality. A man who truly wanted to live in a clay hut would probably not find his peace in a cultured poem about it. The sentimental escapism of the idea, the wish to retreat from modern life to a sunlit simplicity, is a mood that Yeats himself came to mistrust. He later turned against the dreamy manner of his early work, and he spent decades working to make his verse harder, plainer, and more modern. By the standards of his own late work, the poem looks pale.

Still, the weaknesses are mostly the weaknesses of its time. What survives is the spell. The poem's trick is that it almost admits it is a fantasy. The final stanza removes the safety net, saying in effect that the island is a thing he hears rather than a thing he will reach. That honesty, tucked inside a very pretty poem, is what saves it from mere sentiment. It is an imperfect, slightly dated, and nevertheless genuinely beautiful piece of writing, and its modest ambition, one clear feeling in three clean stanzas, is largely achieved.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's impact has been very large, partly because it was so easy to love. It was published in the National Observer in December 1890 and then collected in Yeats's early book The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics in 1892. For a young poet, it was an introduction to a wide public. For decades afterward it was the poem people associated with his name.

Within Irish literature, it helped launch the movement known as the Irish Literary Revival, or the Celtic Twilight, after the title of one of Yeats's own books. The idea was to create a distinctly Irish literature in English, drawing on Irish landscapes, folklore, and legend instead of copying English models. The poem's attention to a specific Irish place, a real island on a real lake, made it a model. Ireland at the time was under British rule, and cultural identity was becoming a political question. A poem that treated an Irish lake as worthy of serious song carried a certain national meaning even though it contains no politics at all.

It is also a landmark in a broader tradition, the poetry of the retreat. Many readers have noticed its kinship with Thoreau's Walden, which Yeats himself said had influenced the idea, and with an older tradition of poems about leaving the city for a simple rural life. The poem's phrases have become part of ordinary English. People who have never heard of Yeats borrow the line about nine bean rows, or speak of "going to Innisfree," as a shorthand for the dream of escape.

That shorthand has had a surprising commercial and cultural afterlife. The name Innisfree has been attached to gardens, to places in fiction, and to products. In the 1952 film The Quiet Man, the Irish village where the hero, an American returning to Ireland, finds his home is called Innisfree, a clear tribute to the poem's mood of rural belonging. Many ordinary people first meet Yeats through that name.

The poem has also been set to music and recited at weddings, funerals, and public occasions. It has been a favorite for people missing home, including the Irish diaspora around the world. A poem about homesickness has comforted people who live far from where they began.

In the classroom it is often one of the first Yeats poems taught, since it is short, melodic, and clear. It gives students an entry into Yeats's early style, which they can then compare with his later, harder work such as Easter, 1916 and Sailing to Byzantium. Seen that way, it is the first step in one of the great careers in modern poetry, the starting point from which Yeats would travel a very long way.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this poem, first of all, because it says what so many people feel and few can put into words: the pull of a place you have left, or a life you have not lived. Almost everyone has a version of Innisfree, a lake, a village, a childhood house, a quiet corner that lives in the mind. The poem gives that feeling a name and a sound.

They have loved its simplicity. In an age when many poems are difficult, this one asks nothing of the reader except to listen. There are no obscure references, no puzzles, and the vocabulary is plain. A child can enjoy it, and so can someone who has read poetry for sixty years. That range is rare, and it is a large reason why the poem has remained in print and in memory for well over a century.

Many people say they love its music above everything. The poem is a pleasure to say aloud, and because the lines are long and slow, speaking it brings a kind of calm. Readers often report that they can recite it from memory without ever having tried to learn it, because the rhythm carries the words. That quality makes it especially well suited to listening, which suits modern readers who hear poems read rather than see them printed.

Another source of affection is the poem's gentle honesty. The ending, in which the speaker admits that the peace he longs for is something he carries inside, strikes many readers as the true, moving center. People who live in cities, and who fantasize about escaping to a cottage, recognize the way the dream follows them onto the grey pavement. The poem does not mock this longing. It respects it.

Fellow writers have admired it too, though sometimes with qualification. Many Irish poets in later generations have acknowledged that a poem like this opened a door. By treating a Sligo lake as a subject worthy of high lyric poetry, Yeats gave later writers permission to write about their own local places. There is a direct line from the spirit of Innisfree to the way later poets approached their own landscapes.

And Yeats himself, despite his later reservations, never quite lost his affection for it. By many accounts he remained fond of the poem for the way it came to him, as a sudden recollection in a city street, and he took a certain satisfaction in having made, so young, something that people actually wanted to read. For a writer who spent most of his life refusing to repeat himself, that is a quiet kind of tribute.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The poem has not been without its detractors, and their objections are worth taking seriously. The main one is that it is too pretty, too escapist, and too easy.

For modern critics, especially those formed by modernism, which prized difficulty, irony, and the harsh facts of modern life, a poem like this can seem soft. It offers a fantasy of withdrawal at a time of enormous social change. Critics have pointed out that the idea of a simple cabin in an unspoiled landscape is a romantic fiction, and that real rural life in Ireland in the nineteenth century was marked by poverty, hunger, and emigration. The famine had devastated the country only a few decades earlier. A poem that sings of nine bean rows and a bee-loud glade without any trace of that history can look, from a certain angle, like a polite way of looking away.

A related complaint concerns the so-called Celtic Twilight style itself. Later readers, and later Irish writers in particular, sometimes found that movement sentimental, a misty, dreamy version of Ireland packaged for English and American tastes. Writers such as those who followed in the realist tradition wanted a harder, more honest picture. They saw in poems like this a romanticized Ireland that did not match the country they knew.

Yeats himself shared some of this skepticism. He grew embarrassed by the poem's popularity, and he is reported to have tired of being asked to recite it. He felt that it represented a phase of his writing that he had outgrown, and that it overshadowed his later and tougher achievements. The poet who would go on to write about civil war, aging, and the collapse of civilizations did not much want to be remembered for a bee-loud glade.

There are also plain stylistic objections. Some critics have found the archaic phrasing, "I will arise and go now," "of clay and wattles made," stagey and artificial, a costume rather than a voice. Others have pointed to the heavy reliance on pleasant adjectives, such as purple glow and linnet's wings, which can feel like postcard language.

It is fair to add a defense. The poem's final stanza complicates the escapism charge, because it makes the island a matter of longing rather than a realistic plan. Defenders say the poem is already aware of its own wishfulness, and that its subject is precisely the gap between dream and city pavement. Even so, the criticism that it is slight, and that it flatters a nostalgic picture of the countryside, has never entirely gone away.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This poem sits at the beginning of a major career and at the crossroads of two traditions. One is the Irish literary revival, which sought to make a native literature from native material. The other is the long tradition of pastoral poetry, which stretches from ancient Greek and Roman writers through the Renaissance and the Romantics, and which imagines the countryside as a place of peace set against the corruption of cities.

In the pastoral line, it descends from poems such as the classical praises of country life, and from the English Romantics, whose reverence for nature Yeats inherited. Wordsworth's poems about the countryside are an obvious ancestor, though Yeats is more dreamy and less moral. It also has a more American relation in Thoreau, whose experiment in living simply beside Walden Pond gave Yeats the basic image of the self-built cabin. A poem such as Robert Frost's work about rural New England shares some of the same instinct, though Frost is far drier and more skeptical.

Looking forward, the poem is a key early step in what became Irish poetry in English. Later Irish poets, including Patrick Kavanagh, wrote about the rural landscape from a more realistic angle, deliberately pushing back against the dreamy Revival style, and Seamus Heaney's poems about digging and bog and field can be read as a more earthy answer to Innisfree. The tradition from Yeats to Kavanagh to Heaney is a conversation, and this poem is one of its first lines.

Inside Yeats's own career, it is useful to read it next to his later work. Compare the peace of the island with the angry, public voice of Easter, 1916, or with the old man's hunger in Sailing to Byzantium, where the longing is no longer for a cabin but for eternity. Both later poems also want an ideal place, but they are far harder on illusion. Reading Innisfree first, you see where he began and how much further he went. The same longing is there, in an early form: the wish for a place where life is whole.

Why does it still matter to read? One reason is simply that it works, in under a minute, as a piece of music. A second is that it names an experience that is more common now than ever, the sense of living in a busy, grey world while an inner landscape calls. In an age of cities and screens, the dream of the bee-loud glade hasn't gone away.

And there is a final reason. The poem is a good reminder that homesickness and imagination are close relatives. Yeats could not go back to Sligo, so he built it in words. That is something poetry has always done, and he did it, at twenty-three, with unusual grace.
