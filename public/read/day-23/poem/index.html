The Lake Isle of Innisfree

W. B. Yeats (1890) • 3 min read

Standing on grey London pavement, a homesick young Irish poet hears lake water lapping in his deep heart’s core and vows to build a cabin of clay and wattles.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Composed in 1888 while a homesick twenty-three-year-old W. B. Yeats walked down Fleet Street in London and saw a tiny jet of water balancing a little tinkle of glass balls in a shop window, which suddenly awakened memories of Lough Gill in County Sligo and Henry David Thoreau’s Walden cabin. Belonging to the Celtic Twilight / Irish Revival tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' (1890) unfolds across 12 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. Published in the National Observer in December 1890 and collected in The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics (1892), it became Yeats’s most popular early lyric—recited by millions and printed in Irish passports—and the first poem in which Yeats felt he had discovered his own authentic musical rhythm.

I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,

And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made:

Nine bean rows will I have there, a hive for the honey bee,

And live alone in the bee-loud glade.

And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,

Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;

There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,

And evening full of the linnet’s wings.

I will arise and go now, for always night and day

I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;

While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey

I hear it in the deep heart’s core.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed in 1888 while a homesick twenty-three-year-old W. B. Yeats walked down Fleet Street in London and saw a tiny jet of water balancing a little tinkle of glass balls in a shop window, which suddenly awakened memories of Lough Gill in County Sligo and Henry David Thoreau’s Walden cabin. Within the broader trajectory of Celtic Twilight / Irish Revival, W. B. Yeats composed 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' (1890) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 12 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' lies the enduring tension between urban alienation ('the roadway, or on the pavements grey') and the restorative sanctuary of rural solitude, where nine bean-rows, a hive for the honey-bee, midnight’s glimmer, noon’s purple glow, and evening’s linnet wings allow peace to come 'dropping slow'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"I hear it in the deep heart’s core."—the central metaphor has transformed the speaker’s initial perception, leaving the reader with an earned.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' using three four-line ABAB quatrains combining loose hexameter/alexandrine lines with shortened four-stress fourth lines, enriched by biblical cadence ('I will arise and go now,' echoing the Prodigal Son in Luke 15:18), hypnotic repetition ('dropping slow, / Dropping from the veils of the morning'), and the low alliterative liquid consonants of 'lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore'. Spanning 12 lines across 3 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree," and "I hear it in the deep heart’s core."

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Published in the National Observer in December 1890 and collected in The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics (1892), it became Yeats’s most popular early lyric—recited by millions and printed in Irish passports—and the first poem in which Yeats felt he had discovered his own authentic musical rhythm. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
