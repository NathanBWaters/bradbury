Twin Peaks Radiolarian Chert and the Endangered Mission Blue Butterfly

San Francisco — How microscopic Jurassic glass-shelled plankton subducted from the equatorial Pacific formed San Francisco's 922-foot peaks—and sustained an iridescent butterfly rescued from extinction (Jurassic Deep-Ocean Time (100 Million Years Ago) to 1976 Endangered Species Act) • 8 min read

“Pick up a red stone on Twin Peaks, and you are holding the glass skeletons of a billion tropical sea creatures that died a hundred million years ago.” — Clyde Wahrhaftig, A Streetcar to Subduction (1984)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rising 922 feet at the geographic center of the San Francisco peninsula, the twin summits of Eureka Peak (North Peak) and Noe Peak (South Peak)—known to the Ohlone as sacred lookouts and to Spanish rancheros as 'Los Pechos de la Chola'—expose one of the most dramatic geological roadcuts in North America. Along Twin Peaks Boulevard, ribbon-like layers of brick-red Marin Headlands radiolarian chert reveal 100-million-year-old deep-ocean ooze composed of microscopic siliceous plankton skeletons scraped off the subducting Farallon Plate. Atop that thin, nutrient-poor chert soil grows the coastal prairie lupine that sustains the endangered Mission Blue butterfly (Icaricia icarioides missionensis).

Walk along the curved asphalt shoulder of Twin Peaks Boulevard just below the Christmas Tree Point overlook, and you will come face to face with a vertical cliff made of hundreds of tightly folded, two-inch-thick layers of brick-red stone alternating with paper-thin seams of dark red shale—looking like a giant ribbon candy or a stack of Persian carpets crumpled in a vise. For nearly a century after the California Gold Rush, geologists at the University of California and the U.S. Geological Survey were baffled by these red 'ribbon cherts' of the Franciscan Complex: how could hard, glassy quartz rock be stacked in thousands of uniform, rhythmic two-inch beds with no sand grains or pebbles anywhere inside them?

When geologists sliced wafer-thin sections of Twin Peaks red chert, dissolved the matrix in dilute hydrofluoric acid, and placed the residue under scanning electron microscopes in the 1960s and 1970s, they saw a breathtaking sight: millions of microscopic, lacy glass spheres, helmets, and spoked wheels belonging to single-celled marine protozoans called radiolarians. As legendary USGS and UC Berkeley geologist Clyde Wahrhaftig (1919–1994) explained in his classic field guide A Streetcar to Subduction, those radiolarians lived between 100 million and 170 million years ago during the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods, floating in the warm sunlit surface waters of the equatorial Pacific Ocean thousands of miles southwest of California.

Unlike clams or corals that build shells out of calcium carbonate (which dissolves completely in cold, high-pressure ocean depths below 14,000 feet), radiolarians secrete intricate skeletons out of opaline silica (SiO2 times nH2O); when the organisms died, their microscopic glass tests rained slowly down onto the abyssal volcanic basalt floor of the Farallon tectonic plate at the rate of one millimeter every thousand years. Why do the red chert layers on Twin Peaks alternate rhythmically between two inches of hard silica rock and a quarter-inch of red clay shale? Paleoclimatologists discovered that these couplets record orbital Milankovitch cycles—specifically the 21,000-year precession wobble of Earth's axis—which periodically strengthened equatorial ocean upwelling and triggered massive radiolarian blooms.

Furthermore, the rusty brick-red color of Twin Peaks chert comes from oxidized ferric iron (Fe2O3): because the ancient abyssal seafloor lay thousands of miles from any continental river mouth, oxygen-rich deep ocean bottom currents rusted the trace iron minerals in the clay as the siliceous ooze slowly compacted into solid stone. Roughly 80 to 100 million years ago, as the oceanic Farallon Plate slid eastward on a conveyor belt and plunged (subducted) beneath the western edge of the North American Plate, the upper layers of radiolarian chert, pillow basalt, and greywacke sandstone were scraped off like snow in front of a snowplow, crumpled into chevron folds, and uplifted nine hundred feet to form Twin Peaks, Mount Davidson, and the Marin Headlands.

Because weathered radiolarian chert and pillow basalt break down into thin, rocky, fast-draining soils that dry out rapidly between winter rains and are raked all summer by 30-knot fog winds blowing through the Golden Gate, tall forests never naturally grew on the upper slopes of Twin Peaks—creating instead a rare coastal prairie grassland. Thriving in those wind-scoured chert gravels are three hardy species of native perennial wildflowers: silver lupine (Lupinus albifrons), summer lupine (Lupinus formosus), and varied-leaved lupine (Lupinus variicolor)—which together serve as the sole larval host plants on Earth for the iridescent sky-blue Mission Blue butterfly (Icaricia icarioides missionensis).

First scientifically described in 1937 by entomologist William Hovanitz from type specimens collected specifically on the slopes of Twin Peaks in the Mission District watershed, the Mission Blue butterfly possesses a wingspan barely the size of a quarter, with males flashing brilliant cobalt-violet wings edged in black and silvery-gray undersides speckled with black eyespots. The life cycle of the Mission Blue on Twin Peaks is a marvel of evolutionary co-dependence: in April and May, adult butterflies live only seven to ten days, mating and laying single pinhead-sized white eggs on young lupine leaves; after hatching and feeding for three weeks, the tiny green caterpillars drop into the dry leaf litter and enter diapause (dormancy) for nine months across summer, fall, and winter.

When the caterpillars wake the following spring and resume feeding on new lupine shoots, they secrete a sugary, amino-acid-rich honeydew drop from a dorsal gland on their backs—attracting native ants that stand guard over the caterpillars and drive off parasitic wasps and predators in a classic mutualism known as myrmecophily. By the early 1970s, after urban housing tracts, quarries, paved roads, and invasive French broom and eucalyptus choked out ninety-eight percent of San Francisco's coastal lupine grasslands, lepidopterist Dr. Robert Langston and conservationists sounded the alarm, leading the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service on June 1, 1976, to list the Mission Blue among the very first insects protected under the federal Endangered Species Act.

Notice a subtle biographical connection when you walk the Twin Peaks chert roadcuts: Clyde Wahrhaftig—the brilliant USGS geologist who mapped San Francisco's Franciscan subduction complex and won the Geological Society of America's Distinguished Career Award in 1989—used his acceptance speech to come out publicly as a gay scientist, inspiring a generation of LGBTQ+ earth scientists in the city below. In 2009–2019, the San Francisco Recreation and Park Department, the Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy, and the Presidio Trust launched a landmark translocation program—removing invasive weeds on Twin Peaks, planting thousands of native silver lupines in the red chert gravel, and reintroducing captive-monitored Mission Blue females from San Bruno Mountain so that a 100-million-year-old ocean floor once again shimmers with living blue wings above the city.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Subduction Accretion: How Equatorial Plankton Built a 922-Foot Peak

Twin Peaks is not a volcano; it is an uplifted tectonic scrap of the Jurassic deep seafloor. For seventy million years, microscopic silica-shelled radiolarians rained onto the abyssal Farallon Plate near the equator. As plate tectonics carried that ocean crust 2,500 miles northeast and subducted it beneath California, the lightweight siliceous chert beds were shaved off into an accretionary prism, folded like accordion pleats under immense pressure, and thrust skyward.

2. The Lupine-Butterfly-Ant Triangle on Chert Grasslands

Why did the Mission Blue butterfly (Icaricia icarioides missionensis) nearly go extinct when invasive grasses and trees covered San Francisco's hills? The butterfly depends on a fragile ecological triad: its caterpillars can digest only the alkaloid-rich leaves of three native lupine species (Lupinus albifrons, L. formosus, and L. variicolor) that colonize disturbed, nutrient-poor chert slopes, while native tending ants protect the caterpillars from parasitoid wasps in exchange for sugary honeydew secretions.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three scientific landmarks on the hill: first, Marin Headlands Terrane Radiolarian Chert, the folded brick-red ribbon rock exposed along Twin Peaks Boulevard; second, geologist Clyde Wahrhaftig (1919–1994), author of A Streetcar to Subduction, who decoded San Francisco's bedrock using Muni transit lines; and third, the June 1, 1976 Endangered Species Act listing that made the Mission Blue butterfly an icon of urban biodiversity conservation.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take Muni bus 37-Corbett or walk up from Portola Drive along the car-free eastern promenade of Twin Peaks Boulevard to the summit saddle. Examine the dramatic chevron folds of red radiolarian chert in the roadcut cliff just below the northern viewing plaza, look for the silvery-gray foliage and purple-blue spikes of native Lupinus albifrons on the fenced restoration slopes in April and May, and climb the wooden steps to the 922-foot crest of Eureka Peak.
