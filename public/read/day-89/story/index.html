The Lightning-Rod Man

Herman Melville (1854) • 13 min read

“You are wet. Stand here on the hearth before the fire.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Herman Melville published The Lightning-Rod Man in 1854, during one of the hardest stretches of his life. Moby-Dick had sold poorly, his next novel had been roundly attacked, and he was now writing short pieces for magazines to earn a living, many of them stranger and sharper than editors expected. The story is a comic duel in a farmhouse during a thunderstorm. A traveling salesman arrives with a copper rod and a talent for scaring people, and he tries to sell protection against lightning to a calm homeowner who refuses. It was later gathered in Melville's collection The Piazza Tales. On the surface it is a funny, crackling little sketch. Underneath, it is about fear, faith, and the people who make money by selling certainty, which makes it feel surprisingly modern. Keep an ear out for how the salesman talks.

What grand irregular thunder, thought I, standing on my hearthstone among the Acroceraunian hills, as the scattered bolts boomed overhead, and crashed down among the valleys, every bolt followed by zigzag irradiations, and swift slants of sharp rain, which audibly rang, like a charge of spear-points, on my low shingled roof. I suppose, though, that the mountains hereabouts break and churn up the thunder, so that it is far more glorious here than on the plain. Hark!⁠—someone at the door. Who is this that chooses a time of thunder for making calls? And why don’t he, man-fashion, use the knocker, instead of making that doleful undertaker’s clatter with his fist against the hollow panel? But let him in. Ah, here he comes. “Good day, sir:” an entire stranger. “Pray be seated.” What is that strange-looking walking-stick he carries: “A fine thunderstorm, sir.”

“Fine?⁠—Awful!”

“You are wet. Stand here on the hearth before the fire.”

“Not for worlds!”

The stranger still stood in the exact middle of the cottage, where he had first planted himself. His singularity impelled a closer scrutiny. A lean, gloomy figure. Hair dark and lank, mattedly streaked over his brow. His sunken pitfalls of eyes were ringed by indigo halos, and played with an innocuous sort of lightning: the gleam without the bolt. The whole man was dripping. He stood in a puddle on the bare oak floor: his strange walking-stick vertically resting at his side.

It was a polished copper rod, four feet long, lengthwise attached to a neat wooden staff, by insertion into two balls of greenish glass, ringed with copper bands. The metal rod terminated at the top tripodwise, in three keen tines, brightly gilt. He held the thing by the wooden part alone.

“Sir,” said I, bowing politely, “have I the honor of a visit from that illustrious god, Jupiter Tonans? So stood he in the Greek statue of old, grasping the lightning-bolt. If you be he, or his viceroy, I have to thank you for this noble storm you have brewed among our mountains. Listen: That was a glorious peal. Ah, to a lover of the majestic, it is a good thing to have the Thunderer himself in one’s cottage. The thunder grows finer for that. But pray be seated. This old rush-bottomed armchair, I grant, is a poor substitute for your evergreen throne on Olympus; but, condescend to be seated.”

While I thus pleasantly spoke, the stranger eyed me, half in wonder, and half in a strange sort of horror; but did not move a foot.

“Do, sir, be seated; you need to be dried ere going forth again.”

I planted the chair invitingly on the broad hearth, where a little fire had been kindled that afternoon to dissipate the dampness, not the cold; for it was early in the month of September.

But without heeding my solicitation, and still standing in the middle of the floor, the stranger gazed at me portentously and spoke.

“Sir,” said he, “excuse me; but instead of my accepting your invitation to be seated on the hearth there, I solemnly warn you, that you had best accept mine, and stand with me in the middle of the room. Good heavens!” he cried, starting⁠—“there is another of those awful crashes. I warn you, sir, quit the hearth.”

“Mr. Jupiter Tonans,” said I, quietly rolling my body on the stone, “I stand very well here.”

“Are you so horridly ignorant, then,” he cried, “as not to know, that by far the most dangerous part of a house, during such a terrific tempest as this, is the fireplace?”

“Nay, I did not know that,” involuntarily stepping upon the first board next to the stone.

The stranger now assumed such an unpleasant air of successful admonition, that⁠—quite involuntarily again⁠—I stepped back upon the hearth, and threw myself into the erectest, proudest posture I could command. But I said nothing.

“For Heaven’s sake,” he cried, with a strange mixture of alarm and intimidation⁠—“for Heaven’s sake, get off the hearth! Know you not, that the heated air and soot are conductors;⁠—to say nothing of those immense iron firedogs? Quit the spot⁠—I conjure⁠—I command you.”

“Mr. Jupiter Tonans, I am not accustomed to be commanded in my own house.”

“Call me not by that pagan name. You are profane in this time of terror.”

“Sir, will you be so good as to tell me your business? If you seek shelter from the storm, you are welcome, so long as you be civil; but if you come on business, open it forthwith. Who are you?”

“I am a dealer in lightning-rods,” said the stranger, softening his tone; “my special business is⁠—Merciful heaven! what a crash!⁠—Have you ever been struck⁠—your premises, I mean? No? It’s best to be provided;”⁠—significantly rattling his metallic staff on the floor;⁠—“by nature, there are no castles in thunderstorms; yet, say but the word, and of this cottage I can make a Gibraltar by a few waves of this wand. Hark, what Himalayas of concussions!”

“You interrupted yourself; your special business you were about to speak of.”

“My special business is to travel the country for orders for lightning-rods. This is my specimen-rod;” tapping his staff; “I have the best of references”⁠—fumbling in his pockets. “In Criggan last month, I put up three-and-twenty rods on only five buildings.”

“Let me see. Was it not at Criggan last week, about midnight on Saturday, that the steeple, the big elm, and the assembly-room cupola were struck? Any of your rods there?”

“Not on the tree and cupola, but the steeple.”

“Of what use is your rod, then?”

“Of life-and-death use. But my workman was heedless. In fitting the rod at top to the steeple, he allowed a part of the metal to graze the tin sheeting. Hence the accident. Not my fault, but his. Hark!”

“Never mind. That clap burst quite loud enough to be heard without finger-pointing. Did you hear of the event at Montreal last year? A servant girl struck at her bedside with a rosary in her hand; the beads being metal. Does your beat extend into the Canadas?”

“No. And I hear that there, iron rods only are in use. They should have mine, which are copper. Iron is easily fused. Then they draw out the rod so slender, that it has not body enough to conduct the full electric current. The metal melts; the building is destroyed. My copper rods never act so. Those Canadians are fools. Some of them knob the rod at the top, which risks a deadly explosion, instead of imperceptibly carrying down the current into the earth, as this sort of rod does. Mine is the only true rod. Look at it. Only one dollar a foot.”

“This abuse of your own calling in another might make one distrustful with respect to yourself.”

“Hark! The thunder becomes less muttering. It is nearing us, and nearing the earth, too. Hark! One crammed crash! All the vibrations made one by nearness. Another flash. Hold!”

“What do you?” I said, seeing him now, instantaneously relinquishing his staff, lean intently forward towards the window, with his right fore and middle fingers on his left wrist. But ere the words had well escaped me, another exclamation escaped him.

“Crash! only three pulses⁠—less than a third of a mile off⁠—yonder, somewhere in that wood. I passed three stricken oaks there, ripped out new and glittering. The oak draws lightning more than other timber, having iron in solution in its sap. Your floor here seems oak.”

“Heart-of-oak. From the peculiar time of your call upon me, I suppose you purposely select stormy weather for your journeys. When the thunder is roaring, you deem it an hour peculiarly favorable for producing impressions favorable to your trade.”

“Hark!⁠—Awful!”

“For one who would arm others with fear you seem unbeseemingly timorous yourself. Common men choose fair weather for their travels: you choose thunderstorms; and yet⁠—”

“That I travel in thunderstorms, I grant; but not without particular precautions, such as only a lightning-rod man may know. Hark! Quick⁠—look at my specimen rod. Only one dollar a foot.”

“A very fine rod, I dare say. But what are these particular precautions of yours? Yet first let me close yonder shutters; the slanting rain is beating through the sash. I will bar up.”

“Are you mad? Know you not that yon iron bar is a swift conductor? Desist.”

“I will simply close the shutters, then, and call my boy to bring me a wooden bar. Pray, touch the bellpull there.”

“Are you frantic? That bell-wire might blast you. Never touch bell-wire in a thunderstorm, nor ring a bell of any sort.”

“Nor those in belfries? Pray, will you tell me where and how one may be safe in a time like this? Is there any part of my house I may touch with hopes of my life?”

“There is; but not where you now stand. Come away from the wall. The current will sometimes run down a wall, and⁠—a man being a better conductor than a wall⁠—it would leave the wall and run into him. Swoop! That must have fallen very nigh. That must have been globular lightning.”

“Very probably. Tell me at once, which is, in your opinion, the safest part of this house?”

“This room, and this one spot in it where I stand. Come hither.”

“The reasons first.”

“Hark!⁠—after the flash the gust⁠—the sashes shiver⁠—the house, the house!⁠—Come hither to me!”

“The reasons, if you please.”

“Come hither to me!”

“Thank you again, I think I will try my old stand⁠—the hearth. And now, Mr. Lightning-rod-man, in the pauses of the thunder, be so good as to tell me your reasons for esteeming this one room of the house the safest, and your own one standpoint there the safest spot in it.”

There was now a little cessation of the storm for a while. The Lightning-rod man seemed relieved, and replied:⁠—

“Your house is a one-storied house, with an attic and a cellar; this room is between. Hence its comparative safety. Because lightning sometimes passes from the clouds to the earth, and sometimes from the earth to the clouds. Do you comprehend?⁠—and I choose the middle of the room, because if the lightning should strike the house at all, it would come down the chimney or walls; so, obviously, the further you are from them, the better. Come hither to me, now.”

“Presently. Something you just said, instead of alarming me, has strangely inspired confidence.”

“What have I said?”

“You said that sometimes lightning flashes from the earth to the clouds.”

“Aye, the returning-stroke, as it is called; when the earth, being overcharged with the fluid, flashes its surplus upward.”

“The returning-stroke; that is, from earth to sky. Better and better. But come here on the hearth and dry yourself.”

“I am better here, and better wet.”

“How?”

“It is the safest thing you can do⁠—Hark, again!⁠—to get yourself thoroughly drenched in a thunderstorm. Wet clothes are better conductors than the body; and so, if the lightning strike, it might pass down the wet clothes without touching the body. The storm deepens again. Have you a rug in the house? Rugs are nonconductors. Get one, that I may stand on it here, and you, too. The skies blacken⁠—it is dusk at noon. Hark!⁠—the rug, the rug!”

I gave him one; while the hooded mountains seemed closing and tumbling into the cottage.

“And now, since our being dumb will not help us,” said I, resuming my place, “let me hear your precautions in traveling during thunderstorms.”

“Wait till this one is passed.”

“Nay, proceed with the precautions. You stand in the safest possible place according to your own account. Go on.”

“Briefly, then. I avoid pine-trees, high houses, lonely barns, upland pastures, running water, flocks of cattle and sheep, a crowd of men. If I travel on foot⁠—as today⁠—I do not walk fast; if in my buggy, I touch not its back or sides; if on horseback, I dismount and lead the horse. But of all things, I avoid tall men.”

“Do I dream? Man avoid man? and in danger-time, too.”

“Tall men in a thunderstorm I avoid. Are you so grossly ignorant as not to know, that the height of a six-footer is sufficient to discharge an electric cloud upon him? Are not lonely Kentuckians, ploughing, smit in the unfinished furrow? Nay, if the six-footer stand by running water, the cloud will sometimes select him as its conductor to that running water. Hark! Sure, yon black pinnacle is split. Yes, a man is a good conductor. The lightning goes through and through a man, but only peels a tree. But sir, you have kept me so long answering your questions, that I have not yet come to business. Will you order one of my rods? Look at this specimen one? See: it is of the best of copper. Copper’s the best conductor. Your house is low; but being upon the mountains, that lowness does not one whit depress it. You mountaineers are most exposed. In mountainous countries the lightning-rod man should have most business. Look at the specimen, sir. One rod will answer for a house so small as this. Look over these recommendations. Only one rod, sir; cost, only twenty dollars. Hark! There go all the granite Taconics and Hoosics dashed together like pebbles. By the sound, that must have struck something. An elevation of five feet above the house, will protect twenty feet radius all about the rod. Only twenty dollars, sir⁠—a dollar a foot. Hark!⁠—Dreadful!⁠—Will you order? Will you buy? Shall I put down your name? Think of being a heap of charred offal, like a haltered horse burnt in his stall; and all in one flash!”

“You pretended envoy extraordinary and minister plenipotentiary to and from Jupiter Tonans,” laughed I; “you mere man who come here to put you and your pipestem between clay and sky, do you think that because you can strike a bit of green light from the Leyden jar, that you can thoroughly avert the supernal bolt? Your rod rusts, or breaks, and where are you? Who has empowered you, you Tetzel, to peddle round your indulgences from divine ordinations? The hairs of our heads are numbered, and the days of our lives. In thunder as in sunshine, I stand at ease in the hands of my God. False negotiator, away! See, the scroll of the storm is rolled back; the house is unharmed; and in the blue heavens I read in the rainbow, that the Deity will not, of purpose, make war on man’s earth.”

“Impious wretch!” foamed the stranger, blackening in the face as the rainbow beamed, “I will publish your infidel notions.”

The scowl grew blacker on his face; the indigo-circles enlarged round his eyes as the storm-rings round the midnight moon. He sprang upon me; his tri-forked thing at my heart.

I seized it; I snapped it; I dashed it; I trod it; and dragging the dark lightning-king out of my door, flung his elbowed, copper sceptre after him.

But spite of my treatment, and spite of my dissuasive talk of him to my neighbors, the Lightning-rod man still dwells in the land; still travels in storm-time, and drives a brave trade with the fears of man.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The setup is simple. A man is at home in a cottage among the mountains while a fierce thunderstorm rolls overhead. He is enjoying it, even calling the thunder grand. A stranger knocks, drips on the floor, and refuses to stand near the fire or sit in a chair. He carries a strange walking stick, a copper rod tipped with three gilded points, held by a wooden handle and two glass balls. He is a lightning-rod salesman, and over the next few minutes he tries to terrify the narrator into buying.

The comedy lies in the reversal of roles. The salesman acts as though he were a priest of danger, issuing warnings and commands. Don't stand by the fireplace, he says, because the soot and the iron firedogs are conductors. Do as I do, in the exact middle of the room, with my rod. The narrator's refusal to obey is partly teasing, as when he calls him "Mr. Jupiter Tonans," after the Roman god of thunder, and partly principle.

Beneath the banter is a clash of two attitudes toward danger. The salesman believes that fear is the proper response to the world and that human skill can buy safety. The narrator believes that trusting God, and accepting that we are not in control, is the only reasonable way to live. When the storm passes and a rainbow appears, the narrator reads it as a sign that God will not make war on man's earth, a plain echo of the story of Noah. The salesman, blackening in the face, calls him an impious wretch and attacks him with his rod. The narrator snaps it and throws it out the door.

There is also a sharper theme about commerce and fear. The salesman makes his living from other people's terror. He boasts about putting up twenty-three rods on five buildings in a single town, and when the narrator points out that a nearby steeple, elm, and cupola were struck anyway, he blames a heedless workman. The product, in other words, cannot fail; only people can. This is the logic of every scheme that sells reassurance, and Melville notices that such a salesman needs the storm to continue.

The narrator compares him to Tetzel, the sixteenth-century friar who sold indulgences, paper pardons that promised to reduce time in purgatory in return for payment. That comparison is the key to the story's moral heart. The lightning-rod man is selling an indulgence against nature, a purchased exemption from the risks of being alive. The last line is a small shock of realism: despite everything, the salesman still travels the land and still drives a brave trade with the fears of man.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Lightning-Rod Man is one of Melville's most compact and playful pieces, and its best quality is energy. The prose crackles. The opening paragraph, with its thunder booming among the hills and rain ringing on the roof like a charge of spear-points, is as vivid as any storm in his sea writing. The dialogue is quick and theatrical, almost like a two-man stage play, with the narrator's mock-grand speeches pitched against the salesman's breathless, interrupted warnings.

The structure also works well. Melville lets the salesman reveal himself through repetition, always pausing in mid-sentence as a crash of thunder gives him another reason to be afraid. This produces a comic rhythm that grows: Hark! Dreadful! Will you order? Each interruption makes his pitch both funnier and more sinister. The way he moves from advice to command to threat is a small masterclass in how fear-based selling escalates.

But the story has problems. The narrator, though charming, is a bit too pleased with himself. His speeches, full of classical allusions and grand theology, can feel like a man showing off, and Melville seems at times to enjoy the performance more than he questions it. Readers sometimes find the narrator smug, especially in the final confrontation, where he breaks the rod with a flurry of verbs that is almost gleefully violent. If the story is meant to expose the salesman as a bully, the hero's own physical triumph complicates that.

There is also an unevenness of tone. The story is a comedy, but it contains a real threat of death in the storm, and the salesman's final lunge, with his trident aimed at the narrator's heart, tilts for a moment into melodrama. Some readers feel the mixture of farce, allegory, and religious argument is not fully digested, and that the allegory can seem a little too obvious, like a sermon wearing a costume.

The theology is also left rather thin. The narrator's faith, that he stands at ease in the hands of God, is stated more than tested. We never see him in real danger, and so his confidence costs him nothing. A harder story might have let the storm strike something. Melville, who elsewhere is fascinated by the ways the universe can be indifferent or cruel, makes this a surprisingly comfortable piece.

Still, as a miniature, it is strong. It is quick, witty, and memorably staged, with a central image, the copper rod held out like a sceptre, that is both comic and slightly menacing. It does not aspire to the depth of Bartleby or Benito Cereno, and it does not need to. It succeeds in what it sets out to do: to make us laugh at fear and then notice how much of our lives it governs.

III. The Impact It Had

The story appeared in Putnam's Monthly Magazine in 1854, and then in the 1856 collection The Piazza Tales, which also contained Bartleby, the Scrivener, Benito Cereno, and The Encantadas. It was never a sensation. Melville's reputation had suffered badly by this time, and his short fiction of this period was read quietly and mostly forgotten. Its impact in its own day was modest, and it would be dishonest to claim otherwise.

Within Melville's career, it belongs to the productive but frustrating years when he turned from big novels to magazine stories. He wrote for money, but he did not write down to his readers. These stories are full of disguised criticism of American complacency, commerce, and certainty. Placing it among them shows how Melville used short, entertaining forms to carry serious ideas, and it reveals his persistent suspicion of anyone who claims to have mastered nature or the divine.

After his death in 1891, Melville was nearly forgotten, until a revival in the 1920s brought Moby-Dick back to life and drew attention to the shorter works. The Lightning-Rod Man has since become a regular in anthologies and classrooms. Because it is brief, funny, and clear in its structure, it is a favorite for teaching satire, allegory, and the unreliable or opinionated narrator. Teachers use it for discussion of faith versus science, of fear and advertising, and of Melville's allusions, which range from Greek myth to the Protestant Reformation.

The story has also been read as an early portrait of the American salesman, a figure that later literature would develop into a major type. Arthur Miller's Willy Loman and the fast talkers of American drama and fiction belong to a much larger tradition, and Melville's lightning-rod man is one of the early notable examples of a pitchman who sells anxiety. Critics have noted a link to the long American suspicion of peddlers and quacks, which stretches from Mark Twain's confidence men to modern advertising satire.

Its influence is hard to trace in direct lines, but it has become a handy cultural reference for the idea of selling fear. Whenever someone is pushed to buy protection against a threat that may or may not be real, the story is waiting there as a precedent. It also anticipates the modern habit of treating scientific or technological tools as moral substitutes for humility, a theme that echoes widely in American writing.

IV. What Readers Loved

One thing readers consistently enjoy in The Lightning-Rod Man is the language. Melville is at his most exuberant here, piling up images of thunder as if he were conducting an orchestra. The grand, comical way the narrator addresses the salesman, treating him as a visiting god, gives the story a mock-heroic glow. Even people who find Melville's long novels hard going often find this story an easy and enjoyable entrance to his style.

Readers also love the sense of drama. The entire story takes place in one room, with two men, a fire, a puddle on the floor, and the sound of thunder. It has the compact tension of a one-act play. The salesman's bizarre posture, planted in the exact middle of the floor, dripping, with his rod held upright, is a vivid and funny image, and the way he flinches at every thunderclap while the narrator lounges on the hearth makes the contrast between the two men visual as well as verbal.

Another source of delight is how the story turns the tables on a familiar kind of person. Almost everyone has met someone who tries to scare them into a purchase, whether it is an insurance salesman, a repairman with a grim diagnosis, or an advertiser with a frightening statistic. Readers see their own experiences in the salesman, and they enjoy the narrator's refusal to be bullied. The final image of the rod snapped and flung out the door is satisfying, a small moment of triumph over every pitchman who ever leaned on someone's fear.

There is, too, the religious and philosophical depth that lies under the comedy. Many readers love the line about standing at ease in the hands of God in thunder as in sunshine, and the image of the rainbow as a promise that the Deity will not make war on the earth. Whether or not one shares the narrator's faith, the sense of calm acceptance that he expresses has a real appeal, particularly in a story that is otherwise so full of noise.

Finally, readers often appreciate the story's last turn. Melville refuses to give us a neat victory. The salesman, despite being driven off, still roams the countryside. This dose of realism gives the story a longer shadow than the broken rod would suggest. It reminds us that fear is a renewable resource, and that no single refusal puts the pitch out of business.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critical reaction to The Lightning-Rod Man has been mild, in part because the story has received less attention than Melville's major works. In his own day, reviewers of The Piazza Tales were generally positive but focused on other stories, and there is little recorded commentary on this one specifically. The most honest thing to say is that the story's reception has been quiet, and that most of the debate about it has come from later readers rather than contemporaries.

The central argument among those later readers is about the narrator. Is he a wise, faithful man who rightly refuses to buy fear, or an arrogant fool who tempts providence? Some critics read the story as a straightforward endorsement of trust in God. Others see Melville's irony at work, noting that the narrator is vain, loud, and insulting, and that his dismissal of lightning rods ignores real science. Benjamin Franklin's invention did, after all, save buildings and lives, and a story that mocks it risks seeming anti-scientific. Melville's other writings show deep ambivalence about both orthodox faith and technological confidence, so it is hard to be sure whose side he is on.

That leads to a related objection. If the salesman's rods work, the narrator's refusal could be dangerous, and critics sometimes point out that the story proves nothing. The storm passes, but a storm passing is not evidence of divine protection. A calm reader may feel the narrator was merely lucky. By not letting the lightning strike, Melville avoids testing his hero's claim, which can seem a cheat.

Others have complained that the story is slight, a witty sketch rather than a fully realized work. Compared with the dark ambiguity of Bartleby or the moral horror of Benito Cereno, it can feel like a bit of fun. The allegory is also quite transparent, with a figure of Fear arguing against a figure of Faith, and some readers find that too neat for a writer of Melville's complexity.

Finally, there is a minor objection about tone, with some readers finding the narrator's constant mockery of the salesman unkind. The man may well be as frightened as he sounds. Melville gives him no moment of sympathy, and the story's hard laughter at his expense feels, to some, a little too sure of its own righteousness.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Lightning-Rod Man belongs to the American tradition of the comic sketch and the tall tale, but with a philosophical edge. It sits in the company of stories by Hawthorne and Poe, with whom Melville shared a taste for allegory, and in a line that includes Twain's satires of con men and quacks. It also fits into a broader nineteenth-century debate between Christian faith and the rising confidence in science and progress, which runs through American writing from Emerson to Hawthorne.

Its ancestors include the old European satires on pardoners and peddlers. Chaucer's Pardoner, who sells fake relics and preaches against greed for profit, is a distant cousin of the lightning-rod man. The reference to Tetzel makes that lineage explicit. Melville transposes the medieval swindler into a modern, American setting, where the relic is a piece of copper and the miracle is electrical.

Looking forward, the story anticipates the American salesman as a literary type. Characters who sell dreams or fears appear throughout later literature, from Twain's King and Duke in Huckleberry Finn to Melville's own The Confidence-Man, a novel he would publish in 1857 that is essentially a book-length study of deception in a world of salesmen. In that sense, this short story is a rehearsal for the larger and darker work. In the twentieth century, writers such as Sinclair Lewis, Arthur Miller, and Flannery O'Connor would explore related territory.

Compared with Melville's other short fiction, it is the lightest. Bartleby is tragic and baffling, and Benito Cereno is a slow nightmare of misreading. The Lightning-Rod Man is closer to a comic duet, but it shares the same suspicion of confident people who claim to understand the world. In each story, someone who seems sure of himself is shown to be mistaken, or dangerous, or both.

Why does it still matter to read? Because we live in an age of products that promise safety from every threat, and of media that profit from alarm. The story asks a simple question that remains hard to answer: where does prudence end and manufactured fear begin? It does not give a final answer, and the narrator is perhaps too sure of himself. But as a short, sharp, funny reminder that someone is always ready to sell you protection against the sky, it is as fresh as ever.
