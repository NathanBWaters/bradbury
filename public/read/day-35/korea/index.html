Jang Yeong-sil's Jagyeokru (1434): How a Former Palace Slave Built the World's Finest Automated Water Clock

Korea — Elevated by King Sejong over aristocratic outrage, a Dongnae metalworker engineered a bronze-ball robotics computer that struck the hours without human hands (Joseon Dynasty / Reign of King Sejong (1434–1536)) • 20 min read

Key Context & Why It Matters

On July 1, 1434 (the sixteenth year of King Sejong), inside the newly built three-bay Borugak Pavilion south of Gyeonghoeru Pond in Gyeongbokgung Palace, a three-story mechanical marvel began keeping the official standard time of the Joseon Kingdom without a single human watchman touching a mallet: the Jagyeokru ('Self-Striking Water Clock'). Engineered by Jang Yeong-sil—born in Dongnae (Busan) as a lowly government slave (*gwanno*) whose extraordinary mechanical genius led King Sejong to emancipate him and appoint him Senior Third Rank Palace Guard Commander over aristocratic protests—the Jagyeokru used constant-head bronze and ceramic water vessels and a binary cascade of thirty-seven bronze balls to trigger twelve wooden mannequins striking bells, drums, and gongs automatically.

1. Fluid Dynamics & Digital Logic: Constant-Head Siphons and Bronze-Ball Relays

In Professor Nam Moon-hyon's thirty-year engineering reconstruction of the 1434 Jagyeokru, two physical breakthroughs stood out.
