Rhapsody on a Windy Night

T. S. Eliot (1915) • 6 min read

Walking home between midnight and four in the morning, a solitary flâneur listens to sputtering street-lamps that beat like a fatalistic drum and shake involuntary memories from the dark.

Twelve o’clock.

Along the reaches of the street

Held in a lunar synthesis,

Whispering lunar incantations

Disolve the floors of memory

And all its clear relations,

Its divisions and precisions,

Every street lamp that I pass

Beats like a fatalistic drum,

And through the spaces of the dark

Midnight shakes the memory

As a madman shakes a dead geranium.

Half-past one,

The street lamp sputtered,

The street lamp muttered,

The street lamp said,

“Regard that woman

Who hesitates toward you in the light of the door

Which opens on her like a grin.

You see the border of her dress

Is torn and stained with sand,

And you see the corner of her eye

Twists like a crooked pin.”

The memory throws up high and dry

A crowd of twisted things;

A twisted branch upon the beach

Eaten smooth, and polished

As if the world gave up

The secret of its skeleton,

Stiff and white.

A broken spring in a factory yard,

Rust that clings to the form that the strength has left

Hard and curled and ready to snap.

Half-past two,

The street-lamp said,

“Remark the cat which flattens itself in the gutter,

Slips out its tongue

And devours a morsel of rancid butter.”

So the hand of the child, automatic,

Slipped out and pocketed a toy that was running along the quay.

I could see nothing behind that child’s eye.

I have seen eyes in the street

Trying to peer through lighted shutters,

And a crab one afternoon in a pool,

An old crab with barnacles on his back,

Gripped the end of a stick which I held him.

Half-past three,

The lamp sputtered,

The lamp muttered in the dark.

The lamp hummed:

“Regard the moon,

La lune ne garde aucune rancune,

She winks a feeble eye,

She smiles into corners.

She smooths the hair of the grass.

The moon has lost her memory.

A washed-out smallpox cracks her face,

Her hand twists a paper rose,

That smells of dust and old Cologne,

She is alone

With all the old nocturnal smells

That cross and cross across her brain.

The reminiscence comes

Of sunless dry geraniums

And dust in crevices,

Smells of chestnuts in the streets

And female smells in shuttered rooms

And cigarettes in corridors

And cocktail smells in bars.”

The lamp said,

“Four o’clock,

Here is the number on the door.

Memory!

You have the key,

The little lamp spreads a ring on the stair,

Mount.

The bed is open; the toothbrush hangs on the wall,

Put your shoes at the door, sleep, prepare for life.”

The last twist of the knife.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in Paris in 1911 while Eliot was attending the lectures of philosopher Henri Bergson at the Collège de France and published alongside 'Preludes' in Blast (July 1915), the poem dramatizes Bergson’s distinction between mechanical clock time and involuntary associative memory. Within the broader trajectory of High Modernism, T. S. Eliot composed 'Rhapsody on a Windy Night' (1915) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Twelve o’clock."—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, T. S. Eliot grounded these 79 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Rhapsody on a Windy Night' lies the nocturnal collision between mechanical urban time and the surreal, fragmented detritus of memory: as the walker passes sputtering street-lamps at twelve, half-past one, half-past two, half-past three, and four o’clock, memory throws up 'twisted things'—a crooked pin, a sun-bleached crab on a beach, a child’s automatic hand, and a feeble moon with a washed-out smallpox cracks—culminating in 'The last twist of the knife'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Slipped out and pocketed a toy that was running along the quay."—T. S. Eliot develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, T. S. Eliot constructs 'Rhapsody on a Windy Night' using seventy-nine lines of irregular free verse punctuated by timestamp markers ('Twelve o’clock,' 'Half-past one,' 'Four o’clock') and the hypnotic personification of each street-lamp muttering and humming like a hypnotic guide before the chilling domestic banality of toothbrush, bed, and shoes at the door. Spanning 79 lines across 8 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how T. S. Eliot balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Twelve o’clock." and "The last twist of the knife." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

A cornerstone of Prufrock and Other Observations (1917), the poem anticipates both Surrealism and film noir, demonstrating how modern urban landscapes externalize psychological isolation. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Rhapsody on a Windy Night' has remained a touchstone of the Modernist & Imagist canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to T. S. Eliot’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form. From nineteenth- and twentieth-century critical debates to contemporary anthologies, T. S. Eliot’s 'Rhapsody on a Windy Night' retains all of its original freshness and authority.
