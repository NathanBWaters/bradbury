Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

Rhapsody on a Windy Night

T. S. Eliot (1915) • 6 min read

Walking home between midnight and four in the morning, a solitary flâneur listens to sputtering street-lamps that beat like a fatalistic drum and shake involuntary memories from the dark.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rhapsody on a Windy Night is T. S. Eliot's strange, hypnotic walk through a city after midnight, and it shows how much a young poet could do with a street lamp. Eliot wrote it in Paris around 1911, when he was about twenty-three and attending lectures by the philosopher Henri Bergson, who argued that human time is not the tick of a clock but a flowing, private experience in which memory keeps surging up unbidden. The poem was first published in July 1915 in Blast, a radical magazine of the Vorticist avant-garde, and then collected in Prufrock and Other Observations in 1917. It marches hour by hour from midnight to four in the morning while a talking lamp points out sights, a woman in a doorway, a cat, a child, the moon. Memory, in this poem, is not comforting. It is a madman shaking a dead plant. The result is a nightmarish, beautifully strange portrait of a mind coming apart in a lit-up city.

Twelve o’clock.

Along the reaches of the street

Held in a lunar synthesis,

Whispering lunar incantations

Disolve the floors of memory

And all its clear relations,

Its divisions and precisions,

Every street lamp that I pass

Beats like a fatalistic drum,

And through the spaces of the dark

Midnight shakes the memory

As a madman shakes a dead geranium.

Half-past one,

The street lamp sputtered,

The street lamp muttered,

The street lamp said,

“Regard that woman

Who hesitates toward you in the light of the door

Which opens on her like a grin.

You see the border of her dress

Is torn and stained with sand,

And you see the corner of her eye

Twists like a crooked pin.”

The memory throws up high and dry

A crowd of twisted things;

A twisted branch upon the beach

Eaten smooth, and polished

As if the world gave up

The secret of its skeleton,

Stiff and white.

A broken spring in a factory yard,

Rust that clings to the form that the strength has left

Hard and curled and ready to snap.

Half-past two,

The street-lamp said,

“Remark the cat which flattens itself in the gutter,

Slips out its tongue

And devours a morsel of rancid butter.”

So the hand of the child, automatic,

Slipped out and pocketed a toy that was running along the quay.

I could see nothing behind that child’s eye.

I have seen eyes in the street

Trying to peer through lighted shutters,

And a crab one afternoon in a pool,

An old crab with barnacles on his back,

Gripped the end of a stick which I held him.

Half-past three,

The lamp sputtered,

The lamp muttered in the dark.

The lamp hummed:

“Regard the moon,

La lune ne garde aucune rancune,

She winks a feeble eye,

She smiles into corners.

She smooths the hair of the grass.

The moon has lost her memory.

A washed-out smallpox cracks her face,

Her hand twists a paper rose,

That smells of dust and old Cologne,

She is alone

With all the old nocturnal smells

That cross and cross across her brain.

The reminiscence comes

Of sunless dry geraniums

And dust in crevices,

Smells of chestnuts in the streets

And female smells in shuttered rooms

And cigarettes in corridors

And cocktail smells in bars.”

The lamp said,

“Four o’clock,

Here is the number on the door.

Memory!

You have the key,

The little lamp spreads a ring on the stair,

Mount.

The bed is open; the toothbrush hangs on the wall,

Put your shoes at the door, sleep, prepare for life.”

The last twist of the knife.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem takes place over about four hours, and its structure is the clock. It begins at "Twelve o'clock," then moves to half-past one, half-past two, half-past three and finally four o'clock. A man is walking through an empty city at night, under a series of street lamps. As he passes each lamp, something happens in his mind, and the lamp itself seems to speak, urging him to look at something. The poem is both an outer walk and an inner journey, and the two keep blurring.

The first stanza sets the mood. The moon casts a "lunar synthesis," and its "whispering lunar incantations / Dissolve the floors of memory / And all its clear relations, / Its divisions and precisions." This is the Bergson idea in poetic form. Normally we keep our memories in order, filed by date and meaning. Under the moon at midnight that order collapses. Then comes the poem's most famous image: "Midnight shakes the memory / As a madman shakes a dead geranium." The memory is a dead plant in a pot, and the night is a lunatic who shakes it, scattering its dried pieces. Each lamp "Beats like a fatalistic drum," so that the walker moves to a menacing rhythm.

Then the lamp begins to speak. At half-past one it tells him to "Regard that woman / Who hesitates toward you in the light of the door / Which opens on her like a grin." We see her torn, sand-stained dress and the eye that "Twists like a crooked pin." She is a prostitute, though the poem never says so. The doorway opens like a smile, a sinister one, and the whole scene is sour and unnerving.

Next, the memory "throws up high and dry / A crowd of twisted things": a polished piece of driftwood and a broken spring in a factory yard. They are images of things from which life has gone, "the form that the strength has left." At half-past two the lamp points to a cat gobbling rancid butter, and the poem moves to a child who automatically pockets a toy. The narrator says, "I could see nothing behind that child's eye." This is unsettling. Even innocence seems mechanical, empty.

At half-past three the lamp hums about the moon, whose face is scarred, who has "lost her memory," and who is "alone / With all the old nocturnal smells." The moon is a ruined woman, a sad parody of the romantic moon. Finally, at four o'clock, the lamp tells the walker he has the key to the door, to go up, take off his shoes, and "sleep, prepare for life." The poem ends with the cruelly flat line "The last twist of the knife." The prospect of ordinary life is the final wound.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Rhapsody on a Windy Night is one of Eliot's most daring early poems, and its daring is mostly rewarded. The central device, a street lamp that narrates, is wonderfully strange. It gives the poem a dramatic structure without a plot, and it lets Eliot comment on the scene from a point of view that is neither the walker's nor quite anyone else's. The lamp "sputtered," "muttered" and "hummed," and those verbs give it a personality that is part prophet, part cabaret performer, part nagging conscience.

The imagery is the poem's greatest achievement. The madman and the dead geranium is among the finest similes of the century, vivid, comic and horrifying at once. It conveys the violence and the futility of memory in a single stroke. The twisted branch eaten smooth by the sea, the "secret of its skeleton, / Stiff and white," and the old crab gripping a stick, are likewise hard, exact and unforgettable. They are not decorative. They carry the emotional logic of the piece, which is that experience leaves behind twisted, lifeless remnants.

The poem's form also deserves praise. Eliot uses free verse with irregular rhyme, bursts of short lines and sudden, slower reflection, so that the verse seems to wander like the walker. The timing of the hours gives a firm skeleton that keeps the dreamlike material from dissolving. And the closing line, brief and brutal, shows how well he understood the force of an abrupt ending.

But there are real weaknesses. The poem is very dense in its allusion to French poetry, including a line in French, "La lune ne garde aucune rancune," meaning the moon bears no grudge, which plays on sound as much as sense and is likely to puzzle readers who do not know the language. The moon stanza, though evocative, is long and a bit overloaded with smells and sensations, and at times the piling-up of images risks blurring rather than sharpening the effect.

The poem is also hard to paraphrase, and some readers feel that its connection between the street scenes and the idea of memory is more asserted than demonstrated. The woman, the cat and the child are presented with great intensity, but exactly what they mean for the narrator is left obscure, and a reader may sense a pose of nausea more than a worked-out insight.

Finally, the poem's view of the city and its inhabitants, especially the woman, is unflinchingly cold. This is part of its power, but it also limits its range of feeling. It is a brilliant study of alienation that offers little warmth in return.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's impact is quieter than that of Eliot's most famous works, but it is real. Appearing in Blast in 1915 alongside Preludes, it signaled to a small, influential audience that something new was happening in English verse. Blast was an aggressive manifesto of the Vorticist movement, with big black capital letters and a pink cover, and putting Eliot's night-time city poems there tied them to the most radical art of the day. The poem then helped fill out Prufrock and Other Observations, the 1917 book that made his reputation.

Within Eliot's own work, the poem is an important rehearsal for later techniques. The idea of an unreal, dreamlike city lit by artificial light recurs in The Waste Land, with its "Unreal City" and its crowds. The sudden appearance of grotesque, symbolic figures in doorways and the use of nightmarish detail also feed into the longer poem. The crab with barnacles that grips a stick recalls the "ragged claws" of Prufrock, and the theme of memory as both curse and key continues through his work to Four Quartets.

The poem also had an effect on how poets used philosophical ideas. Eliot attended Bergson's lectures and took the philosopher's ideas about time and memory not as a doctrine but as raw material for imagery. This gave later poets a model for translating abstract thought into concrete poetic images, a strategy that became central to modernist practice. Eliot later wrote a doctoral thesis on the philosopher F. H. Bradley and moved away from Bergson, but the early poem preserves a fascination with the flow of consciousness.

In terms of broader culture, critics have often noted that the poem seems to anticipate later developments. Its disorienting urban night, with sinister doorways and talking street lights, looks forward to surrealism and, in its mood and imagery, to film noir, the dark cinema of the 1940s with its wet streets and shadowed figures. Whether or not direct influence can be shown, the kinship is plain, and film scholars and literary critics alike have noted it.

In teaching, the poem is a favourite for showing how modernist poetry handles time and consciousness, and how a poem can be structured by an arbitrary scheme such as the hours of the clock. It is regularly paired with Preludes and Prufrock in courses on early Eliot and on urban poetry. Though it is not as widely quoted as some of his lines, specialists value it highly as one of the most original poems of his early period.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who love Rhapsody on a Windy Night tend to love it with unusual intensity, partly because it feels like a secret. It is not as famous as Prufrock, and those who discover it often feel they have found something uncanny. The first thing they mention is usually the image of the madman shaking the dead geranium. It is so startling and so right that it sticks, and it gives memory a sinister physical presence that few poems have matched.

They also love the talking lamp. There is something delightful, and slightly chilling, in the idea of a lamp muttering instructions on what to look at, as though a streetlight were a kind of ghostly guide. The repeated phrases, "The street lamp said," "Regard," "Remark," have the rhythm of a spell or a sinister lesson, and the effect on a listener is hypnotic. The poem works beautifully when read aloud.

Many admirers are drawn to its atmosphere of insomnia and nocturnal wandering. People who have walked a city at night, unable to sleep and half in a dream, recognize the odd clarity and the odd dread of that state. The poem captures the way the mind at night goes over scraps of memory and cannot control them, and it does so with a precision that feels like recognition.

The poem's sensory richness has also won readers over. The smells in the moon's stanza, "chestnuts in the streets / And female smells in shuttered rooms / And cigarettes in corridors / And cocktail smells in bars," are handled with such flair that one can almost smell them. It is a rare poem that uses smell as a principal sense, and it conveys how memory is often triggered by odour more than by sight.

Finally, readers have admired the ending. The instruction to "sleep, prepare for life" followed by the single line "The last twist of the knife" is a masterly piece of bleak comedy and horror. It turns the comfort of bed and toothbrush into a threat, and it reveals the poem's hidden argument that the ordinary routines of life are themselves a kind of quiet violence. Readers love being taken so far into darkness by a poem that is so formally controlled, and they often find that, oddly, it leaves them feeling more awake.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Rhapsody on a Windy Night has had relatively little negative commentary, partly because it is a lesser-known work, and I want to be honest that there is not a rich documented history of dispute about it. What follows are the main reservations that critics and readers have raised in general terms.

One concerns difficulty. The poem moves between dream, memory and street observation without clear signposts, and some readers have found it opaque. The relation between the hour markers and the images is not always obvious, and the philosophical background, Bergson's ideas about memory and time, is not stated in the poem. A reader without that context may feel that the sequence of images is arbitrary, and might wonder whether the strangeness is a mere pose.

A second concerns the poem's attitude to its human figures. The woman in the doorway, with her torn dress and twisted eye, is presented as a grotesque. The child is seen as an empty automaton. Critics who care about how literature treats women and the poor have noted that Eliot's early city poems often present such figures as objects of disgust or as signs of the narrator's alienation rather than as persons. The poem seems more interested in the speaker's nausea than in the woman's situation, and that is a fair complaint.

A third concerns derivativeness. The poem owes much to French Symbolist poets, particularly Jules Laforgue, whose moon poems and ironic tone are close to Eliot's. Some critics have said that the poem is more a brilliant pastiche of a French manner than a fully independent creation. Eliot himself acknowledged how much Laforgue had meant to him, and the debt is open. Whether it diminishes the poem is a matter of taste.

Some have also found the poem too relentlessly bleak. Its vision of memory as a mere heap of broken things, and of life as a twist of the knife, allows no relief, and in a poem of this length the cumulative effect can feel unvaried. A few readers prefer the human mixture of comedy and pathos in Prufrock to this colder performance.

Because its original reception was limited to the small readership of Blast and a small volume, there is little record of major contemporary reviews focused on it specifically. Its reputation has grown through later critical attention, not through immediate acclaim, and it is generally regarded today as a strong, if minor, entry in Eliot's early canon.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Rhapsody on a Windy Night belongs to the first phase of high modernism, and sits naturally beside Prufrock and Preludes as one of Eliot's early city poems. Modernism, broadly, was the movement that tried to capture the fractured experience of modern life through new, often disjointed forms. Rhapsody is a prime example, using the imagery of the night city to express the breakdown of ordered consciousness.

Its ancestors are largely French. Charles Baudelaire's night-time Paris, with its prostitutes, beggars and wanderers, lies behind the poem's streets. Jules Laforgue's ironic moon poems supply its tone and some of its phrases. The Symbolist tradition, which sought to express states of mind through concrete images, is the main current it belongs to. The philosophy of Henri Bergson adds a distinctive intellectual layer, and the title borrows from music, a rhapsody being an irregular, free-flowing piece.

There are also links to the older tradition of the night walk in literature, from Thomas De Quincey's wanderings in London to the dreamlike city passages of Charles Dickens, and the Romantic idea that the mind is most revealing at night. Eliot makes this tradition modern by stripping away romantic comfort. The moon, once a symbol of love and inspiration, becomes a scarred, forgetful old woman.

What descends from the poem is broad if sometimes indirect. The motif of the lonely city walker, observing and unsettled, reappears in a long line of writing, from the later poems of Eliot himself to the work of the Beat poets and the urban novel. The way the poem treats the city as a kind of externalized mind is echoed in Surrealism and in films like those of the noir tradition, with their rain-slicked streets and sinister doorways. In modern poetry it prefigures the dreamlike urban sequences of poets such as John Ashbery and the New York School, though they are lighter in tone.

Compared with Prufrock, Rhapsody is more fragmentary and less dramatic. Compared with Preludes, it is more nightmarish and less tender. Compared with The Waste Land, it is a concentrated first experiment in the same vision. It shows Eliot working out how to let images, rather than argument, carry emotion.

Why does it still matter? Because it shows how a poem can make the inner life visible. Anyone who has been awake at three in the morning with memories running loose will find the experience described here, with eerie accuracy. It is short, strange and unforgettable, and it rewards reading aloud in a quiet room, with the lights low. Its message, that memory is not a gentle companion but a force that shakes us, may be uncomfortable, but it has the ring of truth.
