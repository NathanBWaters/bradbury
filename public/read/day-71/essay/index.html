On Sandals and Simplicity

G. K. Chesterton (1905) • 8 min read

There is much more simplicity in the man who eats caviar on impulse than in the man who eats grape-nuts on principle.

Key Context & Why It Matters

G. K. Chesterton wrote this short essay in 1905 as a chapter of Heretics, a book in which he argued with the famous thinkers of his day. Its target is the simple life, a fashion among the educated of Edwardian England. People gave up meat, wore plain clothes, and admired Tolstoy, the great Russian novelist who had renounced his wealth and preached peaceful, humble living. Chesterton was a big, jolly, cigar-smoking journalist who loved feasts, and he thought these reformers had the whole idea backward. His claim is that they simplify the things that do not matter, like diet and clothing, while tangling up the things that do, like loyalty, philosophy, and joy. The only simplicity worth having, he says, is simplicity of the heart: the ability to be astonished and glad. As you listen, watch for his famous image of a man in sandals with a raw tomato in his hand, and notice how Chesterton uses humor to make a serious argument about what it means to live well.

The great misfortune of the modern English is not at all that they are more boastful than other people (they are not); it is that they are boastful about those particular things which nobody can boast of without losing them. A Frenchman can be proud of being bold and logical, and still remain bold and logical. A German can be proud of being reflective and orderly, and still remain reflective and orderly. But an Englishman cannot be proud of being simple and direct, and still remain simple and direct. In the matter of these strange virtues, to know them is to kill them. A man may be conscious of being heroic or conscious of being divine, but he cannot (in spite of all the Anglo-Saxon poets) be conscious of being unconscious.

Now, I do not think that it can be honestly denied that some portion of this impossibility attaches to a class very different in their own opinion, at least, to the school of Anglo-Saxonism. I mean that school of the simple life, commonly associated with Tolstoy. If a perpetual talk about one’s own robustness leads to being less robust, it is even more true that a perpetual talking about one’s own simplicity leads to being less simple. One great complaint, I think, must stand against the modern upholders of the simple life⁠—the simple life in all its varied forms, from vegetarianism to the honourable consistency of the Doukhobors. This complaint against them stands, that they would make us simple in the unimportant things, but complex in the important things. They would make us simple in the things that do not matter⁠—that is, in diet, in costume, in etiquette, in economic system. But they would make us complex in the things that do matter⁠—in philosophy, in loyalty, in spiritual acceptance, and spiritual rejection. It does not so very much matter whether a man eats a grilled tomato or a plain tomato; it does very much matter whether he eats a plain tomato with a grilled mind. The only kind of simplicity worth preserving is the simplicity of the heart, the simplicity which accepts and enjoys. There may be a reasonable doubt as to what system preserves this; there can surely be no doubt that a system of simplicity destroys it. There is more simplicity in the man who eats caviar on impulse than in the man who eats grape-nuts on principle. The chief error of these people is to be found in the very phrase to which they are most attached⁠—“plain living and high thinking.” These people do not stand in need of, will not be improved by, plain living and high thinking. They stand in need of the contrary. They would be improved by high living and plain thinking. A little high living (I say, having a full sense of responsibility, a little high living) would teach them the force and meaning of the human festivities, of the banquet that has gone on from the beginning of the world. It would teach them the historic fact that the artificial is, if anything, older than the natural. It would teach them that the loving-cup is as old as any hunger. It would teach them that ritualism is older than any religion. And a little plain thinking would teach them how harsh and fanciful are the mass of their own ethics, how very civilized and very complicated must be the brain of the Tolstoyan who really believes it to be evil to love one’s country and wicked to strike a blow.

A man approaches, wearing sandals and simple raiment, a raw tomato held firmly in his right hand, and says, “The affections of family and country alike are hindrances to the fuller development of human love;” but the plain thinker will only answer him, with a wonder not untinged with admiration, “What a great deal of trouble you must have taken in order to feel like that.” High living will reject the tomato. Plain thinking will equally decisively reject the idea of the invariable sinfulness of war. High living will convince us that nothing is more materialistic than to despise a pleasure as purely material. And plain thinking will convince us that nothing is more materialistic than to reserve our horror chiefly for material wounds.

The only simplicity that matters is the simplicity of the heart. If that be gone, it can be brought back by no turnips or cellular clothing; but only by tears and terror and the fires that are not quenched. If that remain, it matters very little if a few Early Victorian armchairs remain along with it. Let us put a complex entrée into a simple old gentleman; let us not put a simple entrée into a complex old gentleman. So long as human society will leave my spiritual inside alone, I will allow it, with a comparative submission, to work its wild will with my physical interior. I will submit to cigars. I will meekly embrace a bottle of Burgundy. I will humble myself to a hansom cab. If only by this means I may preserve to myself the virginity of the spirit, which enjoys with astonishment and fear. I do not say that these are the only methods of preserving it. I incline to the belief that there are others. But I will have nothing to do with simplicity which lacks the fear, the astonishment, and the joy alike. I will have nothing to do with the devilish vision of a child who is too simple to like toys.

The child is, indeed, in these, and many other matters, the best guide. And in nothing is the child so righteously childlike, in nothing does he exhibit more accurately the sounder order of simplicity, than in the fact that he sees everything with a simple pleasure, even the complex things. The false type of naturalness harps always on the distinction between the natural and the artificial. The higher kind of naturalness ignores that distinction. To the child the tree and the lamppost are as natural and as artificial as each other; or rather, neither of them are natural but both supernatural. For both are splendid and unexplained. The flower with which God crowns the one, and the flame with which Sam the lamplighter crowns the other, are equally of the gold of fairytales. In the middle of the wildest fields the most rustic child is, ten to one, playing at steam-engines. And the only spiritual or philosophical objection to steam-engines is not that men pay for them or work at them, or make them very ugly, or even that men are killed by them; but merely that men do not play at them. The evil is that the childish poetry of clockwork does not remain. The wrong is not that engines are too much admired, but that they are not admired enough. The sin is not that engines are mechanical, but that men are mechanical.

In this matter, then, as in all the other matters treated in this book, our main conclusion is that it is a fundamental point of view, a philosophy or religion which is needed, and not any change in habit or social routine. The things we need most for immediate practical purposes are all abstractions. We need a right view of the human lot, a right view of the human society; and if we were living eagerly and angrily in the enthusiasm of those things, we should, ipso facto, be living simply in the genuine and spiritual sense. Desire and danger make everyone simple. And to those who talk to us with interfering eloquence about Jaeger and the pores of the skin, and about Plasmon and the coats of the stomach, at them shall only be hurled the words that are hurled at fops and gluttons, “Take no thought what ye shall eat or what ye shall drink, or wherewithal ye shall be clothed. For after all these things do the Gentiles seek. But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added unto you.” Those amazing words are not only extraordinarily good, practical politics; they are also superlatively good hygiene. The one supreme way of making all those processes go right, the processes of health, and strength, and grace, and beauty, the one and only way of making certain of their accuracy, is to think about something else. If a man is bent on climbing into the seventh heaven, he may be quite easy about the pores of his skin. If he harnesses his wagon to a star, the process will have a most satisfactory effect upon the coats of his stomach. For the thing called “taking thought,” the thing for which the best modern word is “rationalizing,” is in its nature, inapplicable to all plain and urgent things. Men take thought and ponder rationalistically, touching remote things⁠—things that only theoretically matter, such as the transit of Venus. But only at their peril can men rationalize about so practical a matter as health.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Chesterton opens with a joke that is also an argument. The English, he says, are proud of the wrong things. A Frenchman can boast of being logical and stay logical, but an Englishman who boasts of being simple and direct stops being either. Some virtues die the moment you become conscious of them. You cannot, as he puts it, be conscious of being unconscious. That sets up his target: the fans of the simple life, the vegetarians, the plain dressers, the followers of Tolstoy, even the Doukhobors, a Russian Christian sect that refused to fight in wars or own much property and that Tolstoy helped move to Canada.

His complaint is precise. These reformers, he says, make us simple in the unimportant things and complex in the important ones. They want a plain diet, plain clothes, plain manners, a plain economic system. But in philosophy, loyalty, and spiritual belief they ask for something fiercely elaborate. Then comes one of his best lines: it matters very little whether a man eats a grilled tomato or a plain one, but it matters a great deal whether he eats a plain tomato with a grilled mind. In other words, the menu is trivial and the mind is everything.

From there he flips a famous slogan. The reformers love the phrase plain living and high thinking. Chesterton says they need the opposite: high living and plain thinking. A little high living, a feast or a toast, would teach them that celebration is older than philosophy, that the loving cup is as old as hunger, and that ritual is older than any religion. A little plain thinking would teach them how strange their own ethics are. Here is where the man in sandals appears, holding a raw tomato, declaring that love of family and country are obstacles to universal love. The plain thinker's reply is gentle and devastating: what a great deal of trouble you must have taken to feel like that. Chesterton's point is that ordinary human loyalties are natural, and it takes elaborate effort to talk yourself out of them.

The deeper layer is about wonder. He says the only simplicity that matters is the kind that accepts and enjoys, the kind that sees everything with astonishment and fear and joy. His model is the child, who treats a tree and a lamppost as equally marvelous, so that neither is merely natural or merely artificial. Both are splendid and unexplained. The essay's closing note is that the real problem with machines is not that they are mechanical but that people become mechanical, and that when we lose the childlike poetry of things, no change of habit will bring it back.

A first-time reader might miss how personal this is. Chesterton is not defending luxury as such. He is defending the capacity to be grateful, which he thinks the austere crowd has lost while congratulating itself on its virtue.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The essay is a good example of Chesterton at his most quotable, and also at his most slippery. Its strengths are obvious. The central distinction, between simplicity of habit and simplicity of heart, is genuinely useful, and he states it in images that stick: the grilled mind, the tomato, the caviar eaten on impulse versus the grape-nuts eaten on principle. That last pairing is a small masterpiece of comic compression. It lets him say, in a dozen words, that spontaneity is a matter of spirit rather than menu, and that a rule followed solemnly can be more artificial than a luxury enjoyed freely.

His method is the paradox, and here the craft is real. He takes a slogan everyone has heard, turns it upside down, and shows that the inverted version is arguably truer. High living and plain thinking is not just a joke. It contains an actual claim: that those who deny themselves pleasure on principle often become the most tangled thinkers, while those who enjoy things honestly tend to see them more clearly. The prose is brisk, the sentences land with a snap, and the humor never fully covers the argument.

But the essay has weaknesses a careful reader should notice. First, Chesterton lumps together very different people. A vegetarian who simply prefers vegetables, a Tolstoyan pacifist who has thought hard about violence, and a health faddist selling cereal are all dropped into one basket. He is far more convincing against the faddist than against Tolstoy, whose moral seriousness he mostly mocks rather than answers. The line about how much trouble the sandal-wearer must have taken is funny, but it is not a refutation of pacifism. It is a rhetorical dodge that makes a disagreement look like a psychological symptom.

Second, there is a convenient circularity. He defines real simplicity as the simplicity of the heart, then assures us that people who cultivate austerity lack it, while people who enjoy cigars and Burgundy have it. That is the sort of claim you cannot easily check, and it conveniently flatters the author's own habits. His later insistence that war is not invariably sinful is stated far more confidently than it is argued here.

Third, the essay can feel dated. Jaeger woolen underclothes, Plasmon, and the coats of the stomach were specific Edwardian fads, and some of the humor requires footnotes. The closing appeal to the Gospel words about taking no thought for food and clothing is moving, but it shifts the argument from persuasion to scripture without warning.

Even so, the essay's core insight survives. It is not a careful treatise. It is a sharp, funny provocation, and it works best when read as one: an invitation to ask whether our habits of virtue have quietly replaced the joy they were meant to protect.

III. The Impact It Had

It is worth being honest that this particular essay did not change history by itself. Its influence is the influence of Heretics as a whole, and of the Chesterton style it helped define. Heretics appeared in 1905, when Chesterton was in his early thirties and already a well-known columnist. The book collected his pieces criticizing the leading ideas of his age, taking on figures like George Bernard Shaw, H. G. Wells, Rudyard Kipling, and the champions of art for art's sake. Its most lasting effect was to provoke a challenge. A critic and friend, G. S. Street, remarked that he would take Chesterton's complaints more seriously once Chesterton had set out his own philosophy. That challenge produced Orthodoxy three years later, the book many readers consider Chesterton's masterpiece. So one of the most concrete results of the Heretics essays, this one included, was to push their author into stating what he actually believed.

On a wider scale, the essay belongs to a long tradition of skepticism about moral fashion. The idea that a lifestyle can become a performance, that virtue can curdle into smugness, has a long afterlife. Later readers will recognize the type Chesterton sketches in every era's version of the earnest self-improver, from health cults to minimalism to fashionable asceticism. His insight that conspicuous simplicity is often a kind of display, and that it can coexist with complicated vanity, is one cultural critics still borrow, whether or not they know where it came from.

It also fed the Distributist and Christian humanist currents that Chesterton led. His defense of feasts, rituals, and ordinary loyalties became part of the case he later made for family, small property, and local life. Writers who shared his temperament, such as Hilaire Belloc, and later admirers like C. S. Lewis and J. R. R. Tolkien, absorbed the idea that the artificial is not the enemy of the natural, and that joy and ceremony are signs of health.

In education, the essay is mostly encountered in anthologies of Chesterton, in courses on the English essay, or as a short, funny example of paradox and argument by reversal. Teachers use it to show how a writer can attack an idea by turning its own slogan against it. It is also read by people interested in the Edwardian debates over socialism, vegetarianism, pacifism, and Tolstoyan communities.

There were no adaptations to speak of, but the lines survive in quotation. The grilled mind remains a stock phrase among people who admire Chesterton, and the contrast between plain living and high living is the sort of thing a reader can carry away intact after a single listen. That portability, more than any political consequence, is the essay's real legacy.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have most often loved in Chesterton, and in this essay especially, is the sense of being let out of a stuffy room. The Edwardian simple-life movement could be earnest, sanctimonious, and joyless. Chesterton arrived with laughter, a bottle of Burgundy, and an argument that sounded like common sense turned inside out. Many readers, then and now, feel relieved to hear someone say that it is fine to enjoy a good meal, that pleasure is not a betrayal of spirit, and that the habit of constant self-denial can be its own vanity.

They also love the comedy. The man in sandals with the raw tomato is one of the funniest small portraits in English prose. It works because it is specific: the tomato is held firmly in the right hand, as though it were evidence. The reply, what a great deal of trouble you must have taken, is the sort of line people repeat, because it captures a recognizable feeling about performative virtue without being cruel about the person.

Admirers have long praised how Chesterton combines wit with real conviction. Writers as different as Jorge Luis Borges, who admired his stories and paradoxes, and Marshall McLuhan, who credited him as an influence, were drawn to his habit of making the familiar strange. C. S. Lewis later said that reading Chesterton helped open his mind to Christianity, though that credit is for the broader body of his work rather than this single essay. Among general readers, Chesterton is loved for being an optimist who was not a fool: someone who believed in gratitude and wonder but also knew how to argue.

There is another reason this piece resonates. Its central claim, that the childlike capacity to be astonished is the thing to protect, speaks to people who feel the modern world has made them tired and jaded. Chesterton's picture of a child who sees the lamppost and the tree as equally marvelous, and who plays at steam engines in the middle of a field, is genuinely tender. It makes the case for wonder without sentimentality, partly because it is attached to a joke about engines and clockwork.

Finally, readers love the ending's turn. After all the teasing, he lands on something close to a prayer: that the real way to be healthy is to think about something else, to climb toward the seventh heaven and let the pores of the skin look after themselves. It is a lovely, slightly mad sentence, and it leaves the reader feeling that the whole argument has been a way of pointing at joy.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Chesterton's critics have never been in short supply, and this essay offers several easy targets. The most common charge is that he argues by caricature. The man in sandals with the tomato is a comic type, not a real opponent. Tolstoy was a serious moral thinker who had wrestled with violence, property, and faith, and the Doukhobors had endured persecution for their pacifism. Reducing them to the cranks of the health-food shop lets Chesterton score points without engaging their best reasons. Readers sympathetic to pacifism or to voluntary poverty have found the essay glib on exactly the subjects where glibness is least appropriate.

A second objection is that he treats his own preferences as universal wisdom. The defense of cigars, Burgundy, and a hansom cab is charming, but it comes close to saying that the writer's habits are the habits of a healthy soul. Critics have noticed that the simple-life movement, whatever its absurdities, was partly a response to real problems: industrial squalor, adulterated food, urban poverty, and consumerism. Chesterton's reply, that these are matters of philosophy rather than lifestyle, is reasonable but sidesteps the fact that habits and systems do shape lives.

Third, there is the charge that his paradox method proves less than it seems. Reversing a slogan is a wonderful way to be memorable, but it does not by itself establish that the reversal is true. High living and plain thinking is a delightful phrase, and some readers have felt that it works as a debating trick, leaving the substantive question of how much simplicity is wise unresolved. Similarly, the sweeping assurance that plain thinking will reject the idea that war is always wrong is asserted, not demonstrated.

Fourth, some modern readers are uneasy with the way Chesterton's wider outlook shades into a conservative, religiously confident view of society, with assumptions about nation, family, and ritual that are treated as obviously natural. Critics who admire his style have still found that these assumptions go unexamined. Others have pointed out that the essay passes quickly over the poor, for whom plain living was never a choice, and who are absent from this very comfortable argument.

Finally, a historical caution. The essay's anxieties are Edwardian, and a few of its jokes depend on knowing now-forgotten products and fashions. Some find the humor to have aged better than the argument. A fair summary is that the piece is widely enjoyed for its wit, while its reasoning is regarded as stronger as inspired provocation than as rigorous philosophy.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Chesterton sits in a particular English tradition: the essayist as public debater, using humor and common sense to puncture the pretensions of intellectual fashion. He descends from writers like Samuel Johnson, William Hazlitt, and Charles Lamb, and shares a family resemblance with Dickens, whom he loved and wrote about with great warmth. Like Dickens, he believes in comfort, hospitality, and the warmth of the hearth, and he is suspicious of reformers who treat ordinary human pleasure as a problem to be solved.

The essay also belongs to a long argument about simplicity itself. On one side stand Rousseau, Thoreau, Tolstoy, and the Arts and Crafts movement, who believed that modern life had grown corrupt and artificial, and who urged a return to nature and plainness. On the other stand thinkers who think this impulse is itself a luxury or a mask. Chesterton is a witty member of the second camp, though he is unusual in that he is no friend of modern industry either. His quarrel is not with simplicity as a value but with simplicity as a method. Thoreau, for example, is a more subtle opponent than Chesterton's caricature suggests, since Walden is much more about attention and wonder than about vegetarian rules, and Chesterton and Thoreau would have agreed more than the essay admits.

What descends from it? You can trace its spirit in later critiques of lifestyle virtue, in essays about consumerism disguised as minimalism, and in the broader suspicion of what is now called virtue signaling. George Orwell, who had a very different politics, made a similar complaint in his own bluntly funny way about the sandal-wearing, fruit-juice-drinking cranks of the left in The Road to Wigan Pier. Orwell and Chesterton disagreed about almost everything, but they shared a dislike of the faddish and the self-righteous, and the comparison is worth making. Within Chesterton's own work, this essay is a preview of the themes of Orthodoxy: wonder, gratitude, the importance of ordinary things, and the idea that the wildest thing in the world is the world itself.

Why read it now? Partly because the target has never gone away. Every generation produces its own tomato-wielding prophet, and every generation needs someone to ask whether the effort of feeling a certain way is a sign of the feeling's reality. It is also worth reading for the pleasure of the prose, which is high-spirited and funny and surprisingly tender.

And finally, it is worth reading because it asks a question that remains open. If simplicity is not about what we eat or wear, what is it about? Chesterton's answer, astonishment and gladness, may not settle the question, but it points toward a way of living that is generous rather than severe, and that is still a pretty good place to start.
