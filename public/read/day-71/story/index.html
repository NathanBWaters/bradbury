Vanka

Anton Chekhov (1886) • 8 min read

“How about a pinch of snuff?” he was saying, offering the women his snuffbox.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Anton Chekhov wrote Vanka in 1886, when he was twenty-six, a young doctor in Moscow turning out comic sketches for newspapers under a joking pen name. It appeared in a Christmas issue, and it is one of the shortest and most quietly devastating stories he ever wrote. Chekhov knew this world. His grandfather had been a serf, born into bondage until his family bought its freedom, and Chekhov himself had watched children treated as cheap labor. Russia had freed its serfs only twenty-five years earlier, and freedom had left many poor families with few choices except to send their children away to work. The story follows a nine-year-old apprentice who writes a letter on Christmas Eve to the one person who loves him. Listen for the way Chekhov never begs you to cry. He simply lets a child describe his misery in a child's words, and then he lets you notice what the boy does not understand. Pay particular attention to the address on the envelope.

Vanka Zhukov, a boy of nine, who had been for three months apprenticed to Alyahin the shoemaker, was sitting up on Christmas Eve. Waiting till his master and mistress and their workmen had gone to the midnight service, he took out of his master’s cupboard a bottle of ink and a pen with a rusty nib, and, spreading out a crumpled sheet of paper in front of him, began writing. Before forming the first letter he several times looked round fearfully at the door and the windows, stole a glance at the dark icon, on both sides of which stretched shelves full of lasts, and heaved a broken sigh. The paper lay on the bench while he knelt before it.

“Dear grandfather, Konstantin Makaritch,” he wrote, “I am writing you a letter. I wish you a happy Christmas, and all blessings from God Almighty. I have neither father nor mother, you are the only one left me.”

Vanka raised his eyes to the dark icon on which the light of his candle was reflected, and vividly recalled his grandfather, Konstantin Makaritch, who was night watchman to a family called Zhivarev. He was a thin but extraordinarily nimble and lively little old man of sixty-five, with an everlastingly laughing face and drunken eyes. By day he slept in the servants’ kitchen, or made jokes with the cooks; at night, wrapped in an ample sheepskin, he walked round the grounds and tapped with his little mallet. Old Kashtanka and Eel, so-called on account of his dark colour and his long body like a weasel’s, followed him with hanging heads. This Eel was exceptionally polite and affectionate, and looked with equal kindness on strangers and his own masters, but had not a very good reputation. Under his politeness and meekness was hidden the most Jesuitical cunning. No one knew better how to creep up on occasion and snap at one’s legs, to slip into the storeroom, or steal a hen from a peasant. His hind legs had been nearly pulled off more than once, twice he had been hanged, every week he was thrashed till he was half dead, but he always revived.

At this moment grandfather was, no doubt, standing at the gate, screwing up his eyes at the red windows of the church, stamping with his high felt boots, and joking with the servants. His little mallet was hanging on his belt. He was clasping his hands, shrugging with the cold, and, with an aged chuckle, pinching first the housemaid, then the cook.

“How about a pinch of snuff?” he was saying, offering the women his snuffbox.

The women would take a sniff and sneeze. Grandfather would be indescribably delighted, go off into a merry chuckle, and cry:

“Tear it off, it has frozen on!”

They give the dogs a sniff of snuff too. Kashtanka sneezes, wriggles her head, and walks away offended. Eel does not sneeze, from politeness, but wags his tail. And the weather is glorious. The air is still, fresh, and transparent. The night is dark, but one can see the whole village with its white roofs and coils of smoke coming from the chimneys, the trees silvered with hoar frost, the snowdrifts. The whole sky spangled with gay twinkling stars, and the Milky Way is as distinct as though it had been washed and rubbed with snow for a holiday.⁠ ⁠…

Vanka sighed, dipped his pen, and went on writing:

“And yesterday I had a wigging. The master pulled me out into the yard by my hair, and whacked me with a boot-stretcher because I accidentally fell asleep while I was rocking their brat in the cradle. And a week ago the mistress told me to clean a herring, and I began from the tail end, and she took the herring and thrust its head in my face. The workmen laugh at me and send me to the tavern for vodka, and tell me to steal the master’s cucumbers for them, and the master beats me with anything that comes to hand. And there is nothing to eat. In the morning they give me bread, for dinner, porridge, and in the evening, bread again; but as for tea, or soup, the master and mistress gobble it all up themselves. And I am put to sleep in the passage, and when their wretched brat cries I get no sleep at all, but have to rock the cradle. Dear grandfather, show the Divine Mercy, take me away from here, home to the village. It’s more than I can bear. I bow down to your feet, and will pray to God for you forever, take me away from here or I shall die.”

Vanka’s mouth worked, he rubbed his eyes with his black fist, and gave a sob.

“I will powder your snuff for you,” he went on. “I will pray for you, and if I do anything you can thrash me like Sidor’s goat. And if you think I’ve no job, then I will beg the steward for Christ’s sake to let me clean his boots, or I’ll go for a shepherd-boy instead of Fedka. Dear grandfather, it is more than I can bear, it’s simply no life at all. I wanted to run away to the village, but I have no boots, and I am afraid of the frost. When I grow up big I will take care of you for this, and not let anyone annoy you, and when you die I will pray for the rest of your soul, just as for my mammy’s.”

“Moscow is a big town. It’s all gentlemen’s houses, and there are lots of horses, but there are no sheep, and the dogs are not spiteful. The lads here don’t go out with the star, and they don’t let anyone go into the choir, and once I saw in a shop window fishing-hooks for sale, fitted ready with the line and for all sorts of fish, awfully good ones, there was even one hook that would hold a forty-pound sheatfish. And I have seen shops where there are guns of all sorts, after the pattern of the master’s guns at home, so that I shouldn’t wonder if they are a hundred roubles each.⁠ ⁠… And in the butchers’ shops there are grouse and woodcocks and fish and hares, but the shopmen don’t say where they shoot them.”

“Dear grandfather, when they have the Christmas tree at the big house, get me a gilt walnut, and put it away in the green trunk. Ask the young lady Olga Ignatyevna, say it’s for Vanka.”

Vanka gave a tremulous sigh, and again stared at the window. He remembered how his grandfather always went into the forest to get the Christmas tree for his master’s family, and took his grandson with him. It was a merry time! Grandfather made a noise in his throat, the forest crackled with the frost, and looking at them Vanka chortled too. Before chopping down the Christmas tree, grandfather would smoke a pipe, slowly take a pinch of snuff, and laugh at frozen Vanka.⁠ ⁠… The young fir trees, covered with hoar frost, stood motionless, waiting to see which of them was to die. Wherever one looked, a hare flew like an arrow over the snowdrifts.⁠ ⁠… Grandfather could not refrain from shouting: “Hold him, hold him⁠ ⁠… hold him! Ah, the bobtailed devil!”

When he had cut down the Christmas tree, grandfather used to drag it to the big house, and there set to work to decorate it.⁠ ⁠… The young lady, who was Vanka’s favourite, Olga Ignatyevna, was the busiest of all. When Vanka’s mother Pelageya was alive, and a servant in the big house, Olga Ignatyevna used to give him goodies, and having nothing better to do, taught him to read and write, to count up to a hundred, and even to dance a quadrille. When Pelageya died, Vanka had been transferred to the servants’ kitchen to be with his grandfather, and from the kitchen to the shoemaker’s in Moscow.

“Do come, dear grandfather,” Vanka went on with his letter. “For Christ’s sake, I beg you, take me away. Have pity on an unhappy orphan like me; here everyone knocks me about, and I am fearfully hungry; I can’t tell you what misery it is, I am always crying. And the other day the master hit me on the head with a last, so that I fell down. My life is wretched, worse than any dog’s.⁠ ⁠… I send greetings to Alyona, one-eyed Yegorka, and the coachman, and don’t give my concertina to anyone. I remain, your grandson, Ivan Zhukov. Dear grandfather, do come.”

Vanka folded the sheet of writing-paper twice, and put it into an envelope he had bought the day before for a kopeck.⁠ ⁠… After thinking a little, he dipped the pen and wrote the address:

To grandfather in the village.

Then he scratched his head, thought a little, and added: Konstantin Makaritch. Glad that he had not been prevented from writing, he put on his cap and, without putting on his little greatcoat, ran out into the street as he was in his shirt.⁠ ⁠…

The shopmen at the butcher’s, whom he had questioned the day before, told him that letters were put in postboxes, and from the boxes were carried about all over the earth in mailcarts with drunken drivers and ringing bells. Vanka ran to the nearest postbox, and thrust the precious letter in the slit.⁠ ⁠…

An hour later, lulled by sweet hopes, he was sound asleep.⁠ ⁠… He dreamed of the stove. On the stove was sitting his grandfather, swinging his bare legs, and reading the letter to the cooks.⁠ ⁠…

By the stove was Eel, wagging his tail.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, Vanka is almost nothing. A nine-year-old boy, apprenticed to a shoemaker in Moscow, waits until his master and mistress have gone to the Christmas Eve service, steals a little ink and a rusty pen, and writes to his grandfather in the countryside. He begs to be taken away. He seals the letter, drops it into a postbox, and falls asleep dreaming that his grandfather is reading it aloud in the kitchen. That is the whole plot. The meaning lives in the gap between what Vanka believes he has done and what the reader knows he has done.

The key moment is the address. After all the pages of misery, Vanka writes on the envelope: "To grandfather in the village." Then he scratches his head, thinks, and adds the name Konstantin Makaritch. No village is named. No province. The letter will go nowhere, and the boy, who has been taught only a little reading and writing by a kind young lady in the big house, has no way to know that. The tenderness of that small afterthought, the added name, is exactly what breaks the heart. He believes the name is the missing piece, and it is not the piece that matters at all.

Chekhov builds the story by braiding two worlds. In the shoemaker's room, in the cold and the dark, Vanka is beaten with a strap, hit on the head with a shoemaker's last, fed scraps, and made to rock the baby. In the letter, and in the long memory that interrupts it, there is the village: the grandfather with his sheepskin and his snuff and his laughing face, the dogs Kashtanka and Eel, the frosty forest where they cut down the Christmas tree. The memory is so vivid that it threatens to become a rescue in itself. But notice that even the memory is not purely happy. The grandfather is a night watchman who lives in a servants' kitchen, hardly a free man himself. The old man is not a savior. He is another poor servant at the mercy of others, and he is nowhere near able to take in a grandson.

Some readers also catch the quiet detail that Vanka runs out to the postbox without his coat, in his shirt, in a Russian winter. The story lets the reader feel the cold the boy ignores. And the very last line, "By the stove was Eel, wagging his tail," is a perfect piece of misdirection. The dream is warm, and the dog, once a symbol of sly cunning in the memory, is simply glad. The reader leaves the story inside the dream, which makes the waking reality, the one only we can see, even harder to bear.

So the story is about hope without a means. It is about how the powerless reach for the only route they can imagine, and how the systems around them, in this case a postal system and a social order, cannot carry their cry. It is a story about a letter that cannot be delivered, and about the many people in every era whose cries are real but are addressed to nowhere.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged purely as craft, Vanka is close to flawless, and its great virtue is restraint. Chekhov could have narrated the boy's suffering in his own adult voice. Instead he mostly hands the page to Vanka, whose letter is full of childish priorities. In the middle of begging for rescue, he sends greetings to the coachman and says not to give his concertina to anyone. That detail is the kind of thing a writer cannot invent without having listened to real children. It makes the boy a specific child rather than an emblem of poverty, and it shows that the sorrow has not erased his sense of what is his.

The structure is also elegant. The story begins in the sad room, pulls back into a long and loving memory of the village, returns to the letter, and ends with the mailbox and the dream. The memory section is the story's secret engine. It does two jobs at once: it makes the grandfather real enough that we want the rescue, and it supplies the contrast that lets us feel the boy's present pain without a single speech about injustice. The pace is steady, and nothing is wasted. Even the dog Eel, who is introduced with a paragraph about his reputation for sly meekness, comes back at the end, and that small circle closes the story like a lid.

Where does it falter? Some readers find the ending a little too neatly engineered. The sleeping boy, the sweet dream, the wagging tail, the irony of the address: all of it is arranged to deliver one final pang, and a tougher reader might feel the hand of the writer pulling the string. Chekhov himself was suspicious of stories that tell you exactly when to cry, and this one comes closer to that line than most of his later work. It also deals in a classic type, the suffering orphan at Christmas, that Dickens and many sentimental writers of the era had worked heavily. The difference is that Chekhov refuses the consoling lie: nobody comes, the master is not redeemed, and the dream is only a dream. But the story can still feel, at moments, like a perfectly cut small gem rather than a deep investigation of anything.

There is also a limit of scale. The boy has no inner life beyond longing and fear, and the masters are shown only through blows. That is arguably the point, since the story is told from a child's low vantage, but it means there is no moral complexity. In Chekhov's best longer stories, the people who cause harm are as muddled and human as the people who endure it. Here, the shoemaker is a shadow with a strap.

Still, it is hard to complain much. The story is a few pages long, and in those pages it gets a whole social order, a whole childhood, and a whole kind of loneliness onto the page, without a single false note. If it is a minor work by Chekhov's later standards, it is minor in the way a perfectly tuned small instrument is minor.

III. The Impact It Had

Vanka was written early, in the stretch of Chekhov's career when he was supporting his family by writing quick humorous pieces for papers under the name Antosha Chekhonte. A few months before this story, the respected older writer Dmitry Grigorovich had written him a famous letter urging him to take his talent seriously and slow down. Vanka belongs to the moment when Chekhov began to do exactly that. It still carries his comic training, in its economy and its timing, but it uses those tools for something quieter and sadder. In that sense it is one of the early signs of the mature Chekhov, the writer who would go on to produce The Lady with the Dog, Ward No. 6, and the great plays.

Its larger impact is as a model for how to write about suffering without shouting. The story became a staple of Russian schoolrooms and has been taught for generations, often to children not much older than Vanka. It has been widely translated, and it is one of the most frequently anthologized of all Chekhov's stories in English. Because it is short, plain, and emotionally immediate, it is often a reader's first Chekhov, and for many readers it sets up an expectation of what he does: small events, deep consequences, and an ending that opens rather than closes.

In Russia it also had a life as a cultural reference. The phrase "to grandfather in the village" became a proverb of sorts for a message sent to no one, a plea that cannot arrive, or a hopelessly vague address. People who have never read the story use the phrase. That kind of absorption into everyday speech is a rare achievement for a short story.

The story also fits into a long conversation about child labor and the legacy of serfdom. Russia abolished serfdom in 1861, and the decades afterward were full of writing, from reformers and novelists alike, about the poverty and exploitation that survived the legal change. Chekhov did not write a polemic, and he generally avoided stating political positions in his fiction. Yet stories like this fed the same moral current as the reformers, simply by making a nine-year-old's cold room unforgettable. Later Soviet criticism, unsurprisingly, prized the story as an indictment of the old order, and that is one reason it was so widely taught in the twentieth century.

It has also been adapted: there have been Russian and other animated and live film versions, and the story has inspired stage readings and illustrated editions. Among writers, its mark can be seen wherever a short story trusts a child's limited understanding to carry a much larger adult truth. The device of dramatic irony, where the reader knows more than the narrator's central figure, is hardly Chekhov's alone, but Vanka is one of the cleanest examples in the form.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing readers tend to say about Vanka is that it hurts, and that it hurts without seeming to try. People who read it as children remember the address on the envelope for decades. That is a remarkable thing for a story to leave behind: a single line, a few words long, that detonates years later when a reader realizes what the boy did not.

Part of the love is for the child himself. Vanka is not a saint. He is hungry, scared, a little proud of his concertina, and capable of mixed-up thoughts, like his fierce protectiveness of his concertina. He remembers the village with complete affection but also with the odd, vivid, comic details only a real child would remember: the grandfather laughing at him shivering in the forest, the hare shooting across the snow, the shout of "the bobtailed devil." The warmth of those memories makes the story feel generous even as it is cruel. Readers admire that Chekhov lets the village be vivid and funny, rather than turning it into a stale paradise.

Fellow writers have long admired the story's economy. Chekhov's reputation among other writers is enormous, and short fiction writers from Katherine Mansfield to Raymond Carver to Alice Munro have been associated with his influence. For them Vanka represents a certain discipline: no commentary, no lecture, no summary of meaning, just a scene and a gesture. Teachers like it because it is easy to read yet hard to exhaust. You can read it in ten minutes and think about it for a week.

Readers also respond to the emotional honesty of the ending. A sentimental writer would have sent the grandfather to the rescue, or at least hinted that he might. Chekhov leaves the reader with a sweet dream and no help. The story does not tell you what to feel, but it does not let you off easily either. The pleasure of the dream, shared briefly by the reader, makes the reality hit harder, and many readers say that this gap between wish and fact is exactly what stays with them.

There is also a simple, durable appeal in its subject. Anyone who has ever been far from home, scared, and sure that one person could fix everything has some of Vanka in them. The story speaks across cultures and centuries because the feeling is so basic. A child alone at night, writing to someone who loves him, is a figure that needs very little cultural translation, and Chekhov draws him with such precise love that he stays in memory long after the plot is forgotten.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Vanka has not been a lightning rod for controversy, and it is worth saying so plainly. It is a short, early, widely loved story, and little has been written attacking it. But there are real reservations that critics and readers have raised over time, and they are worth taking seriously.

The most common one is sentimentality. The orphan boy, the beloved grandfather, the Christmas setting, the ill-addressed letter: these are the ingredients of Victorian-era pathos, and some critics have felt that Chekhov, for all his cool manner, is operating the machinery of tears here. This criticism has a respectable pedigree. Chekhov himself was famously allergic to overt emotional manipulation and is reported to have advised younger writers to keep a cool head when telling sad stories, so that the reader's own feeling would do the work. Vanka mostly follows that advice, but its final dream sequence and the pointed irony of the address do feel designed to make a reader's throat tighten. Some critics see it as a lesser work for exactly that reason, a polished piece of affecting craft rather than a deeper exploration of the human condition.

A second reservation concerns the political use made of it. In the Soviet period especially, the story was taught as a straightforward indictment of the pre-revolutionary order, and a good deal of later commentary pushed back on that flattening. Chekhov was not a political writer in that sense. He distrusted ideology, and his stories typically refuse to assign blame to a single class or system. Reading Vanka only as a sermon about capitalist or tsarist cruelty can miss its subtler subject, which is loneliness and the limits of the means people have for reaching one another. Critics have also pointed out that the story does not really explore the master and mistress as people, which makes it a more limited instrument for social analysis than the label suggests.

A third objection, more of an aesthetic quibble, is that the story is slight. By the time Chekhov wrote his great works, he was exploring psychological ambiguity and the quiet failures of ordinary lives in a way that makes this early piece look simple. It is a story with a single note, however beautifully sustained. Readers who want the layered irony and moral uncertainty of his later work may find Vanka too clean.

It is fair to say that these criticisms are mild. Few serious readers dismiss the story. The usual verdict is that it is an early masterpiece of compression, with a touch more sentiment than the mature Chekhov would permit himself, and that its weaknesses are the weaknesses of a very small form handled very well.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Vanka sits at the meeting point of several traditions. One is the Russian literature of social conscience, running from Gogol's The Overcoat and Turgenev's sketches of peasant life through Dostoevsky's tormented children. Dostoevsky, in particular, wrote harrowing scenes of suffering kids, including a famous story about a boy at a Christmas tree, and his work in this vein is a clear ancestor. But where Dostoevsky works at fever pitch, philosophizing and weeping, Chekhov takes the same material and cools it down to something almost conversational. That choice of temperature is his signature.

Another tradition is the Christmas story of misery and mercy that Dickens, Hans Christian Andersen, and others made popular in the nineteenth century. Andersen's The Little Match Girl is the obvious relative: a poor child, a holiday, a consoling vision at the end. The comparison is revealing. Andersen's child is gathered into a heavenly consolation, and the story is steeped in religious comfort. Chekhov offers a worldly dream instead, the grandfather reading by the stove, and withholds any promise of ultimate justice. His story is therefore a secular, skeptical descendant of that older form.

It also belongs to the broader birth of the modern short story. Chekhov, along with Maupassant and, later, Joyce, Mansfield, and Hemingway, helped move the short story away from tidy plots with twist endings and toward moments of revelation and suggestion. The idea that a story can end without a resolution, trusting an image to carry the meaning, flows through the twentieth century. You can hear Chekhov in the quiet, open-ended endings of Mansfield, in Carver's flat domestic stories, and in much contemporary literary fiction. Vanka is an early, tidy demonstration of the method, which makes it a good place to see how the machinery works before it gets more complicated.

Among works that might be compared, readers sometimes pair it with Chekhov's own Sleepy, a much darker story about a young girl worked to exhaustion who commits a terrible act, and with Dickens's treatment of apprentices and workhouse children in Oliver Twist. Compared with these, Vanka is gentler and shorter, but it shares their insistence that childhood in the lower ranks of society is a form of captivity.

Why read it now? Because the situation it describes has not vanished. Children are still sent away to work, still separated from the people who love them, still writing messages that no system is built to deliver. And because it is an easy, quick, humane demonstration of how much a short story can do with almost nothing. It takes five minutes to read. It teaches you that restraint can be louder than shouting, that a detail like a misaddressed envelope can carry a whole society's failure, and that the people at the bottom of history are not abstractions. They are boys in their shirtsleeves, running to a postbox in the cold.
