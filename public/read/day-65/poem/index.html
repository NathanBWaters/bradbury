A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning

John Donne (1633) • 4 min read

Parting from his wife before a voyage to France, Donne compares their two souls to beaten gold leaf and the twin legs of a geometric drafting compass.

Key Context & Why It Matters

According to Donne’s seventeenth-century biographer Izaak Walton, John Donne composed this poem in 1611 for his pregnant wife Anne More just before departing on a diplomatic mission to France and Germany with Sir Robert Drury, urging her to part without loud tears before it was printed posthumously in 1633. Belonging to the Seventeenth-Century Metaphysical tradition and categorized under Elizabethan & Metaphysical, 'A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning' (1633) unfolds across 36 verbatim lines in 9 stanzas. Celebrated by Samuel Johnson (despite his skepticism of Metaphysical conceits) and T. S. Eliot as the supreme example of 'heterogeneous ideas yoked by violence together,' the compass metaphor remains the most famous conceit in English poetry.

As virtuous men pass mildly away,

And whisper to their souls to go,

Whilst some of their sad friends do say,

“Now his breath goes,” and some say, “No.”

So let us melt, and make no noise,

No tear-floods, nor sigh-tempests move;

’Twere profanation of our joys

To tell the laity our love.

Moving of th’ earth brings harms and fears;

Men reckon what it did, and meant;

But trepidation of the spheres,

Though greater far, is innocent.

Dull sublunary lovers’ love

—Whose soul is sense—cannot admit

Of absence, ’cause it doth remove

The thing which elemented it.

But we by a love so far refined,

That ourselves know not what it is,

Inter-assured of the mind,

Care less eyes, lips and hands to miss.

Our two souls therefore, which are one,

Though I must go, endure not yet

A breach, but an expansion,

Like gold to airy thinness beat.

If they be two, they are two so

As stiff twin compasses are two;

Thy soul, the fix’d foot, makes no show

To move, but doth, if th’ other do.

And though it in the centre sit,

Yet, when the other far doth roam,

It leans, and hearkens after it,

And grows erect, as that comes home.

Such wilt thou be to me, who must,

Like th’ other foot, obliquely run;

Thy firmness makes my circle just,

And makes me end where I begun.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

According to Donne’s seventeenth-century biographer Izaak Walton, John Donne composed this poem in 1611 for his pregnant wife Anne More just before departing on a diplomatic mission to France and Germany with Sir Robert Drury, urging her to part without loud tears before it was printed posthumously in 1633. Within the broader trajectory of Seventeenth-Century Metaphysical, John Donne composed 'A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning' (1633) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"As virtuous men pass mildly away,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, John Donne grounded these 36 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning' lies how spiritualized love transcends physical separation: unlike 'dull sublunary lovers’ love' (whose soul is sense and therefore collapses when bodies part), refined lovers endure distance 'Like gold to airy thinness beat'—an expansion rather than a breach—and like a pair of 'stiff twin compasses' where the fixed foot in the center leans and hearkens after the roaming foot until the circle closes cleanly. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Inter-assured of the mind,"—John Donne develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"And makes me end where I begun."—the central metaphor has transformed.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, John Donne constructs 'A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning' using nine four-line ABAB iambic tetrameter quatrains that move with quiet liturgical dignity from the dying breath of 'virtuous men' through Ptolemaic astronomy ('Moving of th’ earth brings harms and fears,' 'trepidation of the spheres'), metallurgy, and Renaissance geometry. Spanning 36 lines across 9 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how John Donne balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "As virtuous men pass mildly away," and "And makes me end where I begun." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Celebrated by Samuel Johnson (despite his skepticism of Metaphysical conceits) and T. S. Eliot as the supreme example of 'heterogeneous ideas yoked by violence together,' the compass metaphor remains the most famous conceit in English poetry. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning' has remained a touchstone of the Elizabethan & Metaphysical canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to John Donne’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
