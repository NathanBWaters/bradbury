A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning

John Donne (1633) • 4 min read

Parting from his wife before a voyage to France, Donne compares their two souls to beaten gold leaf and the twin legs of a geometric drafting compass.

Key Context & Why It Matters

A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning is a goodbye poem, written by a man who is about to leave the woman he loves and wants her not to cry. John Donne wrote it in the early 1600s, and it was printed in 1633, two years after his death. A biographer named Izaak Walton, writing decades later, said Donne composed it in 1611 for his wife, Anne, before leaving on a trip to the Continent. Whether or not that story is exactly true, the poem fits the situation. Donne had secretly married Anne against her father's wishes, which damaged his career, and the marriage clearly mattered deeply to him. The poem belongs to a group called the metaphysical poets, who used surprising, brainy comparisons to talk about emotion. Its most famous image compares two lovers to the two legs of a drawing compass. The overall meaning is that real love is not broken by distance, because it is a bond of spirit as much as of body.

As virtuous men pass mildly away,

And whisper to their souls to go,

Whilst some of their sad friends do say,

“Now his breath goes,” and some say, “No.”

So let us melt, and make no noise,

No tear-floods, nor sigh-tempests move;

’Twere profanation of our joys

To tell the laity our love.

Moving of th’ earth brings harms and fears;

Men reckon what it did, and meant;

But trepidation of the spheres,

Though greater far, is innocent.

Dull sublunary lovers’ love

—Whose soul is sense—cannot admit

Of absence, ’cause it doth remove

The thing which elemented it.

But we by a love so far refined,

That ourselves know not what it is,

Inter-assured of the mind,

Care less eyes, lips and hands to miss.

Our two souls therefore, which are one,

Though I must go, endure not yet

A breach, but an expansion,

Like gold to airy thinness beat.

If they be two, they are two so

As stiff twin compasses are two;

Thy soul, the fix’d foot, makes no show

To move, but doth, if th’ other do.

And though it in the centre sit,

Yet, when the other far doth roam,

It leans, and hearkens after it,

And grows erect, as that comes home.

Such wilt thou be to me, who must,

Like th’ other foot, obliquely run;

Thy firmness makes my circle just,

And makes me end where I begun.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

A valediction is a farewell, and this one is a very unusual kind. The speaker is about to go away, and instead of saying how much he will miss his beloved, he tells her how to behave. She should not weep. She should not make a scene. He wants the parting to be calm, and he spends nine stanzas explaining why.

The poem opens with a picture of a good person dying. "As virtuous men pass mildly away, / And whisper to their souls to go, / Whilst some of their sad friends do say, / 'Now his breath goes,' and some say, 'No.'" The moment of death is so peaceful that the bystanders cannot tell whether it has happened. That, he says, is how he and his beloved should part: "So let us melt, and make no noise." He does not want floods of tears or storms of sighs. Loud grief would be a "profanation," a kind of disrespect, of their joys, because it would announce their love to "the laity," meaning ordinary outsiders, as if their love were a sacred mystery that only initiates should understand.

Next he makes a clever comparison. An earthquake causes harm and fear, and people measure what it did. But the "trepidation of the spheres," a slow trembling in the heavens that old astronomers believed in, is far greater yet harmless, because it is gentle and far away. Ordinary lovers who part make an earthquake. He and his beloved will make a heavenly tremor, mighty but silent.

Then he draws a line between two kinds of love. "Dull sublunary lovers' love" belongs to the world below the moon, which was thought to be a place of change and decay. Such love is based on the senses, "whose soul is sense," and so it cannot survive absence, since being apart removes the thing that made it. Their love, in contrast, is "so far refined" that they hardly know what it is, and they care less about missing "eyes, lips and hands."

The poem then offers two images. Their souls are like gold hammered into thin leaf, stretching without breaking, "a breach, but an expansion." And finally the famous compass. One leg stays fixed in the center, while the other travels around it, yet the fixed leg leans toward the traveler and straightens as it comes home. The last lines say it all: "Thy firmness makes my circle just, / And makes me end where I begun." Her steadiness is what lets him draw a perfect circle and return. The whole poem is about how staying and going are two halves of one act of love.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Valediction is often held up as the finest example of a poetic conceit, and it deserves the praise. The compass image is the great achievement. It is surprising, because compasses are cold tools of geometry, not romance. Yet the more you look at it, the more perfectly it fits. The two legs are joined at the top and cannot be separated. One stays still while the other moves. The moving one draws a circle, a shape that was a symbol of perfection, and the fixed one leans in as the other wanders and rises up as it returns. Every detail of the object matches a detail of the relationship, and the poem lets the reader discover each match gradually. That is real artistry.

The structure is also admirable. The nine stanzas follow the logic of an argument. They begin with the peaceful death, move through the astronomical comparison, define two kinds of love, and then conclude with two images, gold and the compass. The rhymes are tight and the rhythm is steady, and this calm sound is part of the message. The poem itself does not make noise, which is exactly what it asks of the lovers.

The tone deserves credit too. Donne manages to be tender without being gushy. The speaker is gentle but confident, and the comfort he offers is a strong argument, not a sentimental reassurance. It treats the beloved as an intelligent person who can follow a case.

But there are weaknesses. The first is that the poem is quite demanding. The astronomical lines about earthquakes and the trembling of the spheres are hard for modern readers, who do not share the old picture of the heavens. A reader without notes may stumble in the third stanza and lose heart.

The second is a certain note of superiority. The speaker looks down on ordinary lovers, calling them dull, and says that he and his beloved are special because their love is of the mind. This is flattering to them and a bit smug about everyone else. It can feel as though he is persuading himself as much as her.

The third is that the poem, for all its tenderness, is oddly lopsided. He gives the instructions, and she is cast as the "fixed foot," the one who stays still while he travels. A modern reader may notice that the supposedly equal partnership gives him the adventure and her the waiting. Poets and critics have pointed out that this is a very traditional division.

The fourth is the risk that the ingenuity may overwhelm the feeling. Some readers admire the compass so much that they forget the pain of parting. Still, most feel that the quiet emotion beneath the argument is strong enough, and that the poem's restraint is its way of showing love.

III. The Impact It Had

The influence of A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning comes from a single image that has become a landmark of English poetry. The compass conceit has been discussed, taught and quoted so often that it is practically a shorthand for the whole idea of the clever, intellectual love poem.

The poem first circulated in manuscript and was then printed in 1633 in Poems, by J. D., two years after Donne's death. The story that he wrote it for his wife before going abroad comes from Izaak Walton's Life of Donne, first published in 1640, long after the event. Biographers treat the tale with some caution, since Walton wrote decades later and liked a good story, but it has shaped how readers imagine the poem, as a gift given at a doorway.

Within the seventeenth century, the poem helped define the metaphysical style that influenced George Herbert, Andrew Marvell, Henry Vaughan, and others. Those poets borrowed the habit of taking a scientific or technical object and turning it into an emotional figure. Marvell's love poems, in particular, use geometry and astronomy in a way that recalls Donne.

Samuel Johnson, who disliked much of the metaphysical manner, nevertheless quoted this poem in his writing on the metaphysical poets, which tells us that even a hostile critic found it hard to ignore. The compass image became the standard example people argued about when they debated whether such comparisons were clever or forced.

The twentieth-century revival of Donne gave the poem new life. Herbert Grierson's anthology of metaphysical poetry in 1921 and T. S. Eliot's famous essay about those poets led modern readers to see Donne as a poet who could think and feel at once. The Valediction became a fixture in university courses. Students often meet it as the example of a conceit, and its compass is the image most often chosen to illustrate the term.

The poem has also had a life outside the classroom. Its opening lines and its compass have been quoted at partings and in letters between people separated by travel or war. Lines from it appear in epigraphs, on cards, and in novels about long distance and loyalty. The idea of the two legs of a compass has been taken up in popular writing on relationships, where it stands for a partnership in which one partner anchors while the other explores.

In short, the poem's impact is that of a perfect example. It taught generations how a comparison can be both intellectual and moving, and it gave English a lasting picture for the way love can stretch across a distance without breaking.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about this poem? Above all, they love the compass. It is the sort of image that makes people stop and say, oh, that is perfect. Even people who dislike difficult poetry often admire it, because once the image is explained it feels both obvious and astonishing. The fixed foot leaning toward its traveling partner is a tiny picture that holds a whole relationship.

They love its tenderness. For all its learning, this is a poem written by someone who does not want to hurt the person he is leaving. The very act of explaining, of taking time to say why they need not grieve, is an act of care. Many readers have felt the poem as a hand held out at a hard moment, and it is frequently turned to by people who have had to part from someone they love, for work, for war, or for illness.

Readers also admire the way the poem teaches a different idea of love. It suggests that real closeness is not about being in the same room, and that a bond can survive absence because it lives in the mind and spirit. That is a hopeful thought, and many people find it true in their own long friendships or long-distance relationships. The gold image, where souls are stretched thin without breaking, is another favorite, because gold leaf is real and amazingly thin, and the comparison makes the stretch feel physical.

The calm voice of the poem is often praised. In a world where partings are usually described as tearful and dramatic, here is a speaker who values quiet. The opening picture of a good man dying so gently that no one can tell the moment he goes is strangely beautiful, and it sets the mood for everything that follows.

Readers who care about craft admire how the poem practices what it preaches. Its rhythm is gentle, its rhyme is regular, and nowhere does it break into shouting. The poem itself makes no noise. Poets have taken this as a lesson in matching form to meaning.

Finally, people love that it feels like a true gift. Whatever the real circumstances, the poem is built as something a person might hand to the one they love before leaving, a small, folded argument against sorrow. That personal quality, together with its brains, explains why it has been loved for nearly four hundred years.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Like most great old poems, A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning has met objections, and they are worth knowing.

The oldest is the general complaint about the metaphysical style. Samuel Johnson, writing in the eighteenth century, thought that Donne and his followers forced unlike things together and showed off their learning at the expense of feeling. He found their comparisons surprising but sometimes more clever than convincing. John Dryden had earlier said Donne mixed philosophy into love poetry where he should have been speaking more simply to women. In this view, comparing lovers to a compass is more of a puzzle than a feeling. Many later critics have answered that the compass is moving precisely because it is so carefully worked out, but the objection has never gone away.

A second line of criticism targets the poem's attitude to gender. The woman is the "fixed foot" who stays at home and waits, while the man is the one who travels, "obliquely runs," and returns. Critics have noted that this assigns her the passive role. She is the center, but only so that he can draw his circle. A number of feminist readers have pointed out that the poem's praise of her firmness is also an instruction to remain in place. Others reply that the image gives her a quiet power, since the traveler cannot draw a true circle without her, and that the poem is more mutual than it seems. The disagreement shows how rich the image is.

A third complaint concerns the speaker's snobbery. The line about "dull sublunary lovers" and the talk of "the laity" imply that most people's love is physical and shallow, while theirs is spiritual. Some find this superior tone off-putting, and some have said that the poem undervalues the body, which Donne celebrated elsewhere. Indeed, in other poems he is frank about physical love, and the contrast has led critics to wonder whether this poem is more of a rhetorical performance than a fully honest statement.

There is also the biographical question. The tale that Donne wrote it for Anne in 1611 comes from Walton, who was writing long after, and scholars have doubted whether the story is accurate. Some think the poem may have been written for a different occasion, or no particular one. Readers who rely on the legend should hold it loosely.

Finally, there are readers who simply find the poem hard. The old astronomy and the dense argument can feel like homework. That is not a flaw in the poem so much as a barrier, but it is one of the real reasons some people never come to love it.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning belongs to the metaphysical poetry of the seventeenth century, the school of English poets that Donne helped found. These poets believed that the most intense feelings could be explored through the most unexpected images, drawn from science, trade, law and geography. A compass, a map, a flea, a drop of blood, a coin, all became material for love poems. The Valediction is perhaps the most polished example of that method.

It has a clear ancestry. The valediction itself, a poem of farewell, goes back to classical models, and the tradition of the love poem as a persuasive speech goes back to the Roman poets and the Italian Renaissance. The idea that love joins souls, and that a true lover is united with the beloved in spirit, comes from Platonic philosophy, which was widely read in the Renaissance. Donne takes these inherited ideas and gives them a fresh, concrete form.

It also reflects the science of its day. The talk of the heavenly spheres and their trembling draws on the old Ptolemaic picture of the universe, in which the stars and planets were carried on nested crystal shells. The phrase "sublunary" refers to the supposed boundary at the moon, below which everything was subject to change. By building his poem from the language of astronomy and geometry, Donne shows how the new learning of his age could be part of the language of feeling.

Its descendants are plentiful. Andrew Marvell and George Herbert carried on the style. After a long eclipse, the metaphysical manner returned in the twentieth century in the work of T. S. Eliot and other modern poets who prized intelligence and compression. Elsewhere in poetry, any love poem that borrows an object from the workshop or the laboratory to describe a feeling owes something to Donne.

It makes a good companion to Shakespeare's Sonnet 116, which also imagines love as steady in the face of separation, using the image of a fixed star. The difference is in tone. Shakespeare declares and defines, while Donne argues and comforts, speaking directly to one person at a moment of parting. Together they show two great but different ways of imagining faithful love.

Why read it today? Because the situation is timeless. People still part, for work, for study, for war, for illness, and they still wonder how to carry love across the gap. This poem offers a calm, intelligent answer, wrapped in one of the most memorable images in the language. It rewards slow reading, and it is short enough to carry in your head, which may be what its speaker would have wanted.
