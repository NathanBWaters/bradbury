San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • Suspension & Cantilever Bridge Engineering & Seismic Design

The San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge: Pier W-4's Caisson, 1989 Loma Prieta, and the 2013 Eastern Span

San Francisco — How Charles Purcell anchored twin suspension spans to a submerged concrete island in 1936, bored the world's widest tunnel through Yerba Buena Island, and replaced the eastern cantilever with a single-tower seismic span (New Deal Mega-Engineering (1933–1936), October 17, 1989 Quake & 2013 Rebuild) • 9 min read

“The Golden Gate Bridge is a single dramatic leap across a deep narrow strait; the Bay Bridge was five impossible bridges linked end to end across four and a half miles of deep mud and rock.” — Chief Engineer Charles H. Purcell (1936)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stretching 4.5 miles across the open waters of San Francisco Bay from Rincon Hill to Yerba Buena Island and Oakland, the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge was hailed upon its November 12, 1936 opening (six months before the Golden Gate Bridge) as the greatest structural work of human history. Because no single suspension span could cross the two-mile West Bay channel, Chief Engineer Charles H. Purcell and Bridge Engineer Charles E. Andrew invented a bold solution: sinking the world's largest fifty-five-cell open-dredged compressed-air caisson (Pier W-4) 218 feet down to bedrock in the middle of the bay so twin suspension bridges could meet nose-to-nose, boring the world's largest-bore double-deck tunnel through Yerba Buena Island, and—after the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake dropped an eastern deck section—erecting the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension Span.

Long before serious engineering began on the Bay Bridge in the late 1920s, San Francisco's beloved eccentric Emperor Joshua Abraham Norton issued an Imperial Proclamation in three Oakland and San Francisco newspapers on August 18, 1869, commanding that a suspension bridge be built immediately from Oakland Point via Goat Island (Yerba Buena Island) to Telegraph Hill—a prophetic route that engineers fulfilled sixty-seven years later. Why was bridging the 4.5-mile expanse between San Francisco and Oakland considered even more daunting by civil engineers in 1930 than spanning the one-mile Golden Gate? Because the West Bay crossing between Rincon Hill and Yerba Buena Island was nearly two miles wide (10,450 feet) with water over 100 feet deep and bedrock buried up to 240 feet below the tide—far too wide for any single suspension span then conceivable.

Appointed Chief Engineer of the California Toll Bridge Authority in 1931 and backed by a $61.4 million Reconstruction Finance Corporation loan signed by President Herbert Hoover and expanded under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, forty-eight-year-old Nebraska-born engineer Charles H. Purcell (assisted by Charles E. Andrew, Glenn B. Woodruff, and San Francisco consulting architect Timothy L. Pflueger) devised an unprecedented structural layout. Instead of one impossible two-mile suspension span across the West Bay, Purcell placed two full-scale suspension bridges (each with a 2,310-foot main span) end to end between Rincon Hill and Yerba Buena Island—anchoring their meeting inner cables to a man-made concrete monolith rising out of the middle of the shipping channel: Pier W-4, the Center Anchorage.

How do you build a concrete island the size of a twenty-story skyscraper (92 feet by 197 feet) in the middle of a swirling seven-knot bay current where solid Franciscan bedrock lies 218 feet below the water surface—deeper than any human diver in a pressurized caisson could survive without fatal 'bends'? Foundation genius Daniel E. Moran invented the domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson: a floating steel-and-timber honeycomb containing fifty-five vertical fifteen-foot-diameter steel cylinders capped at the top with airtight steel domes. Towed out to the center of the West Bay in 1933, the giant Pier W-4 caisson was sunk inch by inch by pumping compressed air into the fifty-five capped cylinders to keep it floating steady while concrete was poured around the outside—and then uncapping a few cylinders at a time so giant clamshell dredge buckets could drop down through the open pipes and scoop mud from beneath the cutting edge until the entire monolith settled onto solid bedrock 218 feet down.

Reaching Yerba Buena Island at the midpoint of the bay, Purcell's crews excavated the largest-bore tunnel in the world (76 feet wide and 58 feet high) straight through 540 feet of fractured sandstone and shale—lining the roof with steel ribs before excavating the core rock so the fractured hilltop never caved in, and accommodating six lanes of automobiles on the upper deck and three truck lanes plus two electric Key System and Southern Pacific commuter rail tracks on the lower deck. East of Yerba Buena Island, because deep bedrock plunged out of reach to over 300 feet below the mudline, Purcell supported the 1936 Eastern Span on deeply driven Douglas fir timber piles and shallow caissons, erecting a 1,400-foot steel cantilever span connected to five truss spans reaching the Oakland mole.

When Governor Frank Merriam cut the golden chain with an acetylene blowtorch on November 12, 1936, while President Roosevelt pressed a telegraph key in Washington, the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge opened at 77 million—six months ahead of schedule and6 million under budget, though at the tragic cost of twenty-eight bridge workers' lives. Twenty-two years later, in April 1958, in a decision urban planners have lamented ever since, the electric Key System trains on the lower deck were ripped out and replaced by five one-way lanes of automobile traffic on each deck (westbound on top, eastbound below).

Then, at 5:04 p.m. on Tuesday, October 17, 1989, just minutes before the first pitch of Game 3 of the Oakland A's–San Francisco Giants World Series, the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta earthquake struck sixty miles south: as seismic shear waves shook the deep soft mud beneath the Eastern Span, the eastern truss shifted seven inches further than its supporting seat on Pier E-9, shearing twenty-eight one-inch steel bolts and dropping a fifty-foot upper-deck section onto the lower deck. Although the broken Pier E-9 deck section was repaired and reopened within one month on November 18, 1989, Caltrans seismic engineers realized a sobering truth: while Purcell's West Bay twin suspension spans could be seismically retrofitted with viscous fluid dampers and replaceable ductile shear links because they stood on solid bedrock, the 1936 Eastern Cantilever Span sitting on deep bay mud could not survive a future magnitude 7.5 earthquake on the nearby Hayward Fault.

Between 2002 and 2013, engineers Marwan Nader (of T. Y. Lin International, based in San Francisco) and architects Donald MacDonald and IraVerne designed and built a $6.5 billion replacement Eastern Span: the world's longest Self-Anchored Suspension (SAS) Bridge. Look at how the 2013 Eastern Span works: instead of anchoring its single 0.85-mile-long main cable into massive concrete blocks on shore (which soft bay mud could not hold against horizontal tension), the continuous steel cable loops around the western and eastern ends of the twin roadway decks themselves and passes over the top of a single 525-foot white steel tower built of four vertical legs linked by replaceable seismic 'shear link' beams designed to absorb earthquake energy like structural fuses. Meanwhile, across the West Spans every night since artist Leo Villareal's The Bay Lights first illuminated 25,000 white LEDs across the vertical suspender ropes in March 2013, Purcell's 1936 twin suspension spans and Nader's 2013 single-tower span shine together across four and a half miles of living tide.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Daniel Moran's Domed-Cylinder Caisson at Pier W-4 (1933–1936)

Why could engineers not use traditional pneumatic caissons (where human 'sandhogs' dig inside a pressurized air chamber at the bottom of a pier) to sink the Bay Bridge's Center Anchorage (Pier W-4) 218 feet down to bedrock? Because water pressure below 120 feet exceeds human physiological limits, causing lethal nitrogen narcosis and decompression sickness. Daniel Moran solved this by clustering fifty-five vertical steel cylinders capped with removable air domes—using compressed air only for buoyancy control during floating and sinking, while mechanical clamshell buckets excavated the mud through open water.

2. How a Self-Anchored Suspension Bridge and 'Shear Links' Survive a Hayward Quake

What makes the 2013 Eastern Span a Self-Anchored Suspension (SAS) bridge, and how does its 525-foot single tower resist a major Hayward Fault rupture? In a classical suspension bridge, the main cables pull against giant concrete shore anchorages; in the 2013 SAS span, the single main cable is anchored directly into the steel box-girder roadway decks, placing the decks in horizontal compression. Meanwhile, the single tower actually consists of four separate vertical steel shafts connected by horizontal I-beam shear links that are engineered to yield plastically during a violent earthquake—protecting the main tower shafts and allowing engineers to unbolt and replace the damaged fuses afterward.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three bridge milestones in view: first, Pier W-4, the 218-foot-deep concrete Center Anchorage mid-way between Rincon Hill and Yerba Buena Island that joins the two 1936 suspension bridges; second, the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta failure at Pier E-9, which catalyzed the modern seismic retrofit of all California bridges; and third, the Alexander Zuckermann Bike/Pedestrian Path on the 2013 Eastern Span.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Start along the Embarcadero at Rincon Park near Folsom and Spear streets to stand directly beneath the soaring western suspension cables of the 1936 Bay Bridge and inspect the massive concrete Western Anchorage at Rincon Hill, returning after dark to watch The Bay Lights shimmer across the cables. Then take the 25-Treasure Island Muni bus through the Yerba Buena Tunnel and walk or bike out onto the Bay Bridge Trail on the 2013 Eastern Span up to the 525-foot Self-Anchored Suspension tower.
