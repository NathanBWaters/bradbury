The San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge: Pier W-4's Caisson, 1989 Loma Prieta, and the 2013 Eastern Span

San Francisco — How Charles Purcell anchored twin suspension spans to a submerged concrete island in 1936, bored the world's widest tunnel through Yerba Buena Island, and replaced the eastern cantilever with a single-tower seismic span (New Deal Mega-Engineering (1933–1936), October 17, 1989 Quake & 2013 Rebuild) • 20 min read

“The Golden Gate Bridge is a single dramatic leap across a deep narrow strait; the Bay Bridge was five impossible bridges linked end to end across four and a half miles of deep mud and rock.” — Chief Engineer Charles H. Purcell (1936)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stretching 4.5 miles across the open waters of San Francisco Bay from Rincon Hill to Yerba Buena Island and Oakland (37.7983° N, 122.3778° W), the **San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge** was hailed upon its **November 12, 1936** opening (six months *before* the Golden Gate Bridge) as the greatest structural work of human history. Because no single suspension span could cross the two-mile West Bay channel, Chief Engineer **Charles H. Purcell** and Bridge Engineer **Charles E. Andrew** invented a bold solution: sinking the world's largest fifty-five-cell open-dredged compressed-air caisson (**Pier W-4**) 218 feet down to bedrock in the middle of the bay so **twin suspension bridges** could meet nose-to-nose, boring the world's largest-bore double-deck tunnel through **Yerba Buena Island**, and—after the **October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake** dropped an eastern deck section—erecting the **2013 Self-Anchored Suspension Span**.

Standing today at the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island in the heart of Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island (37.7983° N, 122.3778° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of New Deal Mega-Engineering (1933–1936), October 17, 1989 Quake & 2013 Rebuild. Long before serious engineering began on the Bay Bridge in the late 1920s, San Francisco's beloved eccentric **Emperor Joshua Abraham Norton** issued an Imperial Proclamation in three Oakland and San Francisco newspapers on **August 18, 1869**, commanding that a suspension bridge be built immediately from Oakland Point via Goat Island (Yerba Buena Island) to Telegraph Hill—a prophetic route that engineers fulfilled sixty-seven years later. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how Chief Engineer Charles H. Purcell (1883–1951), caisson designer Daniel E. Moran, architect Timothy L. Pflueger, and 2013 span designers Marwan Nader (T.Y. Lin) and Donald MacDonald and a generation of San Franciscans confronted bridging two miles of deep West Bay water with twin end-to-end suspension spans in 1936 and replacing the vulnerable 1936 eastern cantilever with a ductile seismic span after the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island. Why was bridging the 4.5-mile expanse between San Francisco and Oakland considered even more daunting by civil engineers in 1930 than spanning the one-mile Golden Gate? Because the West Bay crossing between Rincon Hill and Yerba Buena Island was nearly two miles wide (10,450 feet) with water over 100 feet deep and bedrock buried up to 240 feet below the tide—far too wide for any single suspension span then conceivable. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during July 9, 1933 (groundbreaking), November 12, 1936 (opening), October 17, 1989 (Loma Prieta deck failure at Pier E-9), and September 2, 2013 (new Eastern Span opens). Appointed Chief Engineer of the California Toll Bridge Authority in 1931 and backed by a $61.4 million Reconstruction Finance Corporation loan signed by President Herbert Hoover and expanded under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, forty-eight-year-old Nebraska-born engineer **Charles H. Purcell** (assisted by Charles E. Andrew, Glenn B. Woodruff, and San Francisco consulting architect **Timothy L. Pflueger**) devised an unprecedented structural layout. Contemporary observers who walked past the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood Chief Engineer Charles H. Purcell (1883–1951), caisson designer Daniel E. Moran, architect Timothy L. Pflueger, and 2013 span designers Marwan Nader (T.Y. Lin) and Donald MacDonald, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson foundations, tandem suspension cable anchorage, and shear-link self-anchored suspension seismic engineering proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Instead of one impossible two-mile suspension span across the West Bay, Purcell placed **two full-scale suspension bridges** (each with a 2,310-foot main span) end to end between Rincon Hill and Yerba Buena Island—anchoring their meeting inner cables to a man-made concrete monolith rising out of the middle of the shipping channel: **Pier W-4**, the Center Anchorage. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson foundations, tandem suspension cable anchorage, and shear-link self-anchored suspension seismic engineering imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span. How do you build a concrete island the size of a twenty-story skyscraper (92 feet by 197 feet) in the middle of a swirling seven-knot bay current where solid Franciscan bedrock lies 218 feet below the water surface—deeper than any human diver in a pressurized caisson could survive without fatal 'bends'? Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through July 9, 1933 (groundbreaking), November 12, 1936 (opening), October 17, 1989 (Loma Prieta deck failure at Pier E-9), and September 2, 2013 (new Eastern Span opens), the surrounding streets of Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. Foundation genius **Daniel E. Moran** invented the **domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson**: a floating steel-and-timber honeycomb containing **fifty-five vertical fifteen-foot-diameter steel cylinders** capped at the top with airtight steel domes. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Towed out to the center of the West Bay in 1933, the giant Pier W-4 caisson was sunk inch by inch by pumping compressed air into the fifty-five capped cylinders to keep it floating steady while concrete was poured around the outside—and then uncapping a few cylinders at a time so giant clamshell dredge buckets could drop down through the open pipes and scoop mud from beneath the cutting edge until the entire monolith settled onto solid bedrock 218 feet down. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span toward private speculation—forcing Chief Engineer Charles H. A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island. Reaching Yerba Buena Island at the midpoint of the bay, Purcell's crews excavated the **largest-bore tunnel in the world** (76 feet wide and 58 feet high) straight through 540 feet of fractured sandstone and shale—lining the roof with steel ribs *before* excavating the core rock so the fractured hilltop never caved in, and accommodating six lanes of automobiles on the upper deck and three truck lanes plus two electric **Key System** and Southern Pacific commuter rail tracks on the lower deck. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson foundations, tandem suspension cable anchorage, and shear-link self-anchored suspension seismic engineering and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span, the day-to-day craft of domed-cylinder open-dredged caisson foundations, tandem suspension cable anchorage, and shear-link self-anchored suspension seismic engineering produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island. East of Yerba Buena Island, because deep bedrock plunged out of reach to over 300 feet below the mudline, Purcell supported the 1936 Eastern Span on deeply driven Douglas fir timber piles and shallow caissons, erecting a 1,400-foot steel **cantilever span** connected to five truss spans reaching the Oakland mole. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island. When Governor Frank Merriam cut the golden chain with an acetylene blowtorch on **November 12, 1936**, while President Roosevelt pressed a telegraph key in Washington, the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge opened at $77 million—six months ahead of schedule and $6 million under budget, though at the tragic cost of twenty-eight bridge workers' lives. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in July 9, 1933 (groundbreaking), November 12, 1936 (opening), October 17, 1989 (Loma Prieta deck failure at Pier E-9), and September 2, 2013 (new Eastern Span opens), the entire city turned its eyes toward the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span in Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island. Twenty-two years later, in April 1958, in a decision urban planners have lamented ever since, the electric Key System trains on the lower deck were ripped out and replaced by five one-way lanes of automobile traffic on each deck (westbound on top, eastbound below). What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed July 9, 1933 (groundbreaking), November 12, 1936 (opening), October 17, 1989 (Loma Prieta deck failure at Pier E-9), and September 2, 2013 (new Eastern Span opens), shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span. Then, at **5:04 p.m. on Tuesday, October 17, 1989**, just minutes before the first pitch of Game 3 of the Oakland A's–San Francisco Giants World Series, the magnitude 6.9 **Loma Prieta earthquake** struck sixty miles south: as seismic shear waves shook the deep soft mud beneath the Eastern Span, the eastern truss shifted seven inches further than its supporting seat on **Pier E-9**, shearing twenty-eight one-inch steel bolts and dropping a fifty-foot upper-deck section onto the lower deck. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at the Rincon Hill Western Anchorage at Beale and Bryant streets, Pier W-4 in mid-channel, and Yerba Buena Island adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Although the broken Pier E-9 deck section was repaired and reopened within one month on November 18, 1989, Caltrans seismic engineers realized a sobering truth: while Purcell's West Bay twin suspension spans could be seismically retrofitted with viscous fluid dampers and replaceable ductile shear links because they stood on solid bedrock, the 1936 Eastern Cantilever Span sitting on deep bay mud could not survive a future magnitude 7.5 earthquake on the nearby **Hayward Fault**. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, historians and engineers discovered anew how deeply the Bay Bridge West Spans (Pier W-4 Center Anchorage), the Yerba Buena Tunnel, and the 2013 Self-Anchored Suspension East Span was tuned to the peninsula.

Through painstaking archival recovery and public stewardship in Rincon Hill, South Beach, and Yerba Buena Island, the site was safeguarded so future generations could experience it firsthand.

Look at how the 2013 Eastern Span works: instead of anchoring its single 0.85-mile-long main cable into massive concrete blocks on shore (which soft bay mud could not hold against horizontal tension), the continuous steel cable loops *around* the western and eastern ends of the twin roadway decks themselves and passes over the top of a single **525-foot white steel tower** built of four vertical legs linked by replaceable **seismic 'shear link' beams** designed to absorb earthquake energy like structural fuses. Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Daniel Moran's Domed-Cylinder Caisson at Pier W-4 (1933–1936)

Why could engineers not use traditional pneumatic caissons (where human 'sandhogs' dig inside a pressurized air chamber at the bottom of a pier) to sink the Bay Bridge's Center Anchorage (Pier W-4) 218 feet down to bedrock? Because water pressure below 120 feet exceeds human physiological limits, causing lethal nitrogen narcosis and decompression sickness. Daniel Moran solved this by clustering fifty-five vertical steel cylinders capped with removable air domes—using compressed air only for buoyancy control during floating and sinking, while mechanical clamshell buckets excavated the mud through open water. 2. How a Self-Anchored Suspension Bridge and 'Shear Links' Survive a Hayward Quake

What makes the 2013 Eastern Span a **Self-Anchored Suspension (SAS)** bridge, and how does its 525-foot single tower resist a major Hayward Fault rupture? In a classical suspension bridge, the main cables pull against giant concrete shore anchorages; in the 2013 SAS span, the single main cable is anchored directly into the steel box-girder roadway decks, placing the decks in horizontal compression. Meanwhile, the single tower actually consists of four separate vertical steel shafts connected by horizontal I-beam **shear links** that are engineered to yield plastically during a violent earthquake—protecting the main tower shafts and allowing engineers to unbolt and replace the damaged fuses afterward.
