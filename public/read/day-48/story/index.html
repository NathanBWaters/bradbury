The Stolen Bacillus

H. G. Wells (1894) • 12 min read

“This again,” said the Bacteriologist, slipping a glass slide under the microscope, “is well⁠—a preparation of the Bacillus of cholera⁠—the cholera germ.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

H. G. Wells published "The Stolen Bacillus" in June 1894 in a London magazine called the Pall Mall Budget, and it became the title story of his first book of short stories the following year. At that point Wells was a young science teacher and freelance journalist, not yet famous. The Time Machine was still a year away. Europe was also nervous. Anarchists had thrown bombs in Paris and elsewhere, and newspapers were full of panic about terrorists. Meanwhile, the germ theory of disease, which showed that tiny living things cause illnesses like cholera, was new enough to feel both miraculous and frightening. Wells puts those two fears together in a comedy. A would-be terrorist visits a scientist's laboratory and steals what he believes is a tube of deadly disease. The result is a chase through London that is funnier than it sounds. Notice how the joke depends on what each man assumes about the other.

“This again,” said the Bacteriologist, slipping a glass slide under the microscope, “is well⁠—a preparation of the Bacillus of cholera⁠—the cholera germ.”

The pale-faced man peered down the microscope. He was evidently not accustomed to that kind of thing, and held a limp white hand over his disengaged eye. “I see very little,” he said.

“Touch this screw,” said the Bacteriologist; “perhaps the microscope is out of focus for you. Eyes vary so much. Just the fraction of a turn this way or that.”

“Ah! now I see,” said the visitor. “Not so very much to see after all. Little streaks and shreds of pink. And yet those little particles, those mere atomies, might multiply and devastate a city! Wonderful!”

He stood up, and releasing the glass slip from the microscope, held it in his hand towards the window. “Scarcely visible,” he said, scrutinising the preparation. He hesitated. “Are these⁠—alive? Are they dangerous now?”

“Those have been stained and killed,” said the Bacteriologist. “I wish, for my own part, we could kill and stain every one of them in the universe.”

“I suppose,” the pale man said, with a slight smile, “that you scarcely care to have such things about you in the living⁠—in the active state?”

“On the contrary, we are obliged to,” said the Bacteriologist. “Here, for instance⁠—” He walked across the room and took up one of several sealed tubes. “Here is the living thing. This is a cultivation of the actual living disease bacteria.” He hesitated. “Bottled cholera, so to speak.”

A slight gleam of satisfaction appeared momentarily in the face of the pale man. “It’s a deadly thing to have in your possession,” he said, devouring the little tube with his eyes. The Bacteriologist watched the morbid pleasure in his visitor’s expression. This man, who had visited him that afternoon with a note of introduction from an old friend, interested him from the very contrast of their dispositions. The lank black hair and deep grey eyes, the haggard expression and nervous manner, the fitful yet keen interest of his visitor were a novel change from the phlegmatic deliberations of the ordinary scientific worker with whom the Bacteriologist chiefly associated. It was perhaps natural, with a hearer evidently so impressionable to the lethal nature of; his topic, to take the most effective aspect of the matter.

He held the tube in his hand thoughtfully. “Yes, here is the pestilence imprisoned. Only break such a little tube as this into a supply of drinking-water, say to these minute particles of life that one must needs stain and examine with the highest powers of the microscope even to see, and that one can neither smell nor taste⁠—say to them, ‘Go forth, increase and multiply, and replenish the cisterns,’ and death⁠—mysterious, untraceable death, death swift and terrible, death full of pain and indignity⁠—would be released upon this city, and go hither and thither seeking his victims. Here he would take the husband from the wife, here the child from its mother, here the statesman from his duty, and here the toiler from his trouble. He would follow the water-mains, creeping along streets, picking out and punishing a house here and a house there where they did not boil their drinking-water, creeping into the wells of the mineral water makers, getting washed into salad, and lying dormant in ices. He would wait ready to be drunk in the horse-troughs, and by unwary children in the public fountains. He would soak into the soil, to reappear in springs and wells at a thousand unexpected places. Once start him at the water supply, and before we could ring him in, and catch him again, he would have decimated the metropolis.”

He stopped abruptly. He had been told rhetoric was his weakness.

“But he is quite safe here, you know⁠—quite safe.”

The pale-faced man nodded. His eyes shone. He cleared his throat. “These Anarchist⁠—rascals,” said he, “are fools, blind fools⁠—to use bombs when this kind of thing is attainable. I think⁠—”

A gentle rap, a mere light touch of the fingernails, was heard at the door. The Bacteriologist opened it. “Just a minute, dear,” whispered his wife.

When he reentered the laboratory his visitor was looking at his watch. “I had no idea I had wasted an hour of your time,” he said. “Twelve minutes to four. I ought to have left here by half-past three. But your things were really too interesting. No, positively I cannot stop a moment longer. I have an engagement at four.”

He passed out of the room reiterating his thanks, and the Bacteriologist accompanied him to the door, and then returned thoughtfully along the passage to his laboratory. He was musing on the ethnology of his visitor. Certainly the man was not a Teutonic type nor a common Latin one. “A morbid product, anyhow, I am afraid,” said the Bacteriologist to himself. “How he gloated over those cultivations of disease germs!” A disturbing thought struck him. He turned to the bench by the vapour bath, and then very quickly to his writing-table. Then he felt hastily in his pockets and then rushed to the door. “I may have put it down on the hall table,” he said.

“Minnie!” he shouted hoarsely in the hall.

“Yes, dear,” came a remote voice.

“Had I anything in my hand when I spoke to you, dear, just now?”

Pause.

“Nothing, dear, because I remember⁠—”

“Blue ruin!” cried the Bacteriologist, and incontinently ran to the front door and down the steps of his house to the street.

Minnie, hearing the door slam violently, ran in alarm to the window. Down the street a slender man was getting into a cab. The Bacteriologist, hatless, and in his carpet slippers, was running and gesticulating wildly towards this group. One slipper came off, but he did not wait for it. “He has gone mad!” said Minnie; “it’s that horrid science of his”; and, opening the window, would have called after him. The slender man, suddenly glancing round, seemed struck with the same idea of mental disorder. He pointed hastily to the Bacteriologist, said something to the cabman, the apron of the cab slammed, the whip swished, the horse’s feet clattered, and in a moment cab and Bacteriologist hotly in pursuit, had receded up the vista of the roadway and disappeared round the corner.

Minnie remained straining out of the window for a minute. Then she drew her head back into the room again. She was dumbfounded. “Of course he is eccentric,” she meditated. “But running about London⁠—in the height of the season, too⁠—in his socks!” A happy thought struck her. She hastily put her bonnet on, seized his shoes, went into the hall, took down his hat and light overcoat from the pegs, emerged upon the doorstep, and hailed a cab that opportunely crawled by. “Drive me up the road and round Havelock Crescent, and see if we can find a gentleman running about in a velveteen coat and no hat.”

“Velveteen coat, ma’am, and no ’at. Very good, ma’am.” And the cabman whipped up at once in the most matter-of-fact way, as if he drove to this address every day in his life.

Some few minutes later the little group of cabmen and loafers that collects round the cabman’s shelter at Haverstock Hill were startled by the passing of a cab with a ginger-coloured screw of a horse, driven furiously.

They were silent as it went by, and then as it receded⁠—“That’s ’Arry ’Icks. Wot’s he got?” said the stout gentleman known as Old Tootles.

“He’s a-using his whip, he is, to rights,” said the ostler boy.

“Hullo!” said poor old Tommy Byles; “here’s another bloomin’ loonatic. Blowed if there ain’t.”

“It’s old George,” said Old Tootles, “and he’s drivin’ a loonatic, as you say. Ain’t he a-clawin’ out of the keb? Wonder if he’s after ’Arry ’Icks?”

The group round the cabman’s shelter became animated. Chorus: “Go it, George!” “It’s a race.” “You’ll ketch ’em!” “Whip up!”

“She’s a goer, she is!” said the ostler boy.

“Strike me giddy!” cried Old Tootles. “Here! I’m a-goin’ to begin in a minute. Here’s another comin’. If all the cabs in Hampstead ain’t gone mad this morning!”

“It’s a fieldmale this time,” said the ostler boy.

“She’s a-followin’ him,” said Old Tootles. “Usually the other way about.”

“What’s she got in her ’and?”

“Looks like a ’igh ’at.”

“What a bloomin’ lark it is! Three to one on old George,” said the ostler boy. “Nexst!”

Minnie went by in a perfect roar of applause. She did not like it, but she felt that she was doing her duty, and whirled on down Haverstock Hill and Camden Town High Street with her eyes ever intent on the animated back view of old George, who was driving her vagrant husband so incomprehensibly away from her.

The man in the foremost cab sat crouched in the corner, his arms tightly folded, and the little tube that contained such vast possibilities of destruction gripped in his hand. His mood was a singular mixture of fear and exultation. Chiefly he was afraid of being caught before he could accomplish his purpose, but behind this was a vaguer but larger fear of the awfulness of his crime. But his exultation far exceeded his fear. No Anarchist before him had ever approached this conception of his. Ravachol, Vaillant, all those distinguished persons whose fame he had envied dwindled into insignificance beside him. He had only to make sure of the water supply, and break the little tube into a reservoir. How brilliantly he had planned it, forged the letter of introduction and got into the laboratory, and how brilliantly he had seized his opportunity! The world should hear of him at last. All those people who had sneered at him, neglected him, preferred other people to him, found his company undesirable, should consider him at last. Death, death, death! They had always treated him as a man of no importance. All the world had been in a conspiracy to keep him under. He would teach them yet what it is to isolate a man. What was this familiar street? Great Saint Andrew’s Street, of course! How fared the chase? He craned out of the cab. The Bacteriologist was scarcely fifty yards behind. That was bad. He would be caught and stopped yet. He felt in his pocket for money, and found half a sovereign. This he thrust up through the trap in the top of the cab into the man’s face. “More,” he shouted, “if only we get away.”

The money was snatched out of his hand. “Right you are,” said the cabman, and the trap slammed, and the lash lay along the glistening side of the horse. The cab swayed, and the Anarchist, half-standing under the trap, put the hand containing the little glass tube upon the apron to preserve his balance. He felt the brittle thing crack, and the broken half of it rang upon the floor of the cab. He fell back into the seat with a curse, and stared dismally at the two or three drops of moisture on the apron.

He shuddered.

“Well, I suppose I shall be the first. Phew! Anyhow, I shall be a martyr. That’s something. But it is a filthy death, nevertheless. I wonder if it hurts as much as they say.”

Presently a thought occurred to him⁠—he groped between his feet. A little drop was still in the broken end of the tube, and he drank that to make sure. It was better to make sure. At any rate, he would not fail.

Then it dawned upon him that there was no further need to escape the Bacteriologist. In Wellington Street he told the cabman to stop, and got out. He slipped on the step, and his head felt queer. It was rapid stuff, this cholera poison. He waved his cabman out of existence, so to speak, and stood on the pavement with his arms folded upon his breast awaiting the arrival of the Bacteriologist. There was something tragic in his pose. The sense of imminent death gave him a certain dignity. He greeted his pursuer with a defiant laugh.

“Vive l’Anarchie! You are too late, my friend, I have drunk it. The cholera is abroad!”

The Bacteriologist from his cab beamed curiously at him through his spectacles. “You have drunk it! An Anarchist! I see now.” He was about to say something more, and then checked himself. A smile hung in the corner of his mouth. He opened the apron of his cab as if to descend, at which the Anarchist waved him a dramatic farewell and strode off towards Waterloo Bridge, carefully jostling his infected body against as many people as possible. The Bacteriologist was so preoccupied with the vision of him that he scarcely manifested the slightest surprise at the appearance of Minnie upon the pavement with his hat and shoes and overcoat. “Very good of you to bring my things,” he said, and remained lost in contemplation of the receding figure of the Anarchist.

“You had better get in,” he said, still staring. Minnie felt absolutely convinced now that he was mad, and directed the cabman home on her own responsibility. “Put on my shoes? Certainly, dear,” said he, as the cab began to turn, and hid the strutting black figure, now small in the distance, from his eyes. Then suddenly something grotesque struck him, and he laughed. Then he remarked, “It is really very serious, though.

“You see, that man came to my house to see me, and he is an Anarchist. No⁠—don’t faint, or I cannot possibly tell you the rest. And I wanted to astonish him, not knowing he was an Anarchist, and took up a cultivation of that new species of Bacterium I was telling you of that infest, and I think cause, the blue patches upon various monkeys; and, like a fool, I said it was Asiatic cholera. And he ran away with it to poison the water of London, and he certainly might have made things look blue for this civilised city. And now he has swallowed it. Of course, I cannot say what will happen, but you know it turned that kitten blue, and the three puppies⁠—in patches, and the sparrow⁠—bright blue. But the bother is, I shall have all the trouble and expense of preparing some more.

“Put on my coat on this hot day! Why? Because we might meet Mrs. Jabber. My dear, Mrs. Jabber is not a draught. But why should I wear a coat on a hot day because of Mrs.⁠—Oh! very well.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story opens in the middle of a conversation. A bacteriologist, which means a scientist who studies bacteria, is showing a pale visitor around his laboratory. He slips a slide under the microscope and explains that it is the cholera germ, stained and killed. The visitor squints, then asks the question that gives him away: are these alive, are they dangerous now? The scientist, delighted to have an impressionable audience, gets carried away. He picks up a sealed tube and announces that here is the living thing, bottled cholera, so to speak. Then he makes a speech, which he knows is his weakness, about how a single tube released into drinking water could creep through the pipes and wells of a great city, taking the husband from the wife and the child from the mother.

The visitor's eyes shine. The reader soon understands why. He is an anarchist, and he got into the laboratory with a forged letter of introduction. As soon as the scientist is called out of the room by his wife, he slips the tube into his pocket and leaves. When the scientist realizes it is missing, he runs out in his carpet slippers and chases the cab down the street. His wife, who has no idea what is going on, concludes he has gone mad, grabs his hat and shoes, and chases after him in another cab. Wells gives us a lovely little scene of cab drivers and loafers at a cabmen's shelter treating the procession as a race and laying bets.

Inside the lead cab, the anarchist is full of fear and exultation. Wells lets us into his head, and what we find is not a revolutionary but a lonely, bitter man who has felt ignored all his life. All those who had sneered at him, neglected him, preferred other people to him will have to notice him at last. Then the cab lurches, the tube cracks, and he drinks the last drop to make sure. He steps out in the street, announces with a flourish that he has drunk the cholera, and walks off down the road, deliberately jostling people to spread the infection.

The scientist, meanwhile, smiles. The punchline comes when he explains to his wife what happened. The tube did not contain cholera at all. It held a new bacterium he had been studying that turns monkeys blue, and he had called it cholera just to impress a visitor. It turned a kitten blue, three puppies in patches, and a sparrow bright blue. The only real trouble is that he will have to prepare some more.

So the meaning is partly simple. This is a comic story about a man who is fooled by his own ideas. But look at how the joke works. The terrorist is undone by his own vanity. The scientist is almost undone by his. Both men are performers who want an audience, and both are punished by the other's gullibility.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a slight story, and it knows it. It is an anecdote, almost a sketch, and its pleasures are those of timing and tone. On those terms it works very well. Wells begins in the middle of the action, with a line of dialogue, and he gives us just enough to feel the dramatic irony before the characters do. The scientist's speech about the creeping spread of disease is the best passage in the story, and it has a double function. It is the bait that makes the theft happen, and it is also a vivid piece of writing in its own right, with that image of death picking out a house here and a house there where they did not boil their drinking water. The reader enjoys the rhetoric, and then enjoys seeing it backfire.

The structure is neat. The story has three movements, the lab, the chase, and the revelation, and the final twist reframes everything that came before. Because Wells has kept us in the anarchist's mind during the chase, we feel a flicker of dread, and then the final page pulls the rug out in a way that makes us laugh at ourselves. The device of the husband's absent-minded chatter at the end, in which he starts talking about his coat and Mrs. Jabber, deflates the whole thing perfectly. A science story ends on a note of domestic comedy.

There are also some funny details that show Wells's ear for English life. The cab drivers who treat a dash through London as a sporting event, the wife who thinks science has addled her husband, and the old nickname blue ruin, a slang term for cheap gin, which the scientist cries out as an oath. These touches give the story a spirited, music-hall quality.

The weaknesses are of the kind you would expect in an early piece. The characters are types, the Bacteriologist, the Anarchist, the wife, rather than people. The anarchist in particular is a caricature, a pale, lank-haired, nervous man of vague foreign appearance, and the narration muses on his ethnology in a way that has not aged well. Wells does give him an inner life, and the glimpse of his loneliness is the story's one touch of depth, but it is quickly turned into a joke. The story also leans on coincidence. The scientist happens to have a harmless tube on hand, and the ending depends on the reader forgetting that no one would actually label a blue-monkey culture as cholera.

Overall it is a minor but charming piece. It does not have the haunting depth of the later scientific romances. But it shows an author with a gift for comic reversal and a quick mind, and it is a pleasure to read for what it is.

III. The Impact It Had

It is fair to say that this story did not change the world. Little is documented about its immediate reception, and it is not among the pieces for which Wells is best remembered. Its importance is more modest. It was the title story of his first collection of short fiction, The Stolen Bacillus and Other Incidents, published in 1895, the same year that The Time Machine made his name. In that sense it is part of the moment when a young writer was announcing himself, and it demonstrates the range he would show over the next decade. Alongside tales of wonder and horror, he could write a farce.

The story also belongs to a particular cultural conversation. In the early 1890s, anarchist bombings in France, including the attacks associated with Ravachol and Vaillant, whose names Wells puts into the anarchist's mind, had made terrorism a familiar fear for newspaper readers. In February 1894, a bomb went off near the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, killing the man carrying it, and the incident caught the public imagination. Joseph Conrad would later use it as a basis for The Secret Agent in 1907. Wells's story, which appeared a few months after the Greenwich incident, is an early comic treatment of the same anxiety. It shows how quickly the idea of the lone bomber took hold of the popular mind.

There is also a prophetic edge. The idea of a terrorist using a biological agent against a city's water supply sounds startlingly modern, and the story is sometimes mentioned in discussions of bioterrorism in fiction. It would be a stretch to say it caused anything. But it shows how readily a writer in 1894 could imagine the connection between the new science of bacteriology and the old fear of political violence. The germ theory itself was only a decade or so old in its public form, after Robert Koch identified the cholera bacterium in 1883, and the great cholera outbreak in Hamburg in 1892 was fresh in memory.

The story has been reprinted in many collections of Wells's short fiction and in anthologies of comic and detective-adjacent tales. It is occasionally used in classrooms as an example of dramatic irony, since the reader knows or guesses more than the characters do. Its larger legacy lies in the comic tradition it helped feed. The idea that a dangerous plot can be foiled by the plotter's own misunderstanding is a staple of farce, from silent films to sitcoms. If you have ever watched a character carefully steal the wrong thing, you have seen a descendant of this story, even if the line of descent is hard to trace.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers tend to enjoy most about this story is how much fun it is. After the dread of some of Wells's other fiction, the sheer energy of the chase is refreshing. The cab race, with its snowball effect, has a slapstick quality. First the scientist runs after the anarchist in his slippers, then his wife follows with his hat, and then the loafers at the cabmen's shelter start calling out the odds. There is something very English about the comic escalation, and it is easy to picture on a stage or screen.

Readers also like the scientist. He is an enthusiast, a man in love with his subject, eager to astonish an apparently interested visitor. His rhetorical flourish about the pestilence imprisoned in a little tube is delivered with such relish that it is easy to see how an unstable listener might take it as an invitation. At the same time, the story treats him with affection. He is absent-minded, a little vain, and very human. When he explains the situation to his wife with such offhand calm, it is irresistible.

People also love the punchline. The reveal that the tube held a harmless blue-staining bacterium is delightfully deflating, and the details are good. The kitten blue, the three puppies in patches, the sparrow bright blue. These small images make the joke vivid, and they invite a smile even on a second reading. The final domestic exchange about the coat and Mrs. Jabber is one of the most charming endings in Wells's work, as if the story could not resist one last small joke.

There is also a more reflective pleasure. Beneath the farce, the anarchist's thoughts are oddly touching. His fantasy that all those who ignored him will finally pay attention is recognizably human. Readers sometimes comment that the story, while laughing at him, also understands the loneliness that can curdle into violence. That glimmer of sympathy gives the comedy a slightly sharper edge.

And finally, it is short and easy to enjoy. A reader who knows Wells only from his famous novels will find here a lighter side, a writer who can be witty and nimble. For many people it is a pleasing surprise, the discovery that the author of The War of the Worlds could also write a comedy of errors in about two thousand words.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because this is a small early story, there is not a large body of famous criticism of it. What can be said is that it is generally regarded as a minor piece, amusing but slight, and that most critics of Wells's short fiction place it well below the best of his stories. When scholars discuss Wells's early work, they tend to focus on the scientific romances and on stories like "The Country of the Blind," while this one is treated as a pleasant trifle.

The most common reservation is about depth. The story is a single joke, extended and well told, but a joke nonetheless. It has little of the philosophical weight or social bite of his larger works. Readers looking for the big ideas of Wells, evolution, class, or the fate of civilization, will find only hints. The idea about bacteria as a weapon is raised but not explored, and the anarchist's motives are mentioned but not examined.

A second concern is the portrayal of the anarchist. He is drawn as a pale, haggard, nervous man with an indefinable foreign look, and the scientist muses that he is not a Teutonic type nor a common Latin one, and calls him a morbid product. That kind of casual speculation about a person's ethnic type was common in the period, but it reads as prejudiced today. More broadly, the story takes the common newspaper image of the anarchist as a deranged, resentful loner and plays it for laughs. That is a stereotype, and a reader may feel the story trades on it rather than complicating it. It is worth noting that real anarchism in the 1890s was a varied movement, with thinkers who rejected violence, and the story does not engage with any of that.

There is also the matter of plausibility. Commentators sometimes point out that the premise strains belief, since a scientist would hardly leave a labelled tube of living cholera lying around for a visitor to pick up, nor would a bacteriologist mislabel a harmless culture just to impress someone. But this complaint misses the register of the story. It is farce, not realism, and the improbability is part of the fun. A different objection is that the final twist depends on withholding information from the reader, which some find a little cheap. The scientist knows from the start that the tube is harmless but does not say so until the end. That is how many comic stories work, but it is a device, and it has limits.

In short, the story is liked more than it is praised. It is a charming minor work that shows its author's wit, and its faults are the faults of a young writer still finding his range.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place this story, begin with the setting of 1890s London, a city of magazines, cabs and gaslight, and a literary scene that prized the short story. Wells was one of a generation of writers, along with Arthur Conan Doyle, Rudyard Kipling and Robert Louis Stevenson, who made their names in periodicals. The Strand and the Pall Mall Budget wanted tight, entertaining tales, and Wells learned to provide them. This story is a good example of that commercial craft. It has a hook, a reversal and a laugh.

It also belongs to the tradition of the comic short story, in which a plan goes comically wrong. Some readers will think of O. Henry, who was writing American stories of ironic twists a decade or so later, or of the comedies of Jerome K. Jerome, whose Three Men in a Boat appeared in 1889. The story has the same spirit of English comic chaos, with a sensible woman at the center of it.

In terms of subject matter, it is a link in the chain of fiction about terrorism and science. Conrad's The Secret Agent, G. K. Chesterton's The Man Who Was Thursday and Robert Louis Stevenson and Fanny Stevenson's comic The Dynamiter all touch on anarchist plots, often with a mixture of fear and mockery. Wells's contribution is the scientific twist. The weapon is not a bomb but a germ, which makes the story an early ancestor of the modern thriller about biological terror, though its tone is a long way from the grim seriousness of such works.

It also fits within Wells's career. Many of his early stories are about the unexpected consequences of scientific knowledge. In "The Stolen Bacillus," as in later works, a piece of science escapes the control of the people who understand it and ends up in unexpected hands. Here the consequence is comic. In The Island of Doctor Moreau or The Invisible Man, it is tragic. The comparison shows the same basic situation treated in different moods.

Why read it now? Because it is brief, funny and surprisingly relevant. We live in a time when fear of unseen threats and fear of terrorism are familiar. It is oddly comforting to see an old story take both fears and turn them into laughter. It also reminds us that the person who frightens us may be more foolish than formidable, and that a speech delivered too enthusiastically can have consequences its speaker never intended.
