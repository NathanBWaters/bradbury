A Bad Day for Sales

Fritz Leiber (1953) • 11 min read

“Say, Ma, he doesn’t look like a robot at all,” a child said. “He looks like a turtle.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Fritz Leiber's A Bad Day for Sales, published in 1953, is a very short story with a very long shadow. Leiber was an American writer who moved easily between science fiction, fantasy and horror, and he wrote this one in the early years of the Cold War, when the hydrogen bomb was new and the newspapers talked about a truce that felt like a held breath. The story follows Robie, a friendly mobile vending machine who rolls through Times Square, charming a crowd and selling candy, soda and nail polish. It is funny, bright and light on its feet. Then something happens that Robie is not built to understand, and the comedy turns on you. Listen to how the story treats Robie's cheerfulness, and to what he can and cannot notice. Its point is simple and chilling: a world built to sell things can keep selling long after it should have stopped, and a polite machine can be more frightening than an angry one.

The big bright doors of the office building parted with a pneumatic whoosh and Robie glided onto Times Square. The crowd that had been watching the fifty-foot-tall girl on the clothing billboard get dressed, or reading the latest news about the Hot Truce scrawl itself in yard-high script, hurried to look.

Robie was still a novelty. Robie was fun. For a little while yet, he could steal the show. But the attention did not make Robie proud. He had no more emotions than the pink plastic giantess, who dressed and undressed endlessly whether there was a crowd or the street was empty, and who never once blinked her blue mechanical eyes. But she merely drew business while Robie went out after it.

For Robie was the logical conclusion of the development of vending machines. All the earlier ones had stood in one place, on a floor or hanging on a wall, and blankly delivered merchandise in return for coins, whereas Robie searched for customers. He was the demonstration model of a line of sales robots to be manufactured by Shuler Vending Machines, provided the public invested enough in stocks to give the company capital to go into mass production.

The publicity Robie drew stimulated investments handsomely. It was amusing to see the TV and newspaper coverage of Robie selling, but not a fraction as much fun as being approached personally by him. Those who were usually bought anywhere from one to five hundred shares, if they had any money and foresight enough to see that sales robots would eventually be on every street and highway in the country.

Robie radared the crowd, found that it surrounded him solidly, and stopped. With a carefully built-in sense of timing, he waited for the tension and expectation to mount before he began talking.

“Say, Ma, he doesn’t look like a robot at all,” a child said. “He looks like a turtle.”

Which was not completely inaccurate. The lower part of Robie’s body was a metal hemisphere hemmed with sponge rubber and not quite touching the sidewalk. The upper was a metal box with black holes in it. The box could swivel and duck.

A chromium-bright hoopskirt with a turret on top.

“Reminds me too much of the Little Joe Paratanks,” a legless veteran of the Persian War muttered, and rapidly rolled himself away on wheels rather like Robie’s.

His departure made it easier for some of those who knew about Robie to open a path in the crowd. Robie headed straight for the gap. The crowd whooped.

Robie glided very slowly down the path, deftly jogging aside whenever he got too close to ankles in skylon or sockassins. The rubber buffer on his hoopskirt was merely an added safeguard.

The boy who had called Robie a turtle jumped in the middle of the path and stood his ground, grinning foxily.

Robie stopped two feet short of him. The turret ducked. The crowd got quiet.

“Hello, youngster,” Robie said in a voice that was smooth as that of a TV star, and was, in fact, a recording of one.

The boy stopped smiling. “Hello,” he whispered.

“How old are you?” Robie asked.

“Nine. No, eight.”

“That’s nice,” Robie observed. A metal arm shot down from his neck, stopped just short of the boy.

The boy jerked back.

“For you,” Robie said.

The boy gingerly took the red polly-lop from the neatly fashioned blunt metal claws, and began to unwrap it.

“Nothing to say?” asked Robie.

“Uh⁠—thank you.”

After a suitable pause, Robie continued. “And how about a nice refreshing drink of Poppy Pop to go with your polly-lop?” The boy lifted his eyes, but didn’t stop licking the candy. Robie waggled his claws slightly. “Just give me a quarter and within five seconds⁠—”

A little girl wriggled out of the forest of legs. “Give me a polly-lop, too, Robie,” she demanded.

“Rita, come back here!” a woman in the third rank of the crowd called angrily.

Robie scanned the newcomer gravely. His reference silhouettes were not good enough to let him distinguish the sex of children, so he merely repeated, “Hello, youngster.”

“Rita!”

“Give me a polly-lop!”

Disregarding both remarks, for a good salesman is single-minded and does not waste bait, Robie said winningly, “I’ll bet you read Junior Space Killers. Now I have here⁠—”

“Uh-uh, I’m a girl. He got a polly-lop.”

At the word “girl,” Robie broke off. Rather ponderously, he said, “I’ll bet you read Gee-Gee Jones, Space Stripper. Now I have here the latest issue of that thrilling comic, not yet in the stationary vending machines. Just give me fifty cents and within five⁠—”

“Please let me through. I’m her mother.”

A young woman in the front rank drawled over her powder-sprayed shoulder, “I’ll get her for you,” and slithered out on six-inch platform shoes. “Run away, children,” she said nonchalantly. Lifting her arms behind her head, she pirouetted slowly before Robie to show how much she did for her bolero half-jacket and her form-fitting slacks that melted into skylon just above the knees. The little girl glared at her. She ended the pirouette in profile.

At this age-level, Robie’s reference silhouettes permitted him to distinguish sex, though with occasional amusing and embarrassing miscalls. He whistled admiringly. The crowd cheered.

Someone remarked critically to a friend, “It would go over better if he was built more like a real robot. You know, like a man.”

The friend shook his head. “This way it’s subtler.”

No one in the crowd was watching the newscript overhead as it scribbled, “Ice Pack for Hot Truce? Vanadin hints Russ may yield on Pakistan.”

Robie was saying, “… in the savage new glamor-tint we have christened Mars Blood, complete with spray applicator and fit-all fingerstalls that mask each finger completely except for the nail. Just give me five dollars⁠—uncrumpled bills may be fed into the revolving rollers you see beside my arm⁠—and within five seconds⁠—”

“No, thanks, Robie,” the young woman yawned.

“Remember,” Robie persisted, “for three more weeks, seductivizing Mars Blood will be unobtainable from any other robot or human vendor.”

“No, thanks.”

Robie scanned the crowd resourcefully. “Is there any gentleman here⁠ ⁠…” he began just as a woman elbowed her way through the front rank.

“I told you to come back!” she snapped at the little girl.

“But I didn’t get my polly-lop!”

“… who would care to⁠ ⁠…”

“Rita!”

“Robie cheated. Ow!”

Meanwhile, the young woman in the half bolero had scanned the nearby gentlemen on her own. Deciding that there was less than a fifty percent chance of any of them accepting the proposition Robie seemed about to make, she took advantage of the scuffle to slither gracefully back into the ranks. Once again the path was clear before Robie.

He paused, however, for a brief recapitulation of the more magical properties of Mars Blood, including a telling phrase about “the passionate claws of a Martian sunrise.”

But no one bought. It wasn’t quite time. Soon enough silver coins would be clinking, bills going through the rollers faster than laundry, and five hundred people struggling for the privilege of having their money taken away from them by America’s first mobile sales robot.

But there were still some tricks that Robie had to do free, and one certainly should enjoy those before starting the more expensive fun.

So Robie moved on until he reached the curb. The variation in level was instantly sensed by his under-scanners. He stopped. His head began to swivel. The crowd watched in eager silence. This was Robie’s best trick.

Robie’s head stopped swiveling. His scanners had found the traffic light. It was green. Robie edged forward. But then the light turned red. Robie stopped again, still on the curb. The crowd softly ahhed its delight.

It was wonderful to be alive and watching Robie on such an exciting day. Alive and amused in the fresh, weather-controlled air between the lines of bright skyscrapers with their winking windows and under a sky so blue you could almost call it dark.

(But way, way up, where the crowd could not see, the sky was darker still. Purple-dark, with stars showing. And in that purple-dark, a silver-green something, the color of a bud, plunged down at better than three miles a second. The silver-green was a newly developed paint that foiled radar.)

Robie was saying, “While we wait for the light, there’s time for you youngsters to enjoy a nice refreshing Poppy Pop. Or for you adults⁠—only those over five feet tall are eligible to buy⁠—to enjoy an exciting Poppy Pop fizz. Just give me a quarter or⁠—in the case of adults, one dollar and a quarter; I’m licensed to dispense intoxicating liquors⁠—and within five seconds⁠ ⁠…”

But that was not cutting it quite fine enough. Just three seconds later, the silver-green bud bloomed above Manhattan into a globular orange flower. The skyscrapers grew brighter and brighter still, the brightness of the inside of the Sun. The windows winked blossoming white fire-flowers.

The crowd around Robie bloomed, too. Their clothes puffed into petals of flame. Their heads of hair were torches.

The orange flower grew, stem and blossom. The blast came. The winking windows shattered tier by tier, became black holes. The walls bent, rocked, cracked. A stony dandruff flaked from their cornices. The flaming flowers on the sidewalk were all leveled at once. Robie was shoved ten feet. His metal hoopskirt dimpled, regained its shape.

The blast ended. The orange flower, grown vast, vanished overhead on its huge, magic beanstalk. It grew dark and very still. The cornice-dandruff pattered down. A few small fragments rebounded from the metal hoopskirt.

Robie made some small, uncertain movements, as if feeling for broken bones. He was hunting for the traffic light, but it no longer shone either red or green.

He slowly scanned a full circle. There was nothing anywhere to interest his reference silhouettes. Yet whenever he tried to move, his under-scanners warned him of low obstructions. It was very puzzling.

The silence was disturbed by moans and a crackling sound, as faint at first as the scampering of distant rats.

A seared man, his charred clothes fuming where the blast had blown out the fire, rose from the curb. Robie scanned him.

“Good day, sir,” Robie said. “Would you care for a smoke? A truly cool smoke? Now I have here a yet-unmarketed brand⁠ ⁠…”

But the customer had run away, screaming, and Robie never ran after customers, though he could follow them at a medium brisk roll. He worked his way along the curb where the man had sprawled, carefully keeping his distance from the low obstructions, some of which writhed now and then, forcing him to jog. Shortly he reached a fire hydrant. He scanned it. His electronic vision, though it still worked, had been somewhat blurred by the blast.

“Hello, youngster,” Robie said. Then, after a long pause, “Cat got your tongue? Well, I have a little present for you. A nice, lovely polly-lop.

“Take it, youngster,” he said after another pause. “It’s for you. Don’t be afraid.”

His attention was distracted by other customers, who began to rise up oddly here and there, twisting forms that confused his reference silhouettes and would not stay to be scanned properly. One cried, “Water,” but no quarter clinked in Robie’s claws when he caught the word and suggested, “How about a nice refreshing drink of Poppy Pop?”

The rat-crackling of the flames had become a jungle muttering. The blind windows began to wink fire again.

A little girl marched, stepping neatly over arms and legs she did not look at. A white dress and the once taller bodies around her had shielded her from the brilliance and the blast. Her eyes were fixed on Robie. In them was the same imperious confidence, though none of the delight, with which she had watched him earlier.

“Help me, Robie,” she said. “I want my mother.”

“Hello, youngster,” Robie said. “What would you like? Comics? Candy?”

“Where is she, Robie? Take me to her.”

“Balloons? Would you like to watch me blow up a balloon?”

The little girl began to cry. The sound triggered off another of Robie’s novelty circuits, a service feature that had brought in a lot of favorable publicity.

“Is something wrong?” he asked. “Are you in trouble? Are you lost?”

“Yes, Robie. Take me to my mother.”

“Stay right here,” Robie said reassuringly, “and don’t be frightened. I will call a policeman.” He whistled shrilly, twice.

Time passed. Robie whistled again. The windows flared and roared. The little girl begged, “Take me away, Robie,” and jumped onto a little step in his hoopskirt.

“Give me a dime,” Robie said.

The little girl found one in her pocket and put it in his claws.

“Your weight,” Robie said, “is fifty-four and one-half pounds.”

“Have you seen my daughter, have you seen her?” a woman was crying somewhere. “I left her watching that thing while I stepped inside⁠—Rita!”

“Robie helped me,” the little girl began babbling at her. “He knew I was lost. He even called the police, but they didn’t come. He weighed me, too. Didn’t you, Robie?”

But Robie had gone off to peddle Poppy Pop to the members of a rescue squad which had just come around the corner, more robotlike in their asbestos suits than he in his metal skin.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story begins as a cheerful comedy about the future. Robie, a sales robot shaped like a metal hoopskirt with a turret on top, glides out of an office building into Times Square. He is a demonstration model. If enough people buy stock in his company, there will be thousands of him. The crowd loves him. A child says he looks like a turtle, a young woman poses for him, and Robie works through his routines: a free lollipop to hook a child, a pitch for a comic book, a pitch for a nail polish called Mars Blood, and his best trick, which is stopping politely at a curb when the traffic light turns red.

Everything in these opening pages is a small lesson in how Robie thinks. He does not think. He has reference silhouettes, which are rough outlines of customers, and he matches each person to a script. When he cannot tell a girl from a boy, he guesses, and gets it wrong. When a child asks for the lollipop he gave to someone else, he ignores her, because a good salesman, we are told, is single-minded and does not waste bait. This is funny, but it is also a portrait of a mind with no inside. Robie is not cruel. He simply has no way to register anything that is not a sale.

Then comes the pivot, tucked into a parenthesis. Far above the crowd, where the sky is purple and the stars show, something silver-green is falling at more than three miles a second. A few seconds later, the bomb goes off. Leiber describes it in the language of flowers: an orange flower, a stem and a blossom, flaming flowers on the sidewalk. That gentle, almost pretty vocabulary is deliberate. It mimics the soft-sell language of advertising, and it keeps the horror from being a spectacle. We are made to look at it sideways, the way the crowd, a second before, was looking at a toy.

The rest of the story is Robie surviving, and that is where the meaning lives. He does not understand what has happened, so he simply carries on. He offers a cool smoke to a burned man who runs away screaming. He offers a lollipop to a fire hydrant, because it is a low shape his blurred sensors read as a child. He answers a dying person's cry for water with an advertisement for Poppy Pop. When a lost little girl asks him for help, one of his novelty circuits fires, and he whistles for a policeman, weighs her for a dime, and announces that she weighs fifty-four and a half pounds. Helping is part of his act, so he performs it, and it means nothing.

The final image is the sharpest. Robie wanders off to sell soda to a rescue squad in asbestos suits, who look more robotlike than he does in his metal skin. The people have been made mechanical by the catastrophe, while the machine goes on being exactly what it was. Leiber is not really warning us about robots. He is warning us about the part of us that, given a script and a market, can keep selling and smiling through anything.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Start with what is masterful, because there is a lot of it. The structure is a trap, and it is built with real patience. For roughly two thirds of the story, Leiber lets us enjoy ourselves. The satire is light and sharp: skylon stockings, powder-sprayed shoulders, a newsscript scrawling overhead about a hot truce, a veteran of a future war rolling quietly away because Robie reminds him of a tank. We laugh, and in laughing we join the crowd that is about to die. The story makes us complicit in the very attitude it is about to condemn, the habit of treating a dangerous world as entertainment.

The single best choice is the parenthesis. Leiber does not announce the bomb with a drum roll. He tucks it into brackets, like a stage direction, and cuts directly back to Robie's sales patter. The line that follows, that his pitch was not cutting it quite fine enough, is a sly piece of black comedy, and it makes the explosion feel as sudden and arbitrary as it would be in real life. The prose of the blast, with its flowers and beanstalk, is not subtle, but it does what he needs: it is beautiful enough to be sickening.

The second half is also strong, because Leiber keeps the premise perfectly disciplined. Robie never learns, never softens, never has a glimmer. Every attempt at a sentimental turn, such as the child calling for her mother, is converted into a transaction. That refusal to flinch is the story's moral engine. A weaker writer would have given the robot a flicker of pathos and let readers off the hook.

Now the weaknesses. The story is a one-idea piece, and the idea is fully visible by the time Robie offers a smoke to the burned man. The remaining pages mostly repeat the same joke in a darker key. Some of the early satire is also uneven. The jokes about women, such as the young woman posing in her bolero and the little girl who wants to be treated as a girl, are of their time, and they read as a bit cheap now. They are meant to show a shallow consumer culture, but they lean on stock figures to do it. A few of the invented words and brand names also feel like sci-fi decoration rather than insight.

There is also a question of scale. A nuclear strike on Manhattan is an enormous event, and the story handles it in a few paragraphs of cool irony. That is its strength, but it also means the human suffering stays mostly offstage. We see a seared man, a lost child, a rescue squad. For some readers that restraint is powerful. For others it feels like the story uses atomic death as a punchline. Both reactions are fair, and the story's coldness is meant to produce exactly that discomfort.

III. The Impact It Had

It is worth being honest about this story's footprint. It did not become a famous cultural landmark the way a novel or film might, and detailed documentation of how readers reacted in 1953 is thin. What we can say is that it appeared in the science fiction magazines of the early fifties, and has been reprinted in many anthologies of Leiber's work and of Cold War science fiction ever since. That longevity in anthologies is itself a kind of impact. Editors keep choosing it because it can be read in ten minutes and discussed for an hour.

For Leiber's career, the story belongs to a remarkable stretch of work. He was the son of a famous Shakespearean stage actor, and that theatrical background shows in his love of voice, timing and the staged entrance. He had already written the horror novel Conjure Wife and was building the Fafhrd and Gray Mouser sword-and-sorcery tales. In the fifties he also contributed a series of sharp social satires to the magazine market, and this is one of the cleanest examples. It shows that he could handle the political anxieties of the age with a comic touch rather than a lecture.

The story's larger influence is on a way of thinking about artificial beings. The usual fear about robots, in pulp fiction and in later films, is that they will turn against us. Leiber offers a quieter, more plausible fear: that they will do exactly what they were designed to do, with perfect courtesy, in conditions where that behavior has become obscene. This idea echoes through later fiction about automated systems that keep running after the people who built them have gone, from stories about empty smart houses to satirical visions of customer-service software that cannot recognize an emergency. Modern readers will think of chatbots that cheerfully upsell during a crisis, or of recorded voices that say your call is important to us while the line burns.

It also takes its place in the literature of the Cold War shelter and survival story. By the mid-fifties, American readers were being told to duck and cover, and the stories that came out of that moment, whether comic or grim, shaped the cultural imagination of nuclear war. Teachers use A Bad Day for Sales to introduce students to satire, to the science fiction of the atomic age, and to the idea that tone can be a moral choice. Because it is so short, it makes an ideal classroom text, and it is one of the stories that lets students see how an author can make a reader feel something by withholding feeling.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers tend to love first is the voice. Leiber writes the opening in a bright, sing-song, almost advertising rhythm, with its repeated promise that within five seconds something will happen. That promise becomes a refrain, and when it is interrupted by the bomb it lands with real force. Many readers remember the phrase within five seconds long after they have forgotten the plot, which tells you how well it works as a hook.

Readers also love Robie himself. It is hard to hate him, and that is the point. He is polite, patient and cheerful. He waits for the light to change. He gently asks a crying child if she is in trouble. A reader who laughs at his first routine can feel strangely protective of him in the second half, and that confusion of feelings is the story's richest effect. The best moment for many people is the little girl stepping onto his hoopskirt and handing him a dime to be carried away. It is almost tender, and it is entirely hollow, and the story knows both things at once.

Another source of admiration is the economy. In a few thousand words, Leiber builds a believable commercial future, a nuclear attack, and a moral argument, and none of it feels rushed. Writers often point to the parenthetical bomb as a lesson in timing. You can imagine the scene being handled with a long, loud build-up. Instead, a single sentence in brackets changes everything, and the reader's own imagination does the rest.

There is also the pleasure of recognition. Readers who know modern advertising, or have simply been pitched to by an insistent app, will recognize Robie's relentless friendliness. The story feels more current now than it did in 1953, because the technology it imagines has arrived in softer forms: devices that talk, follow us, and suggest purchases. Readers in the twenty-first century often say that the story seems to be about them, which is a high compliment for a piece written during the Eisenhower years.

Finally, readers admire its emotional honesty about the Cold War. Many people who lived through those decades remember a background hum of dread, mixed with a determined cheerfulness in public life. Leiber captured that mixture precisely: the amusement, the newspaper headlines, the crowd that is delighted by the entertainment of the day, and the sky above it that no one thinks about. That paradox of ordinary life under permanent threat is something later generations of readers still connect with.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because this is a short, lightly documented story, there is no famous wave of hostile reviews to report. What we can describe are the objections that thoughtful readers raise, and the places where the story is most open to challenge.

The most common criticism is that it is too neat. The story has one move, a bright comedy that turns into horror, and once you see the move, it can feel schematic. Some readers find the irony a little heavy-handed, especially the contrast between Robie's slogans and the dying around him. Satire is always at risk of being too sure of itself, and a few moments, such as the rescue squad being more robotlike than the robot, state the theme with a touch more emphasis than the story needs.

A second objection concerns the treatment of women and children. The early scenes rely on a stock figure of the glamorous young woman as consumer, and the little girl is mostly a device, there to be sold to and then to be abandoned. A modern reader may feel that the story's critique of shallow consumer culture is delivered through shallow characters. Leiber's defenders reply that the point is precisely that Robie sees people as silhouettes, and the story shares his limited view on purpose. Even so, some of the jokes hold up less well than the central conceit.

A third line of criticism is moral. Is it fair to use the destruction of a city as the ending to a gag about sales patter? Some readers feel that the story trades on the horror of nuclear war without exploring it, and that the irony keeps both the characters and the reader at a safe distance. A story like John Hersey's account of Hiroshima insists on the particular suffering of individual people. Leiber does the opposite, and flattens everyone into a crowd of silhouettes. That is a legitimate artistic choice, but it will always strike some readers as cold.

Finally, there is the argument that the premise has dated. A robot salesman who needs reference silhouettes seems quaint beside the real technology we now live with. The Cold War specifics, such as the hot truce and the stand-off over Pakistan, belong to a vanished moment. Yet most readers find that the aging of the details only sharpens the story's central point. The gadgets change. The habit of selling through disaster does not.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

A Bad Day for Sales sits at the meeting point of two traditions. The first is the science fiction satire of the early fifties, much of it published in the magazine Galaxy under its editor H. L. Gold. Galaxy specialized in stories that used comic exaggeration to attack advertising, conformity and the military mind. The most famous example is The Space Merchants by Frederik Pohl and C. M. Kornbluth, a novel about a world run by advertising agencies. Leiber's story is a close cousin: it takes the logic of a consumer society to its extreme and lets the extreme speak for itself.

The second tradition is the atomic-age story of aftermath. Ray Bradbury's There Will Come Soft Rains, from 1950, is the closest comparison. In it, an automated house goes on cooking breakfasts and reciting poetry for a family that has been reduced to shadows on the wall. Both stories use a machine's blind routine to measure the size of a human loss. Bradbury's tone is elegiac and Leiber's is satirical, and reading them side by side shows how differently the same insight can be dramatized. Later works in the same vein include Nevil Shute's novel On the Beach, the film Dr. Strangelove, and Kurt Vonnegut's satirical fiction, all of which treat the end of the world with a mix of irony and horror.

Going further back, Robie descends from the mechanical men of Karel Capek's play R.U.R., which gave us the word robot, and from the long literary habit of using automata to hold up a mirror to human behavior. Going forward, he has relatives in every story about an artificial servant that follows its programming off a cliff. The idea that the danger of machines lies in their obedience, not their rebellion, is now central to discussions of automation and artificial intelligence.

Within Leiber's own work, the story shows the same imaginative range that gave us his sword-and-sorcery heroes and his time-war novel The Big Time. He was never confined to one genre, and this short piece shows what he could do with just a few strokes.

Why read it today? Because it is short, because it is funny, and because it performs a trick that few stories manage: it makes you complicit and then makes you ashamed, and it does so without a single character delivering a sermon. In a world where we are surrounded by cheerful, persistent, automated voices, the story reads like a small warning light. It asks what our machines, and our own habits, would do if the worst happened, and whether they would even notice.
