Yi Sang's 'Crow's-Eye View' (1934) and 'The Wings': The Architect-Poet of Colonial Gyeongseong

Korea — How a twenty-three-year-old architectural engineer coughing blood in a Tongin-dong hanok shattered Korean prose with reverse mirrors and rooftop sirens (1930s Colonial Modernism (1910–1937)) • 20 min read

“Thirteen children rush down a street. (A dead-end alley will suffice.) The 1st child says it is terrifying. The 2nd child also says it is terrifying...” — Yi Sang, Ogamdo (Crow's-Eye View), Poem No. 1 (July 24, 1934)

Key Context & Why It Matters

On July 24, 1934, readers opening the morning edition of the Joseon Jungang Ilbo newspaper in colonial Gyeongseong (Seoul) stared in disbelief at a poem that contained zero punctuation, mathematical equations, and a terrifying incantation of thirteen children sprinting down a cul-de-sac: 'Ogamdo' ('Crow's-Eye View'). Its twenty-three-year-old author, Kim Hae-gyeong—who signed his work Yi Sang (1910–1937)—had graduated first in his class in architecture from Gyeongseong Higher Technical School and worked as a colonial government building engineer before pulmonary tuberculosis forced him into the smoky dabang teahouses of Jongno. Before dying at age twenty-six in Tokyo, Yi Sang wrote 'The Wings' (Nalgae, 1936), transforming colonial alienation into a masterpiece of world modernism. Anchored at Tongin-dong and Jongno in Seoul, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

Walk up the quiet alley of **Jahamun-ro 7-gil** in **Tongin-dong**, Jongno-gu, just west of Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, and stop at **154-10 Tongin-dong**: a small, L-shaped wooden *hanok* with dark roof tiles and a narrow concrete-and-steel doorway opened to the street (**Yi Sang’s House**, 이상의 집). From the age of three until he was twenty-three, a boy named **Kim Hae-gyeong** (김해경, 1910–1937) lived behind these sliding paper doors as the adopted heir of his stern, wealthy paternal uncle Kim Yeon-pil. When he walked out of this traditional courtyard into the neon-lit, tram-clanging streets of 1930s colonial **Gyeongseong** (경성, Seoul), he reinvented himself under the pen name **Yi Sang** (이상, 李箱)—and detonated a literary bomb whose shockwaves still reverberate through Korean writing today. How did Kim Hae-gyeong acquire the strange, ironic pen name **Yi Sang** (literally 'Plum Box')? While working on a colonial construction site after graduating first in his class from the Architecture Department of **Gyeongseong Higher Technical School** (*경성고등공업학교*, predecessor of Seoul National University’s College of Engineering) in 1929, a Japanese carpenter mistook the young Korean architect for a contractor named 'Lee-san' (*이상*). Kim Hae-gyeong loved the pun—which also sounded like *isang* (이상, 理想, 'Ideal' or 異常, 'Abnormal/Strange')—and when his close friend, the modernist painter **Gu Bon-ung** (구본웅), gifted him a wooden artist’s paintbox carved from plum wood (*오얏나무 상자*, *李箱*), he adopted *Yi Sang* forever. In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the 1930s Colonial Modernism (1910–1937), the rise of Yi Sang (이상 Kim Hae-gyeong, 1910–1937 Korean avant-garde poet and architect) represented far more than an isolated local experiment.

Because Yi Sang was trained not in traditional Confucian humanities, but in **structural engineering, descriptive geometry, calculus, and architectural drafting**, he looked at language the way an architect looks at steel beams and blueprints. Working from 1929 to 1933 as an architectural engineer (*gisu*) in the Public Works Division of the Japanese Government-General of Korea—designing and inspecting colonial post offices and government buildings—he published his earliest Dadaist-inspired poems in **1931** in the architectural trade journal *Choseon to Kenchiku* ('Korea and Architecture') under titles like *'Three-Dimensional Angle Blueprint'* and *'Blueprint Relative to the Line No. 1'*! Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Tongin-dong and Jongno in Seoul stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Ogamdo (오감도 'Crow's-Eye View' 1934 experimental poem series) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Nalgae (날개 'The Wings' 1936 modernist novella set in Gyeongseong) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

In 1933, however, massive **hemoptysis**—coughing up bright red blood from advanced pulmonary tuberculosis (*폐결핵*)—forced twenty-three-year-old Yi Sang to resign his government engineering post. Knowing that in the pre-antibiotic 1930s tuberculosis was a slow death sentence, he used the inheritance mortgage from his uncle’s Tongin-dong house to open a literary **Dabang** (다방, coffeehouse) in Jongno 1-ga named **Jebi** (제비, 'The Swallow'), followed by cafés named *69*, *Tsuru* ('Crane'), and *Mugi*. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Then, on **July 24, 1934**, the newspaper *Joseon Jungang Ilbo* (where novelist Lee Tae-jun headed the arts desk) began publishing Yi Sang’s planned thirty-part serial poem **Ogamdo** (오감도, 烏瞰圖). Notice the brilliant, chilling visual pun in the title! Two years later, in the **September 1936** issue of the magazine *Jogwang*, Yi Sang published his immortal prose masterpiece: the short novella **Nalgae** (날개, **'The Wings'**). The opening line remains one of the most famous sentences in modern Korean literature: **'Have you ever heard of the ”genius who became a stuffed specimen”? Just one month after *The Wings* appeared in print, in autumn 1936, Yi Sang sailed to **Tokyo**—hoping to confront the metropolitan heart of modernity face-to-face. 1. Architectural Typography: How Engineering Training Shaped 'Ogamdo'

Literary critics and architectural historians note that Yi Sang's 1931–1934 poetry cannot be understood apart from his training at Gyeongseong Higher Technical School. In Ogamdo ('Crow's-Eye View') and his Japanese-language poems ('Architecture Infinite Hexahedron'), Yi Sang treated the printed newspaper page as a two-dimensional structural blueprint: removing inter-word spaces to create load-bearing typographic walls, using mathematical coordinate grids and reversed mirror numbers (Poem No. 4), and mapping the panoptic surveillance of the colonial city from above. Set between a claustrophobic 18-family courtyard house (Room 33) and the modern commercial spectacle of Gyeongseong Station and the 1930 Mitsukoshi Department Store rooftop, 'The Wings' (날개) dramatizes the split consciousness of the colonized modern intellectual. Drugged with Adalin sedatives in a windowless room, the narrator achieves brief existential transcendence atop the department-store roof as the noon siren sounds—refusing to remain a 'stuffed genius' and demanding the right to fly.
