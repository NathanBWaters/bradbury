Crissy Field: From 1921 Army Airfield and Nisei MIS School to a Restored 100-Acre Tidal Salt Marsh

San Francisco — How Ohlone shellfish lagoons became Billy Mitchell's coastal airfield, Hangar 640's secret Japanese American intelligence school, and the largest urban wetland restoration on the Golden Gate (Early Aviation (1915–1936), World War II (1941) & Ecological Renaissance (1998–2001)) • 20 min read

“When the excavator breached the sand berm on November 9, 1999, and the tide of San Francisco Bay rushed back into Crissy Field for the first time in eighty-five years, the birds arrived before the water even settled.” — Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy (2001)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stretching for 1.5 miles along the northern waterfront of the Presidio between Marina Green and Fort Point (37.8041° N, 122.4647° W), Crissy Field compresses four millennia of San Francisco history into a single hundred-acre shoreline. For thousands of years a rich Ramaytush Ohlone tidal lagoon and salt marsh, it was filled by U.S. Army dredges in 1914 for the Panama-Pacific International Exposition racetrack, commissioned in 1921 by General Billy Mitchell as the West Coast's only continuously operating historic Army coast-defense airfield (named for Major Dana H. Crissy), used secretly in 1941 inside Hangar 640 to train the first Japanese American Military Intelligence Service (MIS) linguists of World War II, and finally restored between 1998 and 2001 into a thriving twenty-acre tidal lagoon and dune ecosystem.

Standing today at Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff in the heart of the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field (37.8041° N, 122.4647° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Early Aviation (1915–1936), World War II (1941) & Ecological Renaissance (1998–2001). Before 1912, if you stood on the northern bluffs of the Presidio below the cavalry stables and looked toward the Golden Gate, you did not see flat grass or asphalt: you looked out over a shimmering, 130-acre back-barrier tidal lagoon and salt marsh protected from the bay waves by a natural sandspit, where Ramaytush Ohlone families harvested hornsnails, cockles, mussels, and salmon for thousands of years. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how Major Dana H. Crissy (1886–1919), Col. Kai E. Rasmussen & John Aiso (1941 MIS Language School at Building 640), and landscape architect George Hargreaves and a generation of San Franciscans confronted reconciling the preservation of a National Historic Landmark 1921 grass airfield and WWII Nisei intelligence hangar with the ecological restoration of a buried tidal salt marsh, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center) emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field. In 1912–1914, to prepare the flat shoreline west of the Marina for the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition, hydraulic suction dredges pumped millions of cubic yards of bay sand and mud directly into the Presidio salt marsh—burying the ancient wetland beneath a mile-long dirt auto racetrack and polo ground where aviation pioneer Lincoln Beachey flew thrilling loop-the-loops before plunging to his death in the bay in March 1915. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during 1921 (Crissy Field commissioned), November 1, 1941 (MIS Language School opens in Building 640), and November 9, 1999 (tidal marsh breached). After World War I demonstrated the strategic importance of military airpower, U.S. Army Air Service Brigadier General Billy Mitchell organized the first transcontinental reliability and endurance air race across North America in October 1919, dispatching forty-six De Havilland DH-4 biplanes west from Long Island while fifteen planes took off eastbound from the Presidio's grassy polo field. Contemporary observers who walked past Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood Major Dana H. Crissy (1886–1919), Col. Kai E. Rasmussen & John Aiso (1941 MIS Language School at Building 640), and landscape architect George Hargreaves, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of early transcontinental biplane navigation, estuarine tidal-prism hydrology, and native foredune botanical restoration proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. On October 8, 1919, thirty-two-year-old Major Dana H. Crissy, commander of Mather Field in Sacramento, took off from the Presidio aboard a De Havilland DH-4 biplane with mechanic Sergeant Virgil Thomas and crashed fatally upon attempting a landing at Salt Lake City; when the Army completed its permanent coastal defense aviation station along the Presidio shore in 1921, it named the base **Crissy Field** in his honor. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of early transcontinental biplane navigation, estuarine tidal-prism hydrology, and native foredune botanical restoration imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center). Throughout the 1920s—the heroic age of open-cockpit biplanes—Crissy Field was the nerve center of West Coast aviation: overseen in the mid-1920s by a young Major Henry 'Hap' Arnold (future five-star commander of the U.S. Army Air Forces in World War II), its pilots flew aerial fire patrols over California forests, tested the first aerial torpedoes over the Golden Gate, and made the first dawn-to-dusk transcontinental flight arrival in June 1924 (Lieutenant Russell Maughan in 21 hours and 48 minutes). Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through 1921 (Crissy Field commissioned), November 1, 1941 (MIS Language School opens in Building 640), and November 9, 1999 (tidal marsh breached), the surrounding streets of the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. Despite these triumphs, Crissy Field was one of the most hair-raising airfields in the world to land on: thick summer fog banks poured straight through the Golden Gate at forty knots, creating wicked downdrafts off the Presidio hills—and when construction crews began spinning the 746-foot steel towers and suspension cables of the Golden Gate Bridge directly across the western approach path in 1933–1936, the Army closed Crissy Field to fixed-wing combat aircraft and transferred air operations across the bay to Hamilton Field. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Yet five years after the biplanes departed, one of the most consequential secret chapters of World War II unfolded inside an unassuming wooden seaplane hangar at the western edge of Crissy Field: **Building 640** at 640 Mason Street. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center) toward private speculation—forcing Major Dana H. Crissy (1886–1919), Col. Kai E. Rasmussen & John Aiso (1941 MIS Language School at Building 640), and landscape architect George Hargreaves and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff. In the autumn of 1941, one month before the attack on Pearl Harbor, farsighted Army intelligence officers Lieutenant Colonel John Weckerling and Captain Kai E. Rasmussen realized that the U.S. military possessed almost zero personnel capable of reading Japanese military codebooks or interrogating prisoners—and recruited brilliant twenty-one-year-old Harvard Law graduate John Fujio Aiso as chief civilian instructor of a top-secret Fourth Army Intelligence School. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of early transcontinental biplane navigation, estuarine tidal-prism hydrology, and native foredune botanical restoration and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center), the day-to-day craft of early transcontinental biplane navigation, estuarine tidal-prism hydrology, and native foredune botanical restoration produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field. On November 1, 1941, inside the drafty rooms of Hangar 640 at Crissy Field—furnishing their classrooms with orange crates and old Army cots—four *Nisei* (second-generation Japanese American) instructors and sixty students (fifty-eight *Nisei* soldiers and two Caucasian soldiers) began intensive twelve-hour-a-day training in Japanese military terminology, cursive *sōsho* handwriting, and radio interception. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center) was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff. Look at the moral courage of those *Nisei* soldiers at Crissy Field: after February 1942, when Executive Order 9066 forced the school to relocate to Minnesota and incarcerated the students' own parents, brothers, and sisters behind barbed wire in American internment camps, the graduates of the Military Intelligence Service (MIS)—eventually numbering 6,000 linguists across the Pacific Theater—translated captured Japanese battle plans, including Admiral Yamamoto's flight itinerary and the 'Z Plan,' which General Douglas MacArthur's intelligence chief Charles Willoughby stated 'shortened the Pacific War by two years and saved a million lives.' Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in 1921 (Crissy Field commissioned), November 1, 1941 (MIS Language School opens in Building 640), and November 9, 1999 (tidal marsh breached), the entire city turned its eyes toward Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center) in the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field. After the U.S. Army transferred the Presidio to the National Park Service in 1994 to become part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area, visitors walking along the Crissy Field shoreline found a fenced-off, 100-acre expanse of cracked asphalt, invasive iceplant, rusted chain-link fences, and concrete rubble. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed 1921 (Crissy Field commissioned), November 1, 1941 (MIS Language School opens in Building 640), and November 9, 1999 (tidal marsh breached), shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of Crissy Field Tidal Marsh, the Historic Grass Airfield, and Building 640 (MIS Historic Learning Center). Between 1996 and 2001, the Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy under Greg Moore, the National Park Service, and landscape architect George Hargreaves—funded by more than $34 million in philanthropic gifts led by the Evelyn and Walter Haas Jr. Fund and the Colleen and Robert Haas family alongside 2,600 individual community donors—undertook an unprecedented dual restoration. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at Mason Street and Crissy Field Avenue along the northern bayfront of the Presidio from East Beach to West Bluff adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in early transcontinental biplane navigation, estuarine tidal-prism hydrology, and native foredune botanical restoration. How do you restore both a buried prehistoric Ohlone salt marsh and a 1921 National Historic Landmark military grass airfield on the same 100 acres? Hargreaves and coastal hydrologists recreated the twenty-eight-acre historic western grass airfield strip directly in front of the 1921 hangars and administration buildings, while excavating a twenty-acre tidal lagoon and salt marsh on the eastern half of the site. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of Major Dana H. Crissy (1886–1919), Col. Kai E. Through painstaking archival recovery and public stewardship in the Presidio Waterfront and Crissy Field, the site was safeguarded so future generations could experience it firsthand.

Within months of the tidal breach and the May 6, 2001 public opening of Crissy Field, marine biologists counted more than 17 species of native fish (including Pacific herring and halibut nursery fry) and over 135 species of shorebirds, herons, egrets, and ducks feeding in the restored marsh channels. 1. Estuarine Hydrology: Keeping the Crissy Tidal Inlet Open

What is the primary coastal engineering challenge of a twenty-acre restored urban salt marsh facing the Golden Gate littoral drift? Wave action from the Pacific carries sand eastward along East Beach, constantly attempting to plug the narrow tidal channel connecting Crissy Marsh to San Francisco Bay. Hydrologists designed the twenty-acre lagoon's 'tidal prism' (the volume of water rushing in and out twice daily on the ebb and flood tides) to scour the inlet channel naturally, supplemented by periodic sand clearing when fierce winter El Niño storms build up the beach berm.
