The Wild Swans at Coole

W. B. Yeats (1917) • 4 min read

In the October twilight at Coole Park, fifty-nine wild swans scatter and wheel across the water, unchanged in their passion while the human watcher’s heart has grown old.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Composed in October 1916 at Coole Park, Lady Augusta Gregory’s estate in County Galway where Yeats had spent summers for nineteen years since 1897, the poem was written when fifty-one-year-old Yeats was reeling from the Easter Rising and his final rejected marriage proposals to both Maud Gonne and her daughter Iseult. Belonging to the High Modernism tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'The Wild Swans at Coole' (1917) unfolds across 30 verbatim lines in 5 stanzas. Published in the Little Review in June 1917 and as the title poem of Yeats’s 1919 volume The Wild Swans at Coole, it marks the pivotal transition from Yeats’s early Celtic Twilight dreamy lyricism to the austere, conversational power of his mature style.

The trees are in their autumn beauty,

The woodland paths are dry,

Under the October twilight the water

Mirrors a still sky;

Upon the brimming water among the stones

Are nine and fifty swans.

The nineteenth Autumn has come upon me

Since I first made my count;

I saw, before I had well finished,

All suddenly mount

And scatter wheeling in great broken rings

Upon their clamorous wings.

I have looked upon those brilliant creatures,

And now my heart is sore.

All’s changed since I, hearing at twilight,

The first time on this shore,

The bell-beat of their wings above my head,

Trod with a lighter tread.

Unwearied still, lover by lover,

They paddle in the cold,

Companionable streams or climb the air;

Their hearts have not grown old;

Passion or conquest, wander where they will,

Attend upon them still.

But now they drift on the still water

Mysterious, beautiful;

Among what rushes will they build,

By what lake’s edge or pool

Delight men’s eyes, when I awake some day

To find they have flown away?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed in October 1916 at Coole Park, Lady Augusta Gregory’s estate in County Galway where Yeats had spent summers for nineteen years since 1897, the poem was written when fifty-one-year-old Yeats was reeling from the Easter Rising and his final rejected marriage proposals to both Maud Gonne and her daughter Iseult. Within the broader trajectory of High Modernism, W. B. Yeats composed 'The Wild Swans at Coole' (1917) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"The trees are in their autumn beauty,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 30 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'The Wild Swans at Coole' lies the bittersweet juxtaposition of human aging and nature’s cyclical renewal: nineteen autumns after the speaker first counted the 'nine-and-fifty swans,' his own heart is 'sore,' whereas the swans remaining on the brimming water are 'Unwearied still, lover by lover,' attended wherever they fly by 'Passion or conquest'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"The first time on this shore,"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"To find they have flown away?"—the central metaphor has transformed the speaker’s initial perception, leaving the reader with an earned emotional equilibrium.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'The Wild Swans at Coole' using five six-line stanzas (ABCBDD) alternating four-beat and three-beat lines before closing each stanza with a resonant rhymed couplet, using crisp autumnal imagery ('dry woodland paths,' 'October twilight,' 'bell-beat of their wings') and the poignant odd number 'nine-and-fifty' (one short of thirty pairs, mirroring the solitary poet). Spanning 30 lines across 5 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "The trees are in their autumn beauty," and "To find they have flown away?" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Published in the Little Review in June 1917 and as the title poem of Yeats’s 1919 volume The Wild Swans at Coole, it marks the pivotal transition from Yeats’s early Celtic Twilight dreamy lyricism to the austere, conversational power of his mature style. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'The Wild Swans at Coole' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
