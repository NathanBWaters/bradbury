Hanji (Chunji): Why Korea's Mulberry-Bark Paper Breathes for a Thousand Years

Korea — How one-year-old paper mulberry fibers, hibiscus-root mucilage, and the single-cord 'webal-ttugi' swing preserved a 751 CE scroll and now repair the Louvre (Three Kingdoms & Unified Silla (751 CE) to Present) • 9 min read

“Silk lasts five hundred years, but Korean paper lasts a thousand years.” — Ancient East Asian Scholars' Proverb

Key Context & Why It Matters

In October 1966, when Korean archaeologists dismantled the stone reliquary inside the second-story body of the 751 CE Seokgatap Pagoda at Bulguksa Temple in Gyeongju, they lifted out a crumpled scroll printed before 751 CE: the Mugujeonggwang Daedaranigyeong, the world's oldest surviving woodblock print. Despite 1,215 years sealed inside damp granite, its mulberry paper (Hanji) remained intact and flexible. Whereas nineteenth-century Western wood-pulp paper turns brittle and crumbles into acid dust within a century, traditional Korean Hanji—crafted through ninety-nine manual steps ('baekji') from one-year-old daknamu inner bark boiled in alkaline lye, suspended in Sunset Hibiscus root mucilage (dakpul), and couched with the multidirectional webal-ttugi swing—maintains a neutral pH of 7.5 to 8.5 for over a millennium.

On October 13, 1966, inside the courtyard of Bulguksa Temple in Gyeongju, conservationists carefully lifted the granite lid off the relic chamber inside the second-story stone body of Seokgatap (the Sakyamuni Pagoda, erected in 751 CE). Resting inside a gilt-bronze reliquary casket wrapped in silk was a scroll measuring 6.7 centimeters high and over six meters long: the Mugujeonggwang Daedaranigyeong ('Pure Light Dharani Sutra,' National Treasure No. 126), the oldest dated woodblock-printed text on Earth. When conservators unrolled the eighth-century scroll after 1,215 years inside a humid stone pagoda, the silk wrapping had decayed into fragile dust, yet the Korean mulberry paper (Hanji) remained supple, legible, and structurally whole. Why does a modern paperback book printed in 1950 on industrial wood-pulp paper turn yellow, smell of vinegar, and crumble at the edges after just seventy years, while Korean Hanji routinely survives a full millennium—giving rise to the ancient proverb Gyeon-obaek Ji-cheonnyeon (: 'Silk lasts five hundred years; paper lasts a thousand')?

In Korean, traditional handmade paper is also affectionately called Baekji —which means not only 'white paper,' but 'the paper of ninety-nine touches by the papermaker’s hands before the hundredth touch by the user.' Every stage of those ninety-nine touches is a triumph of natural polymer chemistry and fiber mechanics. First, compare the raw botanical fiber. Western industrial paper is made by grinding whole softwood or hardwood tree trunks into short cellulose pulp (1 to 3 millimeters long) still loaded with lignin—the rigid phenolic polymer that stiffens tree wood—and bleaching it with chlorine and acidic aluminum sulfate (alum sizing). Over decades, lignin oxidizes in light to turn paper yellow, while alum reacts with atmospheric moisture to generate sulfuric acid (H2SO4), which literally scissors the cellulose chains apart from the inside (acid hydrolysis).

Traditional Hanji never uses whole tree wood. Instead, in late November and December after the leaves fall and the sap retreats, Korean papermakers (Hanji-jang) harvest only the one-year-old shoots of the native Korean paper mulberry bush, Daknamu (Broussonetia kazinoki), grown along rocky mountain slopes in Mungyeong, Andong, Gapyeong, and Jeonju where big diurnal temperature swings produce dense, long bast fibers. After steaming the bundled branches in a giant cauldron (dak-muji) to peel off the bark, the artisan scrapes away the dark outer bark and green middle layer with a curved knife until only the snow-white inner bast fiber (baekpi) remains. Those inner bast fibers measure 10 to 18 millimeters long—up to ten times longer than wood pulp fibers—and contain virtually zero lignin and high levels of tough holocellulose and pectin.

Next comes the alkaline secret that prevents acid decay for a thousand years: jaetmul, natural wood-ash lye. Rather than cooking the fibers in caustic industrial sodium hydroxide (which shreds long cellulose chains and strips out protective hemicellulose), the Hanji-jang burns dried buckwheat stalks, soybean stems, or agricultural husks into ash and leaches boiling water through it to produce a gentle, natural potassium carbonate lye with an alkaline pH of 9 to 10. After simmering the white mulberry bark in jaetmul for four hours and bleaching it naturally in cold running mountain stream water and winter sunlight (ilgwang-pyobaek), the artisan refuses to chop the fibers with steel blades in a mechanical Hollander beater. Instead, the wet bark is laid on a flat granite slab (dakdol) and pounded for hours with heavy oak mallets (danchu). Pounding crushes and fibrillates the cell walls into microscopic feathery fibrils without cutting the 15-millimeter fiber length!

The beaten mulberry fibers are then dispersed in a cold water vat mixed with Dakpul (Abelmoschus manihot, the mucilaginous root sap of the Sunset Hibiscus). Viscous and slippery, dakpul acts as a natural botanical deflocculant: it coats the long mulberry fibers so they do not tangle into clumps in the water and slows the drainage rate through the bamboo screen—before evaporating and decomposing cleanly when the finished paper dries on heated plates so no sticky residue attracts insects! Then comes the mechanical masterstroke that sets Korean Hanji apart from both Chinese Xuan paper and Japanese Washi (nagashizuki): the Korean Webal-ttugi (single-cord suspension couching) technique. In China and Japan, the papermaker holds a bamboo screen locked inside a heavy wooden deckle frame suspended by two cords (ssangbal-ttugi), scooping pulp and sloshing it predominantly back and forth along a single front-to-back grain direction.

In Korean webal-ttugi, the flexible bamboo screen (bal, woven from thousands of hair-thin split bamboo splints tied with silk thread) has no heavy locking deckle frame and hangs from a single overhead ceiling cord at the rear. Dipping the screen into the cold mulberry-and-dakpul vat, the master executes two lightning passes: first scooping front-to-back (ap-mul) so the fibers align longitudinally, and second scooping left-to-right (yeop-mul) across the grain before discharging the excess water sideways! When two wet sheets couched via webal-ttugi are laid atop one another with reversed top-and-bottom orientations to form a single two-ply sheet (hapji), the 15-millimeter mulberry fibers are locked in a multidirectional 3D criss-cross matrix (weave). Pull a sheet of industrial paper or two-cord paper along its grain, and it tears in a straight line; pull a sheet of webal-ttugi Hanji, and the multidirectional tensile strength resists tearing from every angle equally!

Finally, for royal documents and archival books, the dried paper undergoes Dojeon : stacks of slightly dampened Hanji sheets brushed with diluted rice starch are beaten hundreds of times under a heavy wooden fulling stamp or stone mallet. Dojeon compacts the porous fiber web into a dense, satin-smooth, ink-receptive surface that shines like ivory and blocks moisture and bookworms. Because Hanji is simultaneously strong, breathable, thermally insulating, and translucent, traditional Koreans used it for everything from castle windows to battle armor! pasted over lattice doors (changhoji), Hanji acts as a passive membrane: blocking winter wind while letting fresh oxygen and humidity diffuse in and out of the room and scattering harsh sunlight into a soft warm glow.

Woven into twisted cords (jiseung), oiled with perilla oil into waterproof floors (jangpanji), or laminated in thirteen layers (ji-gabot), Hanji was even worn by Joseon border soldiers on the northern Amnok River—because thirteen plies of pounded mulberry paper stopped iron arrows and early musket balls in -25°C blizzards without freezing against the soldier’s skin like iron plate! In 2016 and 2020, world conservation history came full circle when the Louvre Museum in Paris and the Vatican Apostolic Library in Rome officially certified Mungyeong and Andong webal-ttugi Hanji to restore Leonardo da Vinci drawings, Maximilian II’s sixteenth-century desk, and ancient Byzantine manuscripts—proving that the thousand-year paper of Korean mountain valleys is now healing the fragile memory of the entire world.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Polymer Chemistry & Fiber Mechanics: Why 'Webal-ttugi' Creates Isotropic Strength

Paper conservation laboratories at the Vatican Library, the Institut National du Patrimoine in Paris, and Kookmin University tested traditional Korean webal-ttugi Hanji against wood-pulp and ssangbal papers. Because one-year-old Broussonetia kazinoki bast fibers are 12–18 mm long, cooked in mild potassium-carbonate plant ash (pH 7.8–8.5), beaten with wooden mallets rather than cut by steel blades, and couched with orthogonal front-to-back and side-to-side strokes, the tensile strength ratio between the longitudinal and transverse axes approaches 1:1 (isotropic strength).

2. The 1966 Seokgatap Discovery & The Louvre's 2017 Restoration Choice

Two dates anchor Hanji's global standing. First, the 1966 unearthing of the pre-751 CE Mugujeonggwang Daedaranigyeong scroll inside Bulguksa's Seokgatap Pagoda demonstrated 1,200-year survival under fluctuating humidity. Second, beginning in 2016–2017, paper conservators at the Louvre Museum used ultra-thin, 2-gram-per-square-meter Mungyeong Hanji to hinge and mend Renaissance drawings and sixteenth-century Boulle marquetry because its long, neutral-pH fibers conform invisibly to fragile surfaces without shrinking.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three papermaking terms in mind: first, Dakpul, the viscous Sunset Hibiscus root mucilage that suspends mulberry fibers evenly in cold water and vanishes upon drying; second, Webal-ttugi, the uniquely Korean single-cord screen technique that weaves fibers in perpendicular directions; and third, Dojeon, the fulling process of pounding finished paper stacks to impart a dense, glossy archival sheen.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit the Mungyeong Traditional Hanji Workshop of Living National Treasure Kim Sam-sik and Kim Chun-ho in Mungyeong, North Gyeongsang Province, the Andong Hanji Factory and Museum near Hahoe Village, or the Jeonju Hanji Museum in Deokjin-gu, Jeonju—where you can dip a bamboo screen into cold dakpul water and try the webal-ttugi swing yourself.
