Makoto Hagiwara's Japanese Tea Garden in Golden Gate Park (1894–1942) and the Drum Bridge

San Francisco — How a Yamanashi immigrant built the oldest public Japanese garden in America, popularized the fortune cookie, and was forcibly evicted to Tanforan and Topaz under Executive Order 9066 (1894 California Midwinter Fair to WWII Internment & 2020s Redress) • 9 min read

“My grandfather planted every cherry tree, built the Moon Bridge, and lived in the garden for forty-eight years—and then they gave our family forty-eight hours to pack two suitcases.” — Takako Hagiwara (1994)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Nestled beside the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park at 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive, the five-acre Japanese Tea Garden is the oldest public Japanese garden in the United States. Created for the 1894 California Midwinter International Exposition and expanded over forty-eight years by Yamanashi-born landscape designer and restaurateur Makoto Hagiwara (1854–1925) and his descendants, the garden introduced American visitors to the steep semi-circular wooden Drum Bridge (Taiko Bashi), centuries-old dwarf bonsai, and the modern vanilla-and-butter fortune cookie (adapted from Japanese tsujiura senbei around 1914). In April 1942, under Executive Order 9066, the City of San Francisco summarily terminated the Hagiwara family's lease and incarcerated them in the Tanforan and Topaz concentration camps without compensation.

In the autumn of 1893, as a severe national financial panic gripped the United States, San Francisco Chronicle publisher Michael H. de Young rallied the city to host the 1894 California Midwinter International Exposition across sixty acres of Golden Gate Park's sandy central hollow—proving to shivering Easterners that flowers bloomed outdoors in San Francisco in January. Among the exposition's most popular concessions was a one-acre 'Japanese Village' enclosed by a remarkable fence fabricated without a single iron nail out of gnarled cedar bark and bamboo, featuring a traditional two-story thatched-roof Teahouse built by Japanese joiners and landscaped with stepping stones, koi ponds, and miniature waterfalls.

When the Midwinter Fair closed in July 1894 and demolition crews began clearing the temporary pavilions around the Music Concourse, Makoto Hagiwara—a wealthy immigrant from Yamanashi Prefecture who had arrived in San Francisco in 1878, operated cafes, and contributed design expertise and funding to the Japanese Village—struck a handshake agreement with Park Superintendent John McLaren to maintain the one-acre garden permanently. Over the next three decades until his death in 1925, Makoto Hagiwara invested his own personal fortune to expand the garden from one acre to five full acres, importing thousands of flowering cherry trees (sakura), azaleas, wisteria arbors, Japanese maples, and South Sea goldfish directly from nurseries in Yokohama and Tokyo alongside a flock of white storks and mandarin ducks.

In 1907, after the April 1906 earthquake damaged their downtown home, the Park Commission invited Makoto Hagiwara, his wife, his daughter Takano, and her children to build a permanent multi-story Japanese family residence inside the western corner of the Tea Garden—where three generations of Hagiwaras lived on-site, pruning pines at dawn and greeting millions of guests in silk kimonos. To anchor the garden's winding pathways around the classical Edo-period principle of shakkei ('borrowed scenery'—framing distant Monterey pines of Golden Gate Park so the five-acre garden felt boundless), Hagiwara commissioned Japanese master carpenter Shinshichi Nakatani around 1910 to construct the steeply arched wooden Drum Bridge (Taiko Bashi).

When the semi-circular wooden arch of the Drum Bridge reflects in the still water of the pond below, the timber arc and its watery reflection form a complete circle—symbolizing the full moon and the Buddhist transition from the noisy secular world into quiet mindfulness as climbers brace their feet against the wooden cleats. Around 1914 (just before the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition), Makoto Hagiwara decided to serve a crisp, folded wafer alongside green jasmine tea at the thatched Teahouse to replace heavier cakes: adapting the centuries-old Kyoto tradition of tsujiura senbei (savory miso-and-sesame crackers containing printed paper fortunes baked near Fushimi Inari Shrine), Hagiwara sweetened the batter with butter and vanilla.

When attendance at the Tea Garden soared so high that the Hagiwara women could no longer fold enough cookies by hand over round iron kata grills inside the garden, Hagiwara contracted with San Francisco's Benkyodo Confectionery in Japantown to mass-produce the sweet vanilla 'fortune tea cakes'—launching the modern American fortune cookie from Golden Gate Park to the world. When the 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition closed in the Marina District, the Hagiwaras acquired the fair's towering five-tiered red Shinto Pagoda Shrine and massive carved wooden South Gate, moving them into Golden Gate Park, where they were joined in 1949 by the ten-foot-tall Amida Bronze Buddha (originally cast in Tajima, Japan, in 1790 and donated by the Gump brothers).

Then came December 7, 1941, and President Franklin D. Roosevelt's signing of Executive Order 9066 on February 19, 1942: despite forty-eight years of selfless civic service to San Francisco, the Park Commission summarily canceled the Hagiwara family's lifetime stewardship agreement in April 1942 and gave Makoto's daughter Takano and her children forty-eight hours to vacate their home inside the garden. Forbidden to take more than they could carry in two suitcases, the Hagiwara family was forced to leave behind decades of family heirlooms, antique lacquerware, rare ceramic pots, and centuries-old dwarf bonsai trees inside their garden house before being bused first to horse stalls at the Tanforan Assembly Center in San Bruno and then behind barbed wire at the Topaz War Relocation Center in the Utah desert.

Japanese garden design, joinery of the semi-circular Taiko Bashi Drum Bridge, and tsujiura senbei confectionery. While the Hagiwaras were locked inside the alkaline dust storms of Topaz, Utah, the San Francisco Park Commission tore down the Hagiwara family home inside the garden, auctioned or lost their personal possessions, stripped the word 'Japanese' from the entrance sign to rename it the 'Oriental Tea Garden,' and hired Chinese American waitresses dressed in cheongsams so wartime tourists would not boycott the teahouse. When the war ended in 1945 and the Hagiwaras were released from Topaz, the San Francisco Park Commission flatly refused to allow the family to return to their home or resume management of the garden their grandfather had built and funded—nor did the city pay a single dollar of compensation for the seized improvements.

In 1952, immediately after the Treaty of San Francisco formally restored peace between the United States and Japan (signed just across the park and downtown at the War Memorial Opera House), the Park Commission reinstated the name 'Japanese Tea Garden' and commissioned Kyoto landscape master Nagao Sakurai to design the dry-landscape Zen contemplation garden (karesansui) of raked gravel and stone islands. In the decades since, tireless organizing by Makoto's descendants, Japanese American historians, and Supervisor Sandra Lee Fewer secured the renaming of the adjacent park road as 'Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive,' the installation of the 1974 bronze plaque by Ruth Asawa honoring Makoto Hagiwara near the entrance gate, and a formal City Hall apology and redress study group—honoring the family whose hands shaped every stone and cherry blossom in the glen.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From Kyoto Tsujiura Senbei to the 1914 Golden Gate Park Fortune Cookie

Why are fortune cookies almost unknown in China yet ubiquitous in American Chinese restaurants? Historical research by Yasuko Nakamachi traced the cookie directly to nineteenth-century Kyoto shrine bakeries making tsujiura senbei—folded crackers pinched around a paper fortune on hand-held iron tongs. Brought to California by Japanese immigrants including Makoto Hagiwara at the Japanese Tea Garden (and Seiichi Kito in Los Angeles), the recipe was sweetened with vanilla and butter for American palates before Chinese American bakeries took over commercial production when Japanese American bakers were interned in 1942. At 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the northern edge of the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park in Golden Gate Park (Music Concourse Precinct), this synthesis of shakkei ('borrowed scenery') Japanese garden design, joinery of the semi-circular Taiko Bashi Drum Bridge, and tsujiura senbei confectionery and civic memory remains visible at every turn.

2. The Mechanics of the Taiko Bashi (Drum Bridge) and Shakkei

Built by carpenter Shinshichi Nakatani for the Hagiwara family, the garden's wooden Drum Bridge (Taiko Bashi) rises in a steep semi-circle so that its timber arch and water reflection complete a full drum-like circle. Its steep pitch is intentional: climbers cannot rush across while staring at their phones or shoes; they must slow down, grip the polished cedar handrail, and shift their gaze at the apex to take in the entire shakkei ('borrowed scenery') of the pond and pagoda.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this story with three essential landmarks inside the gate: first, Ruth Asawa's 1974 bronze commemorative plaque on the boulder just inside the main entrance honoring Makoto Hagiwara and his family (1894–1942); second, the 1790 Tajima Bronze Amida Buddha cast during the Edo Period and gifted by S. & G. Gump Company in 1949; and third, Nagao Sakurai's 1953 Karesansui Zen rock garden commemorating the 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Enter through the hand-carved wooden East Gate at 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the north side of the Golden Gate Park Music Concourse. Pause immediately on your left to read Ruth Asawa's bronze plaque dedicated to the Hagiwara family, climb carefully over the wooden cleats of the Taiko Bashi Drum Bridge, view the 1790 Bronze Buddha and five-tiered pagoda, and sit at the open side of the thatched Teahouse for green tea and fortune cookies.
