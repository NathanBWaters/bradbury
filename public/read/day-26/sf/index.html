Makoto Hagiwara's Japanese Tea Garden in Golden Gate Park (1894–1942) and the Drum Bridge

San Francisco — How a Yamanashi immigrant built the oldest public Japanese garden in America, popularized the fortune cookie, and was forcibly evicted to Tanforan and Topaz under Executive Order 9066 (1894 California Midwinter Fair to WWII Internment & 2020s Redress) • 20 min read

“My grandfather planted every cherry tree, built the Moon Bridge, and lived in the garden for forty-eight years—and then they gave our family forty-eight hours to pack two suitcases.” — Takako Hagiwara (1994)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Nestled beside the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park at 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive (37.7702° N, 122.4704° W), the five-acre Japanese Tea Garden is the oldest public Japanese garden in the United States. Created for the 1894 California Midwinter International Exposition and expanded over forty-eight years by Yamanashi-born landscape designer and restaurateur Makoto Hagiwara (1854–1925) and his descendants, the garden introduced American visitors to the steep semi-circular wooden Drum Bridge (*Taiko Bashi*), centuries-old dwarf bonsai, and the modern vanilla-and-butter fortune cookie (adapted from Japanese *tsujiura senbei* around 1914). In April 1942, under Executive Order 9066, the City of San Francisco summarily terminated the Hagiwara family's lease and incarcerated them in the Tanforan and Topaz concentration camps without compensation.

Standing today at 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the northern edge of the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park in the heart of Golden Gate Park (Music Concourse Precinct) (37.7702° N, 122.4704° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of 1894 California Midwinter Fair to WWII Internment & 2020s Redress. In the autumn of 1893, as a severe national financial panic gripped the United States, San Francisco Chronicle publisher Michael H. de Young rallied the city to host the 1894 California Midwinter International Exposition across sixty acres of Golden Gate Park's sandy central hollow—proving to shivering Easterners that flowers bloomed outdoors in San Francisco in January. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how landscape designer Makoto Hagiwara (1854–1925), his daughter Takano Hagiwara, and bridge builder Shinshichi Nakatani and a generation of San Franciscans confronted transforming a temporary 1894 World's Fair concession into a five-acre masterpiece of Edo-style landscape art and reclaiming the family's historical legacy after wartime internment, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Japanese Tea Garden, the Wooden Drum Bridge (*Taiko Bashi*), and the 1790 Bronze Buddha emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped Golden Gate Park (Music Concourse Precinct). Among the exposition's most popular concessions was a one-acre 'Japanese Village' enclosed by a remarkable fence fabricated without a single iron nail out of gnarled cedar bark and bamboo, featuring a traditional two-story thatched-roof Teahouse built by Japanese joiners and landscaped with stepping stones, koi ponds, and miniature waterfalls. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the northern edge of the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during January 27, 1894 (Midwinter Fair opening), 1914 (fortune cookie introduction), and April 1942 (WWII eviction). When the Midwinter Fair closed in July 1894 and demolition crews began clearing the temporary pavilions around the Music Concourse, Makoto Hagiwara—a wealthy immigrant from Yamanashi Prefecture who had arrived in San Francisco in 1878, operated cafes, and contributed design expertise and funding to the Japanese Village—struck a handshake agreement with Park Superintendent John McLaren to maintain the one-acre garden permanently. Contemporary observers who walked past 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the northern edge of the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood landscape designer Makoto Hagiwara (1854–1925), his daughter Takano Hagiwara, and bridge builder Shinshichi Nakatani, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of *shakkei* ('borrowed scenery') Japanese garden design, joinery of the semi-circular *Taiko Bashi* Drum Bridge, and *tsujiura senbei* confectionery proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Over the next three decades until his death in 1925, Makoto Hagiwara invested his own personal fortune to expand the garden from one acre to five full acres, importing thousands of flowering cherry trees (*sakura*), azaleas, wisteria arbors, Japanese maples, and South Sea goldfish directly from nurseries in Yokohama and Tokyo alongside a flock of white storks and mandarin ducks. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

Around 1914 (just before the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition), Makoto Hagiwara decided to serve a crisp, folded wafer alongside green jasmine tea at the thatched Teahouse to replace heavier cakes: adapting the centuries-old Kyoto tradition of *tsujiura senbei* (savory miso-and-sesame crackers containing printed paper fortunes baked near Fushimi Inari Shrine), Hagiwara sweetened the batter with butter and vanilla. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. From Kyoto *Tsujiura Senbei* to the 1914 Golden Gate Park Fortune Cookie

Why are fortune cookies almost unknown in China yet ubiquitous in American Chinese restaurants? Historical research by Yasuko Nakamachi traced the cookie directly to nineteenth-century Kyoto shrine bakeries making *tsujiura senbei*—folded crackers pinched around a paper fortune on hand-held iron tongs. Brought to California by Japanese immigrants including Makoto Hagiwara at the Japanese Tea Garden (and Seiichi Kito in Los Angeles), the recipe was sweetened with vanilla and butter for American palates before Chinese American bakeries took over commercial production when Japanese American bakers were interned in 1942. At 75 Hagiwara Tea Garden Drive on the northern edge of the Music Concourse in Golden Gate Park in Golden Gate Park (Music Concourse Precinct), this synthesis of *shakkei* ('borrowed scenery') Japanese garden design, joinery of the semi-circular *Taiko Bashi* Drum Bridge, and *tsujiura senbei* confectionery and civic memory remains visible at every turn.

2. The Mechanics of the *Taiko Bashi* (Drum Bridge) and *Shakkei*

Built by carpenter Shinshichi Nakatani for the Hagiwara family, the garden's wooden Drum Bridge (*Taiko Bashi*) rises in a steep semi-circle so that its timber arch and water reflection complete a full drum-like circle. Its steep pitch is intentional: climbers cannot rush across while staring at their phones or shoes; they must slow down, grip the polished cedar handrail, and shift their gaze at the apex to take in the entire *shakkei* ('borrowed scenery') of the pond and pagoda.
