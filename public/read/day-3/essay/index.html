Bradbury 1000 • Night 3 • Nightly Essay

Of Idleness

Michel de Montaigne (1580) • 7 min read

Retiring to his château tower to let his mind rest in total idleness, Montaigne discovers that an unoccupied mind behaves like a runaway horse and breeds fantastic monsters—until he begins to write.

As we see some grounds that have long lain idle and untilled, when grown rich and fertile by rest, to abound with and spend their virtue in the product of innumerable sorts of weeds and wild herbs that are unprofitable, and that to make them perform their true office, we are to cultivate and prepare them for such seeds as are proper for our service; and as we see women that, without knowledge of man, do sometimes of themselves bring forth inanimate and formless lumps of flesh, but that to cause a natural and perfect generation they are to be husbanded with another kind of seed: even so it is with minds, which if not applied to some certain study that may fix and restrain them, run into a thousand extravagances, eternally roving here and there in the vague expanse of the imagination—

Sicut aqua tremulum labris ubi lumen ahenis, Sole repercussum, aut radiantis imagine lunae, Omnia pervolitat late loca; jamque sub auras Erigitur, summique ferit laquearia tecti. [“As when in brazen vats of water the trembling beams of light, reflected from the sun, or from the image of the radiant moon, swiftly float over every place around, and now are darted up on high, and strike the ceilings of the upmost roof.”—AEneid, viii. 22.] —in which wild agitation there is no folly, nor idle fancy they do not light upon:— Velut aegri somnia, vanae Finguntur species. [“As a sick man’s dreams, creating vain phantasms.”—Hor., De Arte Poetica, 7.] The soul that has no established aim loses itself, for, as it is said— Quisquis ubique habitat, Maxime, nusquam habitat. [“He who lives everywhere, lives nowhere.”—Martial, vii. 73.]

When I lately retired to my own house, with a resolution, as much as possibly I could, to avoid all manner of concern in affairs, and to spend in privacy and repose the little remainder of time I have to live, I fancied I could not more oblige my mind than to suffer it at full leisure to entertain and divert itself, which I now hoped it might henceforth do, as being by time become more settled and mature; but I find— Variam semper dant otia mentem, [“Leisure ever creates varied thought.”—Lucan, iv. 704] that, quite contrary, it is like a horse that has broke from his rider, who voluntarily runs into a much more violent career than any horseman would put him to, and creates me so many chimaeras and fantastic monsters, one upon another, without order or design, that, the better at leisure to contemplate their strangeness and absurdity, I have begun to commit them to writing, hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

In 1571, on his thirty-eighth birthday, Michel Eyquem de Montaigne (1533–1592) resigned his seat as a magistrate in the Parlement of Bordeaux amidst the bloody French Wars of Religion and retired to his ancestral château in Périgord. Having an inscription painted on the wall of his third-floor circular tower library dedicating his remaining years to 'freedom, tranquility, and leisure' alongside the thousand books inherited from his beloved late friend Étienne de La Boétie, Montaigne expected serene quietude. Instead, his restless mind erupted into anxiety and wild daydreams. Out of that shock he invented a wholly new literary form—the Essai (from the French essayer, 'to try, test, or weigh')—and 'Of Idleness' (Book I, Chapter 8) is the very essay in which he explains why he started writing.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Montaigne's discovery is one of the great breakthroughs in human psychology: leisure without focus is not peace; it is mental chaos. He illustrates this through three unforgettable metaphors: fertile soil left unplowed that chokes itself with 'innumerable sorts of weeds,' light reflecting off water in a brass basin dancing wildly across a ceiling, and a 'runaway horse' that has broken loose from its rider. When we step away from external busywork without giving the mind a chosen subject ('some certain subject that shall bridle and control them'), the imagination breeds 'chimeras and fantastic monsters.' His cure is equally revolutionary: instead of repressing those wandering thoughts, write them down in a register and converse with great books so the mind can observe—and laugh at—its own absurdity.

3. Craft, Form & Rhetorical Technique

Unlike Francis Bacon's clipped, authoritative aphorisms, Montaigne writes in an intimate, meandering, self-deprecating conversational voice that mimics a mind thinking aloud in real time. His opening sentence is a grand Renaissance period stacking two earthy biological analogies before landing on the simple four-word hinge: 'so it is with our minds.' He seamlessly weaves Latin tags from Lucan and Horace and anecdotes of Socrates into candid confession ('Had I not experienced this in my own person...'), culminating in his famous formulation from Book II: 'I have no more made my book than my book has made me.' Every night you spend weighing an essay's central metaphor against a short story and a poem trains your own intellect to see beneath the surface of convention and discover original connections across centuries of human thought.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Translated into English by John Florio in 1603, Montaigne's Essais directly shaped William Shakespeare (who quoted Montaigne verbatim in The Tempest and echoed his self-questioning in Hamlet), Blaise Pascal, Friedrich Nietzsche, Virginia Woolf, and Stefan Zweig. 'Of Idleness' stands as the foundational origin story of modern autobiographical nonfiction, cognitive-behavioral journaling, and mindfulness: four hundred years before modern neuroscience discovered the 'Default Mode Network' (the brain network that ruminates anxiously when not engaged in a task), Montaigne mapped it in his tower. 5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 3, Montaigne's runaway horse and 'chimeras and fantastic monsters' collide with James Joyce's 'Araby' and Robert Frost's 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening.' Look at the three horses and three solitary minds: in Joyce's story, a boy's unbridled imagination turns a mundane bazaar and a neighbor girl into a chalice of romance that shatters in the dark; in Frost's poem, the 'little horse' shakes its harness bells to pull the speaker back from the hypnotic dark woods toward his 'promises to keep'; and Montaigne shows us that giving your mind a nightly anchor—one story, one poem, one essay, and a brief reflection—keeps the millstone of your intellect grinding true grain instead of grinding itself.
