Of Idleness

Michel de Montaigne (1580) • 7 min read

Retiring to his château tower to let his mind rest in total idleness, Montaigne discovers that an unoccupied mind behaves like a runaway horse and breeds fantastic monsters—until he begins to write.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Michel de Montaigne's "Of Idleness" is a tiny essay with a huge consequence: it explains why the personal essay exists. Montaigne was a French nobleman and magistrate who, in 1571, at thirty-eight, retired from public life to his family estate, hoping to spend the rest of his days in peaceful reading. France was tearing itself apart in religious civil war, and he was exhausted. He had a round tower library, a thousand books, and the memory of his dearest friend, who had died years before. What he found, to his surprise, was that an idle mind does not rest. It runs wild. So he began writing his thoughts down to examine them, and called the results essais, meaning attempts or trials. This short chapter, from the first book he published in 1580, is the moment the habit begins. It is only a few hundred words, but it contains the seed of a form that includes nearly every personal essay, memoir and blog written since.

As we see some grounds that have long lain idle and untilled, when grown rich and fertile by rest, to abound with and spend their virtue in the product of innumerable sorts of weeds and wild herbs that are unprofitable, and that to make them perform their true office, we are to cultivate and prepare them for such seeds as are proper for our service; and as we see women that, without knowledge of man, do sometimes of themselves bring forth inanimate and formless lumps of flesh, but that to cause a natural and perfect generation they are to be husbanded with another kind of seed: even so it is with minds, which if not applied to some certain study that may fix and restrain them, run into a thousand extravagances, eternally roving here and there in the vague expanse of the imagination—

Sicut aqua tremulum labris ubi lumen ahenis, Sole repercussum, aut radiantis imagine lunae, Omnia pervolitat late loca; jamque sub auras Erigitur, summique ferit laquearia tecti. [“As when in brazen vats of water the trembling beams of light, reflected from the sun, or from the image of the radiant moon, swiftly float over every place around, and now are darted up on high, and strike the ceilings of the upmost roof.”—AEneid, viii. 22.] —in which wild agitation there is no folly, nor idle fancy they do not light upon:— Velut aegri somnia, vanae Finguntur species. [“As a sick man’s dreams, creating vain phantasms.”—Hor., De Arte Poetica, 7.] The soul that has no established aim loses itself, for, as it is said— Quisquis ubique habitat, Maxime, nusquam habitat. [“He who lives everywhere, lives nowhere.”—Martial, vii. 73.]

When I lately retired to my own house, with a resolution, as much as possibly I could, to avoid all manner of concern in affairs, and to spend in privacy and repose the little remainder of time I have to live, I fancied I could not more oblige my mind than to suffer it at full leisure to entertain and divert itself, which I now hoped it might henceforth do, as being by time become more settled and mature; but I find— Variam semper dant otia mentem, [“Leisure ever creates varied thought.”—Lucan, iv. 704] that, quite contrary, it is like a horse that has broke from his rider, who voluntarily runs into a much more violent career than any horseman would put him to, and creates me so many chimaeras and fantastic monsters, one upon another, without order or design, that, the better at leisure to contemplate their strangeness and absurdity, I have begun to commit them to writing, hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The essay is a single, tightly built thought, and it is worth walking through slowly. Montaigne begins with a comparison to land. Ground that has "long lain idle and untilled," he says, grows rich by resting, and then it fills with "innumerable sorts of weeds and wild herbs that are unprofitable." Fertility without direction does not produce a harvest; it produces a jungle. To get something useful, the farmer must "cultivate and prepare" the soil with "such seeds as are proper for our service."

Then he pushes the point further with an image that is startling, and not very comfortable to a modern ear. Women who have conceived without a man sometimes bring forth "formless lumps of flesh," whereas a proper birth requires the right seed. He is applying an old and mistaken piece of medical folklore, but his meaning is clear: the mind, left to itself, generates only shapeless matter, and real thought needs something to fertilize it, some subject or purpose.

The rest of the essay shows the same thing in the author's own life. A mind not "applied to some certain study that may fix and restrain" it runs into "a thousand extravagances, eternally roving here and there in the vague expanse of the imagination." To illustrate this he borrows a passage from the Roman poet Virgil, in which trembling light, reflected off a bronze basin of water, flickers across the walls and ceiling of a room. That is what an unoccupied mind looks like: bright, restless, and landing everywhere and nowhere. He adds a line from Horace comparing such thoughts to a sick man's dreams, and one from Martial: "He who lives everywhere, lives nowhere."

Then comes the personal confession. When he "lately retired" to his own house, intending "as much as possibly I could, to avoid all manner of concern in affairs," he assumed the kindest thing he could do for his mind was to let it entertain itself. Instead, "it is like a horse that has broke from his rider," running into "a much more violent career than any horseman would put him to." It creates "chimaeras and fantastic monsters, one upon another, without order or design."

The final sentence is the hinge of the whole essay and one of the most important in the history of writing. To "contemplate their strangeness and absurdity," he says, "I have begun to commit them to writing, hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself." Here is the entire invention: writing as a way of catching your own thoughts in the act. A thought on the page can be looked at, mocked, corrected. The aim is partly therapeutic and partly comic, since the goal is to make the mind blush. What looks like a note about laziness is actually the birth announcement of a new kind of book.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of writing, "Of Idleness" is quietly excellent, though it is also an early and slightly rough version of what Montaigne would later do better. Its strongest quality is the way it moves from general principle to private confession. The first half sounds almost like a gardening manual or a bit of moral philosophy. Then, with the words "When I lately retired," the voice becomes intimate and the argument becomes a story about the author. That turn, from a doctrine to a person, is the move that defines the personal essay, and he makes it in a few sentences.

The imagery is also vivid and well chosen. The weedy field is simple and exact. The runaway horse is the best of the three, because it contains a small irony: he thought he was giving his mind a gift of freedom, and it galloped away with him. The picture of light dancing across the ceiling, borrowed from Virgil, is gorgeous, and it makes a point about the mind that no abstract term would. A reader who has ever lain awake at night with thoughts bouncing around will recognize it at once.

There is wit here, too. The idea that he might write down his chimeras to make his mind "ashamed of itself" is both a serious method and a joke, and the tone, half rueful and half proud, is why people feel they know him. Montaigne never preaches that idleness is wicked; he reports an experiment that did not turn out as planned.

But the essay is uneven in places. The sentence about women and "formless lumps of flesh" is a weak point. It rests on an outdated notion, and the comparison is awkward, since the metaphor turns a human body into a mere illustration. It sits oddly beside the tender observation of his own mind. Modern readers often wince, and it is fair to say the image has aged badly.

There is also a mild overdependence on quotation. Four Latin tags in an essay of this length can feel like decoration, and in the version you heard they come with bracketed translations that interrupt the flow. In Montaigne's own time, this was a mark of learning and an invitation to share a classical heritage with the reader, but it can feel like clutter now. Finally, the essay is more of an announcement than a developed argument. It says that a mind needs an occupation, but it does not tell us what the right occupation would be, and its answer, writing, is delivered almost in passing. The mature Montaigne of the later essays, who wanders further and explores more, is a richer companion. Still, this small piece has the great virtue of showing the form being born.

III. The Impact It Had

The influence of "Of Idleness" is the influence of Montaigne's project as a whole, and this short chapter is its origin story. Montaigne's Essais, first published in 1580 and expanded repeatedly until his death in 1592, created a genre. Before him, there were treatises, sermons, letters, and collections of maxims. Nobody had written a book in which the main subject was the author's own mind, changing from page to page. By naming the practice essai, he gave later writers a word, and the word became the English "essay."

The direct line to English literature is traceable. Francis Bacon borrowed the title and form for his own essays, though he took them in a more compressed and public direction. The English translation by John Florio, published in 1603, was read by Shakespeare, and scholars have long seen echoes of Montaigne in some of his plays, most famously in the speech by Gonzalo in The Tempest, which draws on the essay on cannibals. Later, Montaigne's example runs through the essays of Hazlitt and Lamb, and onward.

In France, his legacy was immense. Pascal admired and argued with him, and Rousseau's Confessions, with its promise to show a man as he is, is unthinkable without him, though Rousseau claimed to be doing something unprecedented. Philosophers of skepticism, from Descartes onward, were shaped by the habits of doubt that Montaigne made fashionable. In the twentieth century, writers such as Virginia Woolf wrote lovingly about him, and the essayists of the personal and confessional kind, from Orwell to James Baldwin to Joan Didion, work in a house he built.

Culturally, the essay's idea, that writing is a way to look at yourself, has become so ordinary that we forget someone had to discover it. Journals, memoirs, blogs and even modern therapeutic practices that encourage writing down anxious thoughts all rest on the same insight. The popular biography by Sarah Bakewell, published in 2010 as How to Live, helped introduce a new generation to Montaigne as a kind of friend and guide.

In education, "Of Idleness" is often taught as an origin text. Teachers use it to show how the essay form began, how classical quotation worked in the Renaissance, and how a short piece can hold a large idea. It is also, increasingly, assigned in discussions of attention and mental health, because its central observation, that an unoccupied mind is not automatically a calm one, sounds uncannily modern in an age of restlessness and distraction.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this essay, and Montaigne generally, because he tells the truth about the inside of a head. Most writers of his time presented themselves as authorities. Montaigne presents himself as a man who tried to retire, expected peace, and got chaos. That admission is endearing. We recognize ourselves in the scene: the person who finally clears the calendar and then cannot stop worrying, or who sits down to rest and finds the mind racing through a thousand imagined conversations.

They love the images. The runaway horse, the unplowed field and the shimmering light on the ceiling have all stayed vivid for centuries because they turn a vague experience into something you can see. The comparison of the mind to light reflected from water in a basin is especially admired. Readers often say it feels like a photograph of what it is to be distracted.

They love the humor and humility of the ending. The idea that he writes in order to make his mind "ashamed of itself" captures a gentle self-mockery that runs through the Essais. He does not set himself up as a model. Over and over in the book, he says he is writing about himself because he is the subject he knows best, and that there is no reason to take him as an authority. For many readers this is a relief after more solemn moral writing.

There is also a deep appeal in the way the essay recasts idleness. People often think of idleness as a failing to be cured by effort. Montaigne's answer is more interesting: the problem is not that you are not busy, but that your mind has no object. The solution is not frantic activity but attention, giving thought a shape. Readers have found this useful, especially those who live with anxiety or who struggle to relax.

Finally, the essay is loved for its sense of friendship across time. Montaigne wrote in a tower in the sixteenth century, during a civil war, and yet the voice feels warm and immediate. Many readers describe it as being addressed to them personally. It is common to hear people say that reading Montaigne is like talking with a clever, honest friend who happens to have lived four hundred years ago. This short chapter is where that friendship begins.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Montaigne has always had critics, and some of their objections apply here. The most famous came from the French thinker Blaise Pascal, who admired much in him but wrote of "the foolish project he had of portraying himself." To Pascal, a deeply religious man, a book that makes the author's own mind the subject was self-indulgent, and distracting from God. Other moralists, including some in the seventeenth-century Catholic tradition, felt his emphasis on the self and his easygoing skepticism were dangerous. The Essais were placed on the Catholic Church's Index of banned books in 1676.

A second charge is privilege. Montaigne could retire at thirty-eight because he owned an estate, had servants, and had inherited wealth. His complaint that idleness produces monsters is the complaint of a man for whom idleness was available. A farmer or a laborer in sixteenth-century France was not worried about having too little to do. Critics have noted that the essay's picture of leisure as a problem to be solved by thoughtful writing is a very aristocratic worry, and that the great themes of the surrounding violence, the wars of religion that were killing his neighbors, are almost absent from this passage.

Third, there is the question of method. Some readers have found that the essay is thin as philosophy. It makes an observation and offers a remedy, but the remedy is not argued, and it quietly assumes that writing will improve the mind. Anyone who has seen a journal turn into an exercise in rumination might doubt it. Writing down monsters can feed them as easily as tame them. The essay does not entertain that possibility.

Then there are specific details that have aged badly. The metaphor of women producing "formless lumps of flesh" draws on a medical belief that is false, and it casts women as passive vessels in an essay about the mind. Even sympathetic readers tend to skip past it. And the heavy use of classical quotation, a normal practice in the Renaissance, has been seen by some as a way of hiding behind authority, which is ironic in a writer who claims to be exploring himself.

Finally, there is a view that the essay overstates its own originality. Montaigne is often credited with inventing the essay, but he drew on Plutarch, Seneca, and a rich tradition of commonplace books. In this reading, "Of Idleness" is less a birth than a clever repackaging. That is probably too harsh, but it is a reminder that nothing begins entirely from nothing.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Montaigne belongs to Renaissance humanism, the movement that revived Greek and Roman writing and placed human experience at the center of study. His particular school was a skeptical, practical one, influenced by Stoics such as Seneca, by Plutarch's lively biographies and moral essays, and by the ancient skeptics whose doubts he brought into French. Within this tradition, "Of Idleness" is typical in drawing on Latin poets for its illustrations and on classical ideas about the discipline of the mind.

What came after is much of modern literature. The personal essay, the memoir, the confessional poem, the diary, and the autobiographical novel all have some debt. In English, the line runs from Bacon through Addison and Johnson to Lamb, Hazlitt, Orwell and Woolf, and in America to Emerson, Thoreau, and the present-day personal essayists. In French, from Pascal through Rousseau to Proust, who turns the study of a single mind into a whole novel.

It is revealing to compare Montaigne with the other writers on reading and attention in this tradition. Bacon, who borrowed the form, uses it to give counsel; Montaigne uses it to ask questions. Where Bacon is a judge, Montaigne is a fellow patient. William James, studying habit, explains in scientific terms why a mind without settled channels runs wild, which is what Montaigne describes in his tower. Emerson, in the woods, finds in solitude a joy that Montaigne found only chaos in, until he started to write. Woolf, who adored Montaigne, shares his belief that a person is best understood through the shifting surface of daily thought.

Why does "Of Idleness" still matter? Partly because it is the beginning of something. If you want to know where the voice of the first-person essay came from, here is the room, the tower, and the moment. But also because it offers a surprisingly current insight. We live in a time when many people are, in a sense, retired from patience, with endless free moments filled by screens, and often they are not rested but agitated. Montaigne would not be surprised. His advice is not to stop thinking but to give thought something to hold. Writing is one remedy, and you may find another. What matters is the discovery that the mind, left alone, is not a quiet room but a crowded and curious one, and that you can learn a great deal by writing down who is inside.
