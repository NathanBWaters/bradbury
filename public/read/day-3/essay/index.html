Of Idleness and the Untamed Mind

Michel de Montaigne (1580) • 7 min read

Retiring to his château tower to let his mind rest in total idleness, Montaigne discovers that an unoccupied mind behaves like a runaway horse and breeds fantastic monsters—until he begins to write.

As we see some grounds that have long lain idle and untilled, if they be grown rich and fertile by rest, to abound with and spend their virtue in the product of innumerable sorts of weeds and wild herbs that are unprofitable, and of naught unless they be properly laboured and sown with certain seeds for our service; and as we see women that, without the knowledge of men, do sometimes of themselves bring forth inanimate and formless lumps of flesh, showing that nature is ever working even when it lacks a proper mould: so it is with our minds. If we do not occupy them with some certain subject that shall bridle and control them, they cast themselves disorderly hither and thither into the vague field of imagination.

In that wide and boundless expanse there is no folly nor idle fancy which they do not produce in the agitation of their own liberty. As the poet Lucan says of the waters trembling in a brazen vessel when struck by the sun, sending quivering reflections dancing erratically across the vaulted ceiling, so the mind, having no fixed mark to aim at, loses itself in its own motion. For the soul that has no established aim loses itself; because, as the proverb says, to be everywhere is to be nowhere.

Had I not experienced this in my own person, I should hardly have believed how wild and untamable a creature the human spirit is when left to its own devices. Lately, when I retired myself to my own house, with a resolution, as much as possibly I could, to avoid all manner of concern in affairs, and to spend in privacy and repose the little remainder of time I have to live, I fancied I could not more oblige my mind than to suffer it at full leisure to entertain and divert itself, and to settle and stay in itself, which I hoped it might now the more easily do, as being by time become more settled and ripe, and no longer green or hot with youth.

But I find—variam semper dant otia mentem, as Lucan writes: idleness ever gives a wavering mind—that, quite on the contrary, like a horse that has broke from his rider and runs away, my mind gives itself a hundred times more career and liberty than it used to take for others, and begets in me so many chimeras and fantastic monsters, one upon the neck of another, without order or design, that the better at leisure to contemplate their strangeness and absurdity, I have begun to keep a register of them, hoping with time to make my mind ashamed of itself.

Consider how we treat our other faculties when we wish them to serve us well: no horseman expects a spirited courser to move with grace unless he feels the gentle pressure of the bit and the guidance of the rein; no gardener expects a vine to bear sweet grapes if he leaves its shoots to straggle wild across the brambles. Yet men foolishly imagine that retirement from public office, from the law courts, or from the bustle of the marketplace automatically bestows peace upon the understanding. They forget that when we flee the crowd, we carry our most restless companion inside our own breast.

Ambition, avarice, irresolution, fear, and inordinate desires do not leave us because we have changed our country or locked the door of our study; as Horace says, they oftener follow us even into the cloisters and the schools of philosophy. Neither deserts, nor hollow rocks, nor hair-shirts, nor fastings can disentangle us from them if the mind itself lacks an anchor. Socrates was told that a certain man had grown no whit the better by his travels. 'I very well believe it,' said he, 'for he took himself along with him.'

Therefore it is not enough to have gotten aloof from the populace; it is not enough to shift from one place to another; we must get aloof from the popular conditions that are within us; we must sequester and recover ourselves from ourselves. A wise solitude is not a vacuum in which the soul slumbers or wanders blindfold; it is an inner back-shop—an arrière-boutique—wholly our own, entirely free, wherein we establish our true liberty and principal retreat, and wherein we converse with ourselves and with the wisest minds of every age upon some chosen theme.

Plutarch tells us of those who, having neither wife nor children of their own, lavish their affection upon puppies and monkeys; and just so our soul, if it is not furnished with a worthy and substantial object upon which to exercise its native vigour, entangles itself in a thousand vain and frivolous imaginations. A man who has never learned how to converse with a book or how to examine his own nature in quietness will find an hour of rainy weather in a country house more insupportable than a campaign in the trenches. He paces from window to door, counts the flies upon the ceiling, calls for his boots only to pull them off again, and flees back into the noise of the street not because he loves company, but because he cannot endure his own.

When the mind is left without a definite book, problem, or meditation upon which to fasten its teeth, it begins to gnaw upon itself. Out of every slight rumour at the village gate, out of every shadow upon the tapestry or creak of the rafters at midnight, it fashions whole tragedies of apprehension and vain longing. It runs forward into the future to borrow griefs that have not yet arrived, or backward into the past to stir up the ashes of old regrets. Like a millstone that grinds itself to powder when there is no corn between the stones, an active intellect without a worthy object consumes its own peace.

Yet notice the strange remedy I stumbled upon almost by accident in my tower: by taking up the pen to record and examine these very chimeras and wandering monsters of my brain, and by testing my wayward humours against the histories and verses of Plutarch, Seneca, and Virgil, the very act of observation became a bridle. To look steadily at the disorder of one's own thoughts—to set them down in black and white upon the page without cosmetics or ceremony—is already to begin to tame them. I have no more made my book than my book has made me: a book consubstantial with its author, concerned with my own self, an integral part of my life.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

In 1571, on his thirty-eighth birthday, Michel Eyquem de Montaigne (1533–1592) resigned his seat as a magistrate in the Parlement of Bordeaux amidst the bloody French Wars of Religion and retired to his ancestral château in Périgord. Having an inscription painted on the wall of his third-floor circular tower library dedicating his remaining years to 'freedom, tranquility, and leisure' alongside the thousand books inherited from his beloved late friend Étienne de La Boétie, Montaigne expected serene quietude. Instead, his restless mind erupted into anxiety and wild daydreams. Out of that shock he invented a wholly new literary form—the Essai (from the French essayer, 'to try, test, or weigh')—and 'Of Idleness' (Book I, Chapter 8) is the very essay in which he explains why he started writing.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Montaigne's discovery is one of the great breakthroughs in human psychology: leisure without focus is not peace; it is mental chaos. He illustrates this through three unforgettable metaphors: fertile soil left unplowed that chokes itself with 'innumerable sorts of weeds,' light reflecting off water in a brass basin dancing wildly across a ceiling, and a 'runaway horse' that has broken loose from its rider. When we step away from external busywork without giving the mind a chosen subject ('some certain subject that shall bridle and control them'), the imagination breeds 'chimeras and fantastic monsters.' His cure is equally revolutionary: instead of repressing those wandering thoughts, write them down in a register and converse with great books so the mind can observe—and laugh at—its own absurdity.

3. Craft, Form & Rhetorical Technique

Unlike Francis Bacon's clipped, authoritative aphorisms, Montaigne writes in an intimate, meandering, self-deprecating conversational voice that mimics a mind thinking aloud in real time. His opening sentence is a grand Renaissance period stacking two earthy biological analogies before landing on the simple four-word hinge: 'so it is with our minds.' He seamlessly weaves Latin tags from Lucan and Horace and anecdotes of Socrates into candid confession ('Had I not experienced this in my own person...'), culminating in his famous formulation from Book II: 'I have no more made my book than my book has made me.' Every night you spend weighing an essay's central metaphor against a short story and a poem trains your own intellect to see beneath the surface of convention and discover original connections across centuries of human thought.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Translated into English by John Florio in 1603, Montaigne's Essais directly shaped William Shakespeare (who quoted Montaigne verbatim in The Tempest and echoed his self-questioning in Hamlet), Blaise Pascal, Friedrich Nietzsche, Virginia Woolf, and Stefan Zweig. 'Of Idleness' stands as the foundational origin story of modern autobiographical nonfiction, cognitive-behavioral journaling, and mindfulness: four hundred years before modern neuroscience discovered the 'Default Mode Network' (the brain network that ruminates anxiously when not engaged in a task), Montaigne mapped it in his tower. 5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 3, Montaigne's runaway horse and 'chimeras and fantastic monsters' collide with James Joyce's 'Araby' and Robert Frost's 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening.' Look at the three horses and three solitary minds: in Joyce's story, a boy's unbridled imagination turns a mundane bazaar and a neighbor girl into a chalice of romance that shatters in the dark; in Frost's poem, the 'little horse' shakes its harness bells to pull the speaker back from the hypnotic dark woods toward his 'promises to keep'; and Montaigne shows us that giving your mind a nightly anchor—one story, one poem, one essay, and a brief reflection—keeps the millstone of your intellect grinding true grain instead of grinding itself.
