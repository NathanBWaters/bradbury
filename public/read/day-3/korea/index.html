The Haenyeo of Jeju Island: Free-Diving Matriarchs of the Basalt Coast

Korea — Into the cold volcanic waters of Jeju, where seventy-year-old sea women hold their breath for two minutes and sing the song of the tide () • 20 min read

“We carry our coffins on our backs in the water, and leave our breath upon the waves.” — Traditional Jeju Haenyeo proverb

Key Context & Why It Matters

Along the black vesicular basalt coastlines of Jeju Island and Udo, a high, melodic whistle known as sumbisori rises across the cold swells of the Korea Strait whenever a seventy-five-year-old grandmother breaks the surface after two minutes on the seafloor. These are the Haenyeo ('sea women') of Jeju, a UNESCO-inscribed semi-matriarchal free-diving sisterhood whose members plunge up to twenty meters deep on a single human breath to harvest abalone, conch, sea urchin, and hijiki kelp without scuba tanks. Emerging during the seventeenth-century Joseon tribute system when male maritime labor was heavily taxed, the Haenyeo evolved extraordinary cold-water mammalian dive reflexes, splenic oxygen adaptations, and a strict cooperative commons ethic inside their seaside stone bulteok hearths that forbids compressed air to protect the island's volcanic reefs.

Walk out onto the jagged, vesicular black basalt ledges of Seongsan or Udo Island off the eastern coast of Jeju just as first light turns the Korea Strait the color of hammered pewter, and you will hear the sound long before you see a human face. Rising across the whitecaps comes a high, piercing, melodic whistle—*sumbisori* (숨비소리)—as eerie and vital as the cry of a curlew or a reed flute blown across an empty moor. A heartbeat later, twenty yards beyond the surf break, a bright orange spherical float (*tewak*) jerks against its tether, and a seventy-six-year-old grandmother breaks the surface in a black neoprene wetsuit. Spitting salt water from her lips, she flings a heavy iron prying chisel (*bitchang*), two prized horned turban shells (*sora*), and purple sea urchins into her woven net (*mangsari*), draws one deep breath, jackknifes at the waist, and kicks her rubber fins straight back down into forty feet of freezing volcanic current.

These are the *Haenyeo* (해녀, literally 'sea women', or *jamnyeo* 잠녀 in the Jeju tongue)—the free-diving matriarchs of Jeju Island and one of humanity's last authentic breath-hold foraging cultures. For more than four centuries, on a wind-scoured volcanic island fifty miles south of the Korean mainland, these women inverted the strict patriarchal hierarchy of East Asian Neo-Confucianism. Diving entirely without scuba tanks, compressed-air hoses, or mechanical thrusters, they plunge to depths of ten to twenty meters for up to two minutes on a single lungful of air, repeating ninety to a hundred dives across a five-hour shift (*muljil*) in waters that drop below ten degrees Celsius in midwinter. While their husbands historically stayed ashore to tend infants in woven bamboo cradles (*agi-gudeok*), mend dry-stone field walls, and cultivate barley and tangerines, the *haenyeo* paid for their children's university tuition, built their villages' schools and shrines, and governed the sea as a self-regulating female commons.

To understand why women rather than men became the masters of Jeju's ocean requires reading the geology of the island itself. Born from the Pleistocene eruptions of Mount Hallasan—the 1,947-meter shield volcano that crowns the center of the island—Jeju is paved with porous dark basalt (*hyeonmuam*) riddled with gas bubbles and subterranean lava tubes. When torrential summer monsoons fall on Hallasan's slopes, the rainwater does not collect in broad alluvial valleys as it does on the Korean mainland; instead, it drains instantly through the volcanic rock and *gotjawal* boulder forests until it bubbles out at the coastline as icy freshwater springs (*yongcheonsu*) right at the high-tide line. Because the porous soil could never hold standing water for wet-rice paddies, Jeju Islanders summarized their harsh homeland as *Samdado*—the 'Island of Three Abundances': wind, stone, and women. Against the incessant gales, islanders stacked 22,000 kilometers of black basalt field walls (*batdam*, the 'Black Dragon'), and for protein and cash income, they turned to the rich kelp forests and volcanic reefs of the surrounding sea (*badang*).

In early centuries, both men (*pojak-in*) and women dived along the Jeju coast. During the seventeenth century, however, the fiscal policies of the Joseon royal court triggered a profound demographic shift. Under King Injo and King Hyeonjong, the central government imposed crushing annual royal tributes (*gongnap*) of dried abalone (*jeonbok-cho*) and mother-of-pearl upon Jeju Island. Because the Joseon tax code levied tribute quotas directly upon registered adult males, Jeju's male deep-sea fishermen were relentlessly flogged by local magistrates whenever typhoons reduced their catch; thousands drowned at sea or fled the island, prompting King Injo in 1629 to impose the *Chuljeok-ryeong*—a two-century travel ban forbidding Jeju residents from leaving the island. Because women's labor was legally exempt from the male tribute tax, wives and daughters took over the diving reefs. By 1629, the exiled scholar Yi Geon observed in his *Jejupungtorok* (*Records of Jeju Customs*) that women dived with sickles to harvest seaweed, and by 1702, when Magistrate Yi Hyeong-sang commissioned the painted album *Tamna Sullyeokdo* (*Inspection Tour of Jeju*), royal registers recorded over two thousand female *jamnyeo* diving across the island's coves.

Biology reinforced what taxation had begun. Until the early 1970s, when Japanese-style black neoprene wetsuits (*gomu-ot*) were finally introduced to Jeju, every *haenyeo* plunged into the frigid winter swells wearing nothing but a *mulsojungi* (물소중이)—a thin, unlined white cotton one-shoulder tunic fastened along the side with adjustable buttons so a diver could loosen the cloth as she progressed through the ninth month of pregnancy—paired with a cotton headscarf (*mulsugeon*). In water temperatures between 10°C and 14°C, male divers rapidly succumbed to violent shivering and hypothermia, whereas women possessed a naturally higher layer of subcutaneous adipose tissue and superior peripheral vascular insulation that preserved core organ heat.

Since the 1960s, when physiologist Dr. Hong Seok-Kyu of Yonsei University and SUNY Buffalo set up field laboratories beside Jeju's coastal villages, and continuing into twenty-first-century genomic studies led by evolutionary biologists at UC Berkeley and Seoul National University, the bodies of the *haenyeo* have astonished medical science. During the pre-wetsuit era, Hong discovered that Jeju *haenyeo* exhibited the lowest shivering threshold ever measured in human beings, suppressing involuntary shivering until water temperatures dropped nearly four degrees lower than non-diving Koreans could tolerate. Even more dramatic is what happens the instant a *haenyeo* submerges her face and descends twenty meters under three atmospheres of pressure: her body triggers an extreme mammalian dive reflex rivaling that of Weddell seals. Within seconds, her heart rate plunges by up to fifty percent (profound bradycardia), peripheral capillaries in her limbs clamp shut to reserve oxygen for her brain and heart, and her spleen—which genomic sequencing reveals is significantly enlarged in Jeju diving families carrying natural-selection variants in the *PDE10A*, *EPAS1*, and *OXTR* genes—contracts like a muscular sponge, injecting a concentrated surge of oxygen-rich red blood cells directly into her circulation.

When those two minutes on the ocean floor expire and the diver kicks back toward the sunlight with lungs compressed by hydrostatic pressure and blood saturated with carbon dioxide, the most dangerous five seconds of free-diving begin: shallow-water blackout. As pressure drops near the surface, the partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs plummets. To purge toxic carbon dioxide and draw fresh oxygen into her alveoli in a fraction of a second without hyperventilating, the resurfaced *haenyeo* purses her lips and blasts air outward from deep in her diaphragm—producing the unmistakable *sumbisori* whistle. Out on the tossing swells, where fog and six-foot waves hide one bobbing orange *tewak* from the next, that whistle is also an acoustic lifeline: every diver within half a mile listens subconsciously for her sisters' *sumbisori*, knowing that a whistle means a friend has returned alive from the dark.

Onshore, the social heartbeat of every diving village is the *bulteok* (불턱)—a circular, roofless enclosure of stacked black basalt boulders built right above the surf line to block the salt wind. In the center of the stone ring burns a pit of driftwood and dried pine needles. For centuries, the *bulteok* served simultaneously as changing room, maternity ward, schoolhouse, and democratic parliament. Arriving three hours before low tide, divers from their thirties to their late eighties gather around the embers to warm their joints, sharpen their tools, and debate village business. Within the *bulteok* and the village *Haenyeo-hoe* (Divers' Association), mainland wealth and husband's lineage mean nothing; authority flows strictly from breath, courage, and ecological wisdom across a three-tiered sisterhood.

At the apex stand the *Sanggun* (상군, Top-Rank Divers)—elite veterans capable of diving below fifteen meters into treacherous offshore rip currents (*mulgil*), reading the subtle tilt of kelp fronds to predict underwater surges, harvesting prized deep-ledge abalone, and rescuing younger divers whose weight belts or nets snag on submerged rock overhangs. Below them are the *Junggun* (중군, Middle Rank), diving eight to twelve meters, and the *Hagun* (하군, Lower Rank), diving three to five meters in the nearshore shallows. Historically, a Jeju girl's apprenticeship began at age seven or eight in knee-deep tidepools (*ttong-jamnyeo*), learning to hold her breath and pry periwinkles from stones; by fifteen she received her first hollowed bottle-gourd *tewak* and iron *bitchang* as a *Hagun*; by her mid-twenties she rose to *Sanggun*, often diving until the afternoon her labor pains began—and occasionally delivering her baby right beside the *bulteok* fire, cutting the umbilical cord with a sharp reed or clean harvesting blade before wrapping the newborn in a warm cotton *mulsojungi*.

Every tool in a *haenyeo*'s kit is calibrated to human muscle and safety. Around her waist she buckles the *yeonchul* (연출), a belt of cast lead weights heavy enough to counteract the buoyancy of her wetsuit so she can glide downward without burning precious oxygen kicking against the surface. On the waves floats her *tewak* (테왁)—traditionally crafted from a giant dried bottle gourd (*bak*) sealed with persimmon tannin—from which hangs the *mangsari* catch net weighted at the bottom with a stone sinker. In her hand she grips the *bitchang* (빗창), a flat, slightly curved iron bar forged by village blacksmiths to resemble a willow leaf, tethered to her wrist by a braided cord. Because a wild abalone (*jeonbok*) clings to basalt crevices with a muscular foot capable of withstanding hundreds of pounds of pull, a diver has only a split second to slide the thin tip of her *bitchang* beneath the shell before the mollusk clamps down on the rock. Every elder diver carries stories of sisters whose fingers were pinned under a giant abalone or wedged inside a basalt fissure fifteen meters down, forced to use the iron *bitchang* to pry themselves free before their breath ran out.

Beneath the waves, where an outsider sees only uniform gray rock and swaying brown kelp, a *haenyeo* navigates an invisible, named landscape as detailed as any city street map. Every underwater ridge, cavern, sand patch, and boulder off a Jeju village bears a centuries-old proper name passed down orally around the *bulteok* fire—names like *Gureong-yeo* ('Serpent Reef'), *Halmi-yeo* ('Grandmother Ledge'), or *Neobun-yeo* ('Broad Flat Rock'). The *haenyeo* call the ocean floor *badang-bat* ('sea fields'), because they do not merely hunt the sea; they farm it.

Long before modern marine biologists coined the term 'sustainable fisheries management,' the Jeju *haenyeo* institutionalized one of the world's strictest ecological conservation codes through their village fishery cooperatives (*Eochongye*). First and most sacred is the technological ban: no diver may ever use a scuba tank, rebreather, or surface-supplied air hose. If a diver used compressed air, she could remain on the reef for an hour and strip every breeding abalone from the rock ledges in a week. By tethering harvesting power to the physiological limit of a single human breath, the *haenyeo* guarantee that the deepest reefs remain natural broodstock sanctuaries. Second, the *Eochongye* enforces strict seasonal closures (*geumryeonggi*): during the autumn spawning months of abalone and horned turban shells (*sora*), or the spring spore-release of *tot* (*hijiki*) and *miyeok* (*wakame*) seaweeds, harvesting those species is forbidden. Third, any abalone smaller than seven centimeters or *sora* smaller than five centimeters is returned immediately to the sea, and several times a year the village diving corps performs *gaetdakkgi*—underwater weeding and culling of predatory sea stars—before hand-seeding juvenile shellfish spat into sheltered crevices.

Just as remarkable is how the *haenyeo* distribute the wealth of the reefs to protect their aging elders. As a once-mighty *Sanggun* passes her seventy-fifth or eightieth birthday and her lung capacity shrinks, she steps down voluntarily to the *Hagun* rank. Rather than leaving these octogenarian grandmothers to compete with stronger divers in deep water, many Jeju villages partition their coastal waters to create a protected nearshore zone called the *Halttang-badang* or *Halmang-badang* ('Grandmothers' Sea'). Located in calm, shallow water right beside the harbor wall and seeded generously with conch and sea urchins by the cooperative, this shallow sanctuary is reserved exclusively for the oldest divers—ensuring that an eighty-five-year-old grandmother can still pull on her wetsuit, float on her *tewak* beside her lifelong friends, earn her own income, and never feel like a burden to her children.

Beginning in 1895, as commercial fishing boats encroached upon Korean waters, young Jeju *haenyeo* launched an extraordinary maritime diaspora known as *chulga* (출가, 'leaving home for the sea'). Boarding wooden sailing vessels every spring, thousands of teenage and twenty-something Jeju divers migrated seasonally to the rocky coasts of Busan, Ulleungdo, Dokdo, the Liaodong Peninsula in China, Vladivostok in Russian Siberia, and as far east as Miyakejima and Tokyo Bay in Japan. Bending over the heavy single sculling oar (*no*) of a wooden *tewoo* raft or coastal schooner, they sang *Ieodo Sana*—the haunting Jeju rowing ballad invoking *Ieodo*, the mythical underwater paradise island beyond the southern horizon where drowned sailors and divers know neither hunger nor cold. The cash remittances sent home by these *chulga haenyeo* became the backbone of Jeju's twentieth-century economy.

Yet the same solidarity that bound the *haenyeo* together in the water made them formidable revolutionaries on land. During the Japanese colonial occupation (1910–1945), colonial authorities took control of the Jeju Haenyeo Cooperative (*Haenyeo Johap*), appointing the Japanese island governor as its head and allowing Japanese middlemen to buy the divers' hard-won abalone and seaweed at half of market value. In the winter of 1931–1932, when corrupt inspectors in Hado-ri, Gujwa-eup, slashed prices yet again, three fearless young *Sanggun* divers in their early twenties—Bu Chun-hwa (age twenty-five), Kim Ok-ryeon (twenty-three), and Bu Deok-ryang (twenty-two), who had attended night school together in Hado-ri—organized a clandestine island-wide strike. On January 12, 1932, market day in Sehwa, more than a thousand *haenyeo* marched through the snow brandishing their iron *bitchang* chisels and *kkakuri* hooks, singing the 'Haenyeo Resistance Song' and surrounding the Japanese governor's motorcade until he agreed on the spot to meet their demands. Over three months, 17,130 *haenyeo* staged 238 coordinated demonstrations—the largest women-led anti-colonial uprising in Korean history.

Because every dive flirts with death among unpredictable currents and basalt overhangs, the *haenyeo* balance their fierce pragmatism with deep spiritual reverence. Each spring, during the second lunar month when *Yeongdeung Halmang* (the Goddess of the Wind) visits Jeju to sow the reefs with shellfish and kelp spores, the divers trade their black wetsuits for luminous silk *hanbok* and gather at the village seaside shrine (*Haesindang*) for the *Jamsugut* (잠수굿) shamanic ritual. As the village *simbang* (shaman) beats brass gongs and dances beneath paper streamers representing the Dragon King of the Sea (*Yongwang*), the women cast millet cakes (*dolrae-tteok*) and scatter seeds onto the waves, calling out the names of mothers and sisters who never surfaced and praying for calm tides. In 2016, UNESCO inscribed the 'Culture of Jeju Haenyeo' on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Today, with fewer than three thousand active divers remaining across Jeju—over eighty percent of them past age sixty-five—these grandmothers represent the final generation of a living matriarchy that proved human beings can harvest the wild ocean on the measure of a single breath.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Evolutionary Physiology: The Genetic Secret of the Haenyeo Spleen

Like the Bajau 'Sea Nomads' of Southeast Asia, the Haenyeo of Jeju Island have become a touchstone for human evolutionary biology. Research on elderly Jeju divers revealed that decades of cold-water breath-hold diving—combined with island-specific genetic variations—produce extraordinary cold-water basal metabolic adaptations and splenic contraction responses. Even in the era before rubber wetsuits (introduced in the late 1960s and 1970s), when divers spent winter shifts in thin cotton mulsojungi and warmed themselves afterward over open bulteok driftwood fires, their core temperature regulation exceeded every standard textbook limit for human hypothermia. 2. Ecological Ethics: Why Banning Scuba Tanks Saved Jeju's Reefs

Economist Elinor Ostrom won the Nobel Prize for demonstrating how local communities can manage 'the commons' without privatization or state coercion, and the Jeju Haenyeo Eochongye is one of the planet's purest examples. If a single diver used compressed air, she could strip an entire basalt reef of mature abalone in an afternoon.
