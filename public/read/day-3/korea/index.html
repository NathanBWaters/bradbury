The Haenyeo of Jeju Island: Free-Diving Matriarchs of the Basalt Coast

Korea — Into the cold volcanic waters of Jeju, where seventy-year-old sea women hold their breath for two minutes and sing the song of the tide (Joseon Era to Present (UNESCO Intangible Heritage, 2016)) • 4 min read

“We carry our coffins on our backs in the water, and leave our breath upon the waves.” — Traditional Jeju Haenyeo proverb

If you walk along the black vesicular basalt rocks of Seongsan or Udo Island off the eastern coast of Jeju just after dawn, you will hear the sound before you see who is making it. Out across the slate-gray swells of the Korea Strait rises a high, haunting, melodic whistle—*sumbisori* (숨비소리)—like the call of a dolphin or a reed flute carried on the wind. Seconds later, an orange spherical float (*tewak*) bobs violently, and a seventy-five-year-old grandmother breaks the surface in a black neoprene wetsuit, flinging a heavy iron harvesting hook (*bitchang*) and a handful of purple sea urchins and prized abalone into her net before drawing a deep breath and jackknifing back down into thirty feet of freezing water.

These are the *Haenyeo* (해녀, literally 'sea women') of Jeju Island, one of the world’s last authentic semi-matriarchal free-diving cultures. For centuries, on a volcanic island whose porous basalt soil could not hold standing water for traditional mainland rice paddies, the women of Jeju became the primary breadwinners of their households. Diving without oxygen tanks, snorkels, or mechanical propulsion, they plunge up to twenty meters deep for two to three minutes at a time, repeating up to a hundred dives over a five-hour shift while their husbands traditionally tended children, volcanic stone walls (*batdam*), and tangerine orchards onshore.

How did diving become exclusively women’s work on an island governed by Joseon Dynasty Neo-Confucianism? In the seventeenth century, King Hyeonjong’s royal court imposed crushing annual tributes of dried abalone (*jeonbok*) on Jeju Island. Because men were heavily taxed on their maritime labor and frequently conscripted onto royal warships or drowned while deep-sea fishing in typhoons, women gradually took over nearshore harvesting—which was exempt from the male tribute tax. Moreover, physiological reality favored the female divers: a higher ratio of subcutaneous body fat provided crucial thermal insulation in 10°C winter waters when divers wore only thin white hand-woven cotton tops (*mulsojungi*) before wetsuits arrived in the 1970s.

Physiologists from Seoul National University and international marine laboratories have studied the Jeju Haenyeo in awe. When a *haenyeo* submerges, her body triggers an extreme mammalian dive reflex: her heart rate drops by up to fifty percent (bradycardia), peripheral blood vessels constrict to shunt oxygen exclusively to the brain and heart, and her spleen—which recent genetic studies show has evolved to be significantly larger in Jeju diving lineages—contracts to inject a reserve boost of oxygen-rich red blood cells directly into her bloodstream. When she resurfaces after two minutes on the seafloor, she purses her lips to expel carbon dioxide and inhale fresh air rapidly without hyperventilating or blacking out—producing the signature *sumbisori* whistle.

Inside the coastal changing huts known as *bulteok* (불턱)—traditionally circular enclosures of stacked black lava stones centered around a driftwood fire Pit—the *haenyeo* operate according to a strict, democratic sisterhood divided into three ranks of skill and lung capacity. At the top are the *Sanggun* (상군), elite veteran divers who plunge below fifteen meters into strong currents and mentor the village; in the middle are the *Junggun* (중군), who harvest at eight to twelve meters; and at the base are the *Hagun* (하군), novices—often in their fifties or sixties today—who dive in the shallower kelp beds of three to five meters.

What makes the *haenyeo* a model of twenty-first-century ecological sustainability is their self-imposed code of marine conservation, governed by each village’s *Eochongye* (Fishing Village Cooperative). By unanimous rule, oxygen tanks are strictly banned: a diver may harvest only what she can gather on a single human breath. During the spawning seasons of abalone, conch (*sora*), octopus, and *hijiki* seaweed, harvesting is forbidden; undersized shellfish are thrown back immediately; and the *haenyeo* conduct regular underwater weeding (*gaetdakkgi*) to clear invasive seaweeds and scatter shellfish spat across the volcanic reefs.

Even the profits of the sea are shared with communal solidarity. Older *Hagun* divers in their eighties whose lungs can no longer reach the deep abalone ledges are granted exclusive harvesting rights to a reserved shallow zone called the *Halttang-badang* ('Grandmothers’ Sea') right next to the shore, ensuring that no elder diver loses her dignity or income as her breath shortens. Before every major spring harvest, the women gather in silk robes at the seaside shrine to perform the *Jamsugut* shamanic ritual, offering rice cakes, fruit, and prayers to *Yongwang* (the Dragon King of the Sea) and *Seolmundae Halmang*, the giantess goddess who mythologically formed Jeju Island by scooping volcanic mud with her skirt.

Equally fierce on land, the Jeju *haenyeo* led the largest women’s anti-colonial uprising in Korean history. In January 1932, over 17,000 *haenyeo* brandishing their iron *bitchang* chisels and mesh nets marched across Gujwa and Seongsan to protest Japanese colonial exploitation and price-fixing of their catch, singing the 'Haenyeo Resistance Song' and forcing the Japanese governor to back down. Today, with fewer than 3,000 active divers remaining and most over age seventy, UNESCO inscribed the 'Culture of Jeju Haenyeo' on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016—honoring women who turned cold basalt waters and a single human breath into a matriarchal republic.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Evolutionary Physiology: The Genetic Secret of the Haenyeo Spleen

Like the Bajau 'Sea Nomads' of Southeast Asia, the Haenyeo of Jeju Island have become a touchstone for human evolutionary biology. Research on elderly Jeju divers revealed that decades of cold-water breath-hold diving—combined with island-specific genetic variations—produce extraordinary cold-water basal metabolic adaptations and splenic contraction responses. Even in the era before rubber wetsuits (introduced in the late 1960s and 1970s), when divers spent winter shifts in thin cotton mulsojungi and warmed themselves afterward over open bulteok driftwood fires, their core temperature regulation exceeded every standard textbook limit for human hypothermia. 2. Ecological Ethics: Why Banning Scuba Tanks Saved Jeju's Reefs

Economist Elinor Ostrom won the Nobel Prize for demonstrating how local communities can manage 'the commons' without privatization or state coercion, and the Jeju Haenyeo Eochongye is one of the planet's purest examples. 3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Learn three indelible Jeju terms: first, Sumbisori (숨비소리), the high-pitched whistling exhalation at the surface that expels trapped carbon dioxide from the lungs and signals to fellow divers across the waves that a sister has surfaced safely; second, Bulteok (불턱), the circular black-basalt windbreak and hearth where divers warm their bodies, share village news, and pass down reef maps orally from Sanggun elders to novices; and third, Tewak (테왁), the bright orange float (originally made from hollowed-out bottle gourds) from which the diver's harvest net hangs.
