Ureuk's Twelve Silk Strings: The Gayageum from Sixth-Century Gaya to Sanjo Improvisation

Korea — How a refugee musician from the iron kingdom of Gaya carried a twelve-bridge paulownia zither to Silla in 551 CE and seeded the jazz of nineteenth-century Korea (Gaya Confederacy (551 CE) to Late Joseon Sanjo (1890s–Present)) • 20 min read

“Do not discard the music of Gaya because its kingdom fell; a state rises or falls by the virtue of its rule, not the sound of its strings.” — King Jinheung of Silla, Samguk Sagi (551 CE)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Carved from a single aged trunk of lightweight paulownia wood (*odongnamu*) and strung with twelve twisted raw-silk strings resting on movable wooden bridges shaped like the webbed feet of wild geese (*anjok*), the Gayageum is Korea's most iconic plucked zither. According to the Samguk Sagi, King Gasil of the sixth-century Gaya Confederacy designed the instrument after the twelve lunar months and commanded court master Ureuk to compose twelve regional suites. When Gaya faced annexation in 551 CE, Ureuk fled with his disciple Imun carrying the Gayageum to King Jinheung of Silla—preserving a musical lineage at Chungju's Tangeumdae cliff that blossomed 1,300 years later into Kim Chang-jo's dazzling solo improvisational art of Gayageum Sanjo.

Perch on the pine-shaded granite cliff of **Tangeumdae** (탄금대, 彈琴臺, literally 'Terrace of Plucked Zithers') where the Dalcheon Stream flows into the Namhan River in Chungju, North Chungcheong Province. Fourteen hundred and seventy years ago, in the spring of 551 CE, an aging refugee musician named **Ureuk** (우륵, 于勒) sat on this exact riverside rock ledge with a five-foot hollowed wooden board resting across his right knee. As his right index finger flicked a twisted silk string and his left hand pressed down deeply behind a movable cherry-wood bridge, a resonant, weeping pitch bent across the water like a wild goose calling in the mist. That instrument was the **Gayageum** (가야금, 伽倻琴, literally 'Zither of Gaya'), and Ureuk had risked his life to carry it across a collapsing border so its voice would not perish with his homeland. In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Gaya Confederacy (551 CE) to Late Joseon Sanjo (1890s–Present), the rise of Gayageum (가야금 twelve-stringed Korean paulownia zither) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Ureuk (우륵 sixth-century Gaya court musician and composer) and Anjok & Sanjo (안족과 산조 wild-goose-foot bridges and scattered melodies) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Before Silla unified the Korean peninsula in the seventh century, the fertile Nakdong River basin along the southern coast was home to the **Gaya Confederacy** (가야, 42–562 CE)—a federation of six iron-smelting city-states (*Geumgwan Gaya* in Gimhae, *Daegaya* in Goryeong, *Ara Gaya* in Haman) whose rich iron ingots were traded to Han China, Lelang, and Wa Japan, and whose royal tumuli at Jisan-dong and Gimhae recently joined the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023. Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Tangeumdae in Chungju and Goryeong stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Ureuk (우륵 sixth-century Gaya court musician and composer) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Anjok & Sanjo (안족과 산조 wild-goose-foot bridges and scattered melodies) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

According to Book 32 (*Akji*, Treatise on Music) of Kim Bu-sik’s 1145 *Samguk Sagi*, in the early sixth century **King Gasil** (가실왕) of Daegaya (in modern Goryeong) examined the Chinese *zheng* zither and declared: 'When countries differ in their speech and customs, how can their musical voices be forced to be the same?' Commissioning his court music master **Ureuk** of Seongyeol-hyeon, King Gasil engineered a native twelve-stringed zither modeled on cosmic time: a convex upper soundboard representing the round dome of Heaven, a flat bottom board representing the square Earth, and **twelve silk strings representing the twelve lunar months of the year**. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Gayageum (가야금 twelve-stringed Korean paulownia zither) aligned Ureuk (우륵 sixth-century Gaya court musician and composer) with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of Anjok & Sanjo (안족과 산조 wild-goose-foot bridges and scattered melodies) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

King Gasil then commanded Ureuk to compose twelve musical suites named after the twelve federated districts and towns of Gaya—*Hagarado*, *Sanggarado*, *Bogisa*, *Dalgi*, *Sa-mul*, and seven others—using music to bind the decentralized confederacy into a cultural commonwealth. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. By 551 CE, however, Daegaya was crumbling under relentless military pressure from its neighbors Baekje and Silla. Seeing that his kingdom was doomed, Ureuk made a fateful choice: rather than letting the *Gayageum* burn in the fall of Goryeong, he took his young disciple **Imun** (이문) and his zither and defected to **King Jinheung of Silla** at娘성 (Nangseong, modern Cheongju). Conservative Silla court ministers immediately urged King Jinheung to destroy the instrument, warning: 'The music of Gaya is the sound of a dying, ruined state (*mang-guk-ji-eum*); it is ominous to play it in our royal court!' King Jinheung rejected their superstition with a reply that saved Korean music history: 'The King of Gaya brought ruin upon himself through misrule; what sin does the music itself bear? The sages created music to express human emotion, and a country’s rise or fall does not lie in the pitches of its strings.' Settling Ureuk at Gukwon (modern Chungju) above the Namhan River at *Tangeumdae*, King Jinheung dispatched three gifted young Silla aristocrats—**Beopji** (법지, for song), **Gyego** (계고, for the gayageum), and **Mandeok** (만덕, for dance)—to study under the Gaya master. After learning Ureuk’s twelve Gaya suites, the three Silla disciples privately condensed the twelve sprawling regional pieces into five tighter, refined court movements. That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Tangeumdae in Chungju and Goryeong. What gives the *Gayageum* its unmistakable acoustic warmth and expressive vocal bend? Look at the luthier’s craft (*Akgi-jang*, 악기장, National Intangible Cultural Heritage No. 42). The original sixth-century court instrument—preserved today as the **Beopgeum** or **Pungnyu Gayageum** (풍류가야금), whose eighth-century Silla examples survive intact in the Shosoin Repository in Nara, Japan!—is hollowed out from a single solid log of **Paulownia wood** (*odongnamu*, 오동나무) aged outdoors in rain, snow, and sun for at least five to ten years to leach out sap and tannin. To experience Gayageum (가야금 twelve-stringed Korean paulownia zither) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. From the tactile grain of Ureuk (우륵 sixth-century Gaya court musician and composer) beneath the fingers to the rhythmic cadence of Anjok & Sanjo (안족과 산조 wild-goose-foot bridges and scattered melodies) echoing across Tangeumdae in Chungju and Goryeong, every detail reinforced a shared sense of civic and spiritual belonging.

At the tail end of the paulownia body, the wood is carved directly into two horn-like projections called **Yangidu** (양이두, 羊耳頭, 'Ram’s Ears') around which the string cords are knotted. Across the curved soundboard sit twelve freestanding, three-legged movable bridges carved from dense cherry or jujube wood called **Anjok** (안족, 雁足—literally 'Wild Goose Feet'), because when the twelve bridges are lined up diagonally from low to high pitch, they trace the V-shaped formation of wild geese flying across the autumn sky! Unlike a Western guitar, harp, or piano—where strings are plucked with picks or hammers and pitch is fixed by frets—the Korean *Gayageum* player plucks and flicks the bare twisted raw-silk strings (*myeongju-sil*, boiled with rice straw ash) directly with the flesh and nail of the right thumb, index, and middle fingers without artificial plectrums. Simultaneously, the player’s left hand rests two inches to the left of the *anjok* bridges, pressing, shaking, and sliding the silk string deeply toward the soundboard. That Gayageum (가야금 twelve-stringed Korean paulownia zither) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion. That left-hand technique—**Nonghyeon** (농현, 弄絃, 'playing with the string,' encompassing deep vibrato *jeonseong*, pitch slides *chuseong*, and falling sighs *toeseong*)—allows a single plucked silk string to bend its pitch by up to two full whole-tones after the note has already been struck! When a master plays *nonghyeon*, the wooden zither stops sounding like a stringed instrument and begins to breathe, sob, and laugh like a *pansori* singer’s human voice. In the 1890s, in 영암 (Yeongam), South Jeolla Province, that vocal freedom sparked a musical revolution. A brilliant southern *gayageum* virtuoso named **Kim Chang-jo** (김창조, 1856–1919) took the court *Beopgeum*, narrowed and shortened its paulownia body, backed it with resonant hard chestnut wood (*bamnamu*), spaced the twelve strings closer together for lightning finger velocity, and synthesized folk shamanic rhythms (*sinawi*) and *pansori* vocal phrasing into **Gayageum Sanjo** (가야금 산조, 'Scattered Melodies'). 1. Acoustic Physics: Paulownia Wood ('Odongnamu'), Silk Strings, and 'Nonghyeon' Pitch Bending

Acoustic impedance tests explain why traditional luthiers (Akgi-jang) insist on aged Korean paulownia wood (Paulownia coreana, 오동나무) for the Gayageum soundboard. Paulownia has one of the lowest densities (0.26 g/cm³) and highest sound-propagation velocities relative to mass of any hardwood, reinforced by scorching the wood surface with a hot iron (indujil, 인두질) to seal pores and harden the top grain. Because twisted raw-silk strings have high elasticity and low inharmonicity compared to steel wire, left-hand nonghyeon pressure behind the movable anjok bridge can bend frequencies smoothly by 200 to 350 cents without snapping. 2. From 6th-Century Shosoin Relics to 1890s 'Sanjo' (Scattered Melodies)

Two historical anchors bracket the Gayageum's evolution. First, inside the eighth-century Shosoin Imperial Repository at Todai-ji in Nara, Japan, survive three intact Unified Silla Pungnyu Gayageums (labeled Shiragi-goto) featuring the exact ram's-ear tailpiece (yangidu) created in Gaya. Second, in the 1890s, Kim Chang-jo developed the smaller Sanjo Gayageum and codified Gayageum Sanjo (National Intangible Cultural Heritage No. 23)—a solo instrumental form progressing from slow Jinyangjo to rapid Hwimori rhythmic cycles. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Tangeumdae in Chungju and Goryeong, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Gayageum (가야금 twelve-stringed Korean paulownia zither) to Anjok & Sanjo (안족과 산조 wild-goose-foot bridges and scattered melodies) across the centuries.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three musical terms in mind: first, Anjok (안족, 'Wild Goose Feet'), the twelve movable cherry-wood bridges that tune the strings and trace a diagonal flight of geese; second, Nonghyeon (농현), the left-hand vibrato and pitch-bending technique that gives Korean string music its vocal soul; and third, Nak-i-bul-ryu Ae-i-bul-bi (낙이불류 애이불비, 'Joyous yet not excessive, sorrowful yet not broken'), Ureuk's timeless aesthetic standard.
