The Song of Wandering Aengus

W. B. Yeats (1897) • 3 min read

Because a fire was in his head, a seeker catches a silver trout in a hazel wood that transforms into a glimmering girl, launching a lifelong quest for the golden apples of the sun.

Key Context & Why It Matters

First published in The Sketch in August 1897 under the title 'A Mad Song' and given its mythological title in Yeats’s 1899 Symbolist collection The Wind Among the Reeds, the poem draws on the ancient Irish myth of Aengus Óg, the Celtic god of youth, love, and poetic inspiration who searched all Ireland for a dream-maiden. Belonging to the Celtic Twilight / Irish Revival tradition and categorized under Gothic & Ballad, 'The Song of Wandering Aengus' (1897) unfolds across 24 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. Notably giving Ray Bradbury the title of his beloved 1953 short-story collection The Golden Apples of the Sun, Yeats’s ballad stands as the supreme lyric of creative longing and visionary pursuit.

I went out to the hazel wood,

Because a fire was in my head,

And cut and peeled a hazel wand,

And hooked a berry to a thread;

And when white moths were on the wing,

And moth-like stars were flickering out,

I dropped the berry in a stream

And caught a little silver trout.

When I had laid it on the floor

I went to blow the fire a-flame,

But something rustled on the floor,

And someone called me by my name:

It had become a glimmering girl

With apple blossom in her hair

Who called me by my name and ran

And faded through the brightening air.

Though I am old with wandering

Through hollow lands and hilly lands,

I will find out where she has gone,

And kiss her lips and take her hands;

And walk among long dappled grass,

And pluck till time and times are done

The silver apples of the moon,

The golden apples of the sun.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'The Song of Wandering Aengus' lies the lifelong, unquenchable quest of the artist and dreamer for ideal beauty: sparked when 'a fire was in my head,' the speaker catches a little silver trout that turns into a 'glimmering girl / With apple blossom in her hair' who calls his name and vanishes, compelling him to wander hollow lands and hilly lands even when old with wandering. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"It had become a glimmering girl"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"The golden apples of the sun."—the central metaphor has.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'The Song of Wandering Aengus' using three eight-line ballad stanzas (ABCBDEFE) in propulsive iambic tetrameter enriched by enchanting Celtic folk-song parallelism, vivid sensory contrasts (white moths, moth-like stars, hazel wand, berry on a thread), and the unforgettable closing chiasmus of 'The silver apples of the moon, / The golden apples of the sun'. Spanning 24 lines across 3 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "I went out to the hazel wood," and "The golden apples of the sun." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
