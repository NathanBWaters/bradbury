The Song of Wandering Aengus

W. B. Yeats (1897) • 3 min read

Because a fire was in his head, a seeker catches a silver trout in a hazel wood that transforms into a glimmering girl, launching a lifelong quest for the golden apples of the sun.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This poem is built from an old Irish myth about Aengus, the god of youth, love and poetry, who fell in love with a girl he had seen only in a dream and searched all of Ireland for her. Yeats first published it in 1897 under the title A Mad Song, then renamed it for the myth and placed it in his 1899 collection The Wind Among the Reeds, a high point of the Celtic Twilight, the Irish Revival's dreamy, folklore-filled style. In three short stanzas, a speaker catches a magic fish, sees it turn into a glimmering girl who vanishes, and spends his life wandering in search of her. The poem matters because it captures, in a few simple lines, the restless longing behind much art and love: the sense that something beautiful has been glimpsed and lost, and must be pursued. It also gave Ray Bradbury the title of his 1953 story collection, The Golden Apples of the Sun. Listen to it as a fairy tale that is secretly about a lifetime.

I went out to the hazel wood,

Because a fire was in my head,

And cut and peeled a hazel wand,

And hooked a berry to a thread;

And when white moths were on the wing,

And moth-like stars were flickering out,

I dropped the berry in a stream

And caught a little silver trout.

When I had laid it on the floor

I went to blow the fire a-flame,

But something rustled on the floor,

And someone called me by my name:

It had become a glimmering girl

With apple blossom in her hair

Who called me by my name and ran

And faded through the brightening air.

Though I am old with wandering

Through hollow lands and hilly lands,

I will find out where she has gone,

And kiss her lips and take her hands;

And walk among long dappled grass,

And pluck till time and times are done

The silver apples of the moon,

The golden apples of the sun.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem tells a small, strange story in three stages: a beginning, a vision, and a vow.

In the first stanza, the speaker goes out to the hazel wood because a fire was in my head. That phrase is the doorway to everything. It is not a literal fire; it is feverish restlessness, creative or romantic or spiritual. He cuts a hazel wand, which in Irish tradition is associated with wisdom and magic, and fishes with a berry on a thread. At the hour when white moths are on the wing and the moth-like stars are flickering out, he catches a little silver trout. Notice the way the moths and the stars are linked. Everything in this world is faintly transformed already, and the line between the earth and the sky is thin. It is dusk, or the edge of dawn, a time when magic is allowed.

In the second stanza, the trout is laid on the floor, and while the speaker goes to blow the fire a-flame, something rustles. Someone called me by my name. The fish has become a glimmering girl with apple blossom in her hair. She calls his name and runs, and fades through the brightening air. Two details are worth pausing on. First, she knows his name; this is not a random encounter but something that seems destined. Second, she fades through brightening air, which suggests the coming of daylight and the cold return of ordinary reality. The vision cannot survive the sun.

The third stanza leaps forward in time. Now the speaker is old with wandering, through hollow lands and hilly lands. The vision has taken over his whole life. Yet his purpose has not weakened: I will find out where she has gone, and kiss her lips and take her hands. The final four lines describe what he imagines at the end of his search, walking among long dappled grass and plucking, till time and times are done, the silver apples of the moon, the golden apples of the sun.

The apples are the poem's most famous image. Silver for the moon, gold for the sun, they are impossible fruit, belonging to a paradise outside time. Plucking them means touching something eternal. The phrase till time and times are done suggests that the speaker pictures a union that lasts beyond the end of the world.

What is the poem really about? On one level, it is a love story. On another, it is about the creative impulse and the longing for an ideal that can never quite be caught. The girl is the beloved, but she is also beauty, inspiration, the thing every poet or seeker chases. The poem suggests that once you have seen it, you cannot go back to ordinary life, and that the pursuit may itself be the meaning of a life.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of craft, this is one of the most accomplished short ballads in the language, and it is worth noting why before looking at where it is open to question.

Its greatest strength is its simplicity. The language is plain and concrete: hazel wood, berry, thread, stream, trout, floor, fire. The story moves by direct statement. Yet that plain surface carries an enormous charge of strangeness, because the events are impossible and the speaker narrates them as if they were ordinary. This combination, a matter-of-fact voice telling a magical tale, is the engine of the poem's power. It is how folk tales work, and Yeats uses it with great skill.

The sound is also remarkable. The lines are short and rhythmic, close to the beat of a song, and there are repeated words that tie it together: called me by my name appears twice in the second stanza, and the rhyme on name and flame, with the echo of floor and floor, makes the stanza feel like a charm being recited. The final stanza opens out into long, slow lines, with phrases such as hollow lands and hilly lands, then ends with a grand chime in the last two lines, moon and sun, silver and golden, that is among the loveliest endings in English poetry.

The structure is flawless in its economy. Eight lines for the quest, eight for the vision, eight for the lifelong consequence. The jump in time between the second and third stanza is the poem's best trick. We go from a moment of magic to a life of loss with no transition, and the reader feels the years collapsing.

Where is it weaker? One could say that it is a little too beautiful for its own good. The imagery, apple blossom, silver, gold, moths, stars, is the very vocabulary of the dreamy, decorative style that Yeats later said he had outgrown. The poem lacks the knotty, argumentative toughness of his later work, and a reader seeking intellectual challenge may find it more pretty than profound. There is also a slight vagueness in the central figure. The glimmering girl is more an image than a person, and she is given no character, no voice beyond the repeating of his name. She functions as a symbol, and the poem cannot quite make us care about her as a human being.

The poem also leaves some questions open that a reader might wish answered. Why does the speaker go out in the first place? What is the fire in his head? The poem refuses to say, which is part of its charm, but it can also read as evasive. Still, these are small quibbles. The poem's achievement is that it makes a story that sounds like a nursery tale feel like a statement about an entire life, in twenty-four lines, without a wasted word.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's reputation spread widely and has remained high for more than a century, which makes it one of the most quoted pieces from Yeats's early work.

Within his career, the poem was part of the book that is often seen as the high-water mark of his early, symbolist period. The Wind Among the Reeds appeared in 1899, when Yeats was deeply involved with the circle around the Order of the Golden Dawn, a society of ritual and magic, and with the French and English Symbolist poets who believed that images could stand for hidden spiritual realities. This poem is one of the clearest examples of what that approach could do. It also helped establish the myth-based approach to Irish identity that was central to the Irish Revival.

The best-known literary descendant is, of course, Ray Bradbury's 1953 story collection The Golden Apples of the Sun. Bradbury borrowed the line and also used the title for a story in which a spaceship flies into the sun to scoop up a piece of it. The borrowing is revealing. It shows how a line of nineteenth-century Irish verse could be reused in science fiction, with the poem's sense of impossible longing carried into the age of rockets. Bradbury was devoted to poetry, and the title stands as one of the clearest tributes by a major American storyteller to the Irish poet.

Many other writers have drawn on the poem's imagery. The phrases about silver apples and golden apples show up in the titles and epigraphs of novels, songs and albums. Musicians have set the poem to music in many styles, from folk to classical, and it is sometimes performed as a song. Its imagery has also appeared in visual art and illustration, and it is a staple of anthologies of love poetry and of Irish poetry in particular.

In the classroom, it is widely taught as an example of the ballad form, of mythic allusion, and of the way a poet can build a symbol. Because it is so short and so musical, it is often learned by heart, and many readers can recite it years after first encountering it.

Politically, its impact was subtle but real. By drawing on Irish myth and presenting it as a source of lasting beauty, Yeats and his contemporaries gave the country a cultural identity that did not depend on British models. A poem about an Irish god, written in English, helped show that Irish tradition could produce world-class literature.

It is hard to name a single moment of influence, but the poem's accumulated presence in the culture, its lines turning up in unexpected places, shows that it has become part of the shared vocabulary of the English-speaking world.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this poem for so many years that it is worth sorting out the main reasons.

First is the music. It is a poem that sings. Read aloud, it has the drive of a folk ballad and the gloss of a lyric. People often say they can hear a tune behind it. That musical quality makes it easy to remember and satisfying to speak, and it is a main reason the poem has been so often set as a song.

Second is the sense of enchantment. The story is brief and strange, and it never explains itself. A fish becomes a girl, a girl becomes a vanishing light, and the reader feels something like what a child feels on first hearing a fairy tale: wonder, with a faint shiver. Adults, who may think they have outgrown such things, often find that the poem returns them to that feeling.

Third is the emotional truth beneath the magic. Almost everyone has had the experience of glimpsing something wonderful and not being able to hold it, a person, a place, a possibility, a moment of insight, a calling. The poem turns that experience into a story, and it does not pretend the search is easy. The old man still going, still determined, is both sad and heroic. Readers, particularly those who are artists, scientists or dreamers of any kind, find in him a picture of what a committed life can feel like: you keep going, long after the vision has gone, because you cannot do otherwise.

Fourth is the ending. The last two lines are among the most admired in all of Yeats. The silver apples of the moon, The golden apples of the sun. There is a perfect symmetry, a balance of metals, bodies in the sky, and syllables. The words have a ring that stays in the ear. Many readers cannot say exactly what the apples mean, but they feel that the lines express a kind of ultimate happiness, and this uncertainty is part of what they treasure.

Fifth is its hopefulness. Many of Yeats's poems are tragic or bitter. This one, despite its sorrow, ends with a vow and a vision of fulfilment. The old man is not defeated. He still believes in the possibility of joy, and the poem lets him keep that belief. For readers who find a great deal of poetry too gloomy, that is a welcome gift.

Finally, there is the pleasure of connection. Because the poem has been quoted and borrowed so often, meeting it feels a little like meeting the source of a river one has long been downstream of. Many readers first encounter its lines in other works, and the discovery of the original is a small thrill.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a poem this admired has attracted some reservations, and they are worth hearing.

The most common is that the poem is too pretty, too much in the manner of the Celtic Twilight, a style that Yeats himself later criticized. He came to feel that the early poetry, with its mists, fairies and faded loveliness, was a kind of escape from real life, and he deliberately moved toward harder, more direct writing. Modernist critics in the early twentieth century, including friends and younger poets who admired his later work, sometimes spoke of the early lyrics as thin or over-decorated. By that standard, this poem can look like exquisite embroidery, finely worked but light.

A second criticism concerns the role of the woman. The girl in the poem has no character. She is an apparition, a glimmering image who exists to be chased. Feminist readers have pointed out that the poem treats the female figure as a vanishing ideal, an object of male pursuit, and that the speaker's desire to kiss her lips and take her hands gives her little say in the matter. The poem could be taken as an example of a long tradition in which women exist mainly as inspiration for men's quests. Defenders reply that she is clearly a symbol and that the poem is about a state of mind, not about real courtship, but the objection persists.

There is also a question about escapism. The poem's world is a magical one, and some critics have felt that this kind of Irish myth-making can romanticize the past and avoid the actual conditions of Irish life. Writers of later generations, especially those dealing with hard social realities, sometimes reacted against the Revival's mythological mood, regarding it as a sentimental fiction created largely by writers of a different class from the people they wrote about.

Others have wondered about the poem's meaning. It is so open that readers have taken it as a love poem, a religious allegory, a statement about poetic inspiration, a poem of death, and more. For some, this is richness. For others, it is evasiveness: a poem so willing to mean everything may be failing to commit to anything.

It is also worth noting that the poem is sometimes criticized for being so well known that it has become a cliche. The phrase about the apples of the sun and moon has been used so often in popular culture that it can seem like a commonplace, and readers may have to work to hear it freshly.

For a poem so loved, however, the criticism has been mild. Few serious readers have suggested it is a bad poem. The usual verdict is that it is a lovely one in a limited mode, and that its limits are also its virtues.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place this poem properly, we should begin with the myth it draws on. Aengus, in Irish tradition, is a god of the Tuatha De Danann, the old divine people of Ireland, and is associated with love, youth and poetry. In one story he sees a girl in a dream, falls ill with longing, and searches the whole country until he finds her. Yeats took that outline and compressed it into a ballad, changing the god into a human-seeming wanderer, and adding the magic fish, the hazel wand, and the apples. The result is partly a retelling and partly a new myth of Yeats's own.

The poem belongs to the Celtic Twilight, the name sometimes given to the Irish Revival's early, folklore-soaked style, which took its title from one of Yeats's own books. It is also a product of European Symbolism, a movement that sought to express inner states through suggestive images rather than direct statement. In that way it is related to the French poets Mallarme and Verlaine, and to English contemporaries such as Arthur Symons, who championed the movement and became a close friend of Yeats.

Its deeper ancestry reaches back to the quest romances of the Middle Ages, in which a hero pursues an ideal that is always just ahead, and to the dream-vision poems that medieval writers loved. Among English poets, it recalls Keats's La Belle Dame sans Merci, in which a knight meets a mysterious woman and is left wandering, although Keats's poem ends in doom while Yeats's ends in hope. It also resembles Coleridge's Kubla Khan, a dreamlike poem about a vision that fades. In another direction, it is related to the folk ballad tradition, where simple language carries uncanny events.

Its descendants are many. Bradbury's collection is the most famous example, but the poem also stands behind a large body of fantasy and science fiction in which longing for a lost or impossible beauty drives the story. The idea of the dream that must be pursued, and the lifetime consumed by the pursuit, recurs in writers as different as Tolkien, with his yearning for a vanished elvish world, and many modern poets who have taken up Irish myth. In Irish poetry, later writers such as Seamus Heaney both honored and quarreled with the Revival's mythic habit, but they worked in a landscape that Yeats had helped create.

Why does it still matter? Partly because it is one of those poems that can be understood at several ages. A child can enjoy the magic fish and the girl with blossoms in her hair. A teenager can feel the romantic ache. An older reader can feel the weight of the line about being old with wandering, and the stubbornness of the vow that follows. It also matters because it shows how poetry can compress a whole life into a handful of images, and how a single phrase, the silver apples of the moon, the golden apples of the sun, can carry a dream across more than a hundred years. Its lasting message is simple and large: the search itself may be the thing worth giving a life to.
