Three Questions

Leo Tolstoy (1903) • 8 min read

“And learned men came to the King, but they all answered his questions differently.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Leo Tolstoy wrote Three Questions in 1903, when he was seventy-four, famous around the world, and officially an outcast from the Russian Orthodox Church, which had excommunicated him two years earlier. He had recently survived a serious illness, and he was thinking hard about how to spend whatever time he had left. The story is a fable, a short tale with a lesson, about a king who believes that if he could only answer three questions, he would never fail at anything. When should I act, whom should I listen to, and what matters most? The wise men of his kingdom all give different answers, so he goes to see a hermit in the forest. Tolstoy spent his whole late career trying to boil life down to a simple practice, and this tale is one of his clearest attempts. Listen for what the king ends up doing before anyone answers him.

It once occurred to a certain king, that if he always knew the right time to begin everything; if he knew who were the right people to listen to, and whom to avoid; and, above all, if he always knew what was the most important thing to do, he would never fail in anything he might undertake.

And this thought having occurred to him, he had it proclaimed throughout his kingdom that he would give a great reward to anyone who would teach him what was the right time for every action, and who were the most necessary people, and how he might know what was the most important thing to do.

And learned men came to the King, but they all answered his questions differently.

In reply to the first question, some said that to know the right time for every action, one must draw up in advance, a table of days, months and years, and must live strictly according to it. Only thus, said they, could everything be done at its proper time. Others declared that it was impossible to decide beforehand the right time for every action; but that, not letting oneself be absorbed in idle pastimes, one should always attend to all that was going on, and then do what was most needful. Others, again, said that however attentive the King might be to what was going on, it was impossible for one man to decide correctly the right time for every action, but that he should have a Council of wise men, who would help him to fix the proper time for everything.

But then again others said there were some things which could not wait to be laid before a Council, but about which one had at once to decide whether to undertake them or not. But in order to decide that, one must know beforehand what was going to happen. It is only magicians who know that; and, therefore, in order to know the right time for every action, one must consult magicians.

Equally various were the answers to the second question. Some said, the people the King most needed were his councillors; others, the priests; others, the doctors; while some said the warriors were the most necessary.

To the third question, as to what was the most important occupation: some replied that the most important thing in the world was science. Others said it was skill in warfare; and others, again, that it was religious worship.

All the answers being different, the King agreed with none of them, and gave the reward to none. But still wishing to find the right answers to his questions, he decided to consult a hermit, widely renowned for his wisdom.

The hermit lived in a wood which he never quitted, and he received none but common folk. So the King put on simple clothes, and before reaching the hermit’s cell dismounted from his horse, and, leaving his bodyguard behind, went on alone.

When the King approached, the hermit was digging the ground in front of his hut. Seeing the King, he greeted him and went on digging. The hermit was frail and weak, and each time he stuck his spade into the ground and turned a little earth, he breathed heavily.

The King went up to him and said: “I have come to you, wise hermit, to ask you to answer three questions: How can I learn to do the right thing at the right time? Who are the people I most need, and to whom should I, therefore, pay more attention than to the rest? And, what affairs are the most important, and need my first attention?”

The hermit listened to the King, but answered nothing. He just spat on his hand and recommenced digging.

“You are tired,” said the King, “let me take the spade and work awhile for you.”

“Thanks!” said the hermit, and, giving the spade to the King, he sat down on the ground.

When he had dug two beds, the King stopped and repeated his questions. The hermit again gave no answer, but rose, stretched out his hand for the spade, and said:

“Now rest awhile⁠—and let me work a bit.”

But the King did not give him the spade, and continued to dig. One hour passed, and another. The sun began to sink behind the trees, and the King at last stuck the spade into the ground, and said:

“I came to you, wise man, for an answer to my questions. If you can give me none, tell me so, and I will return home.”

“Here comes someone running,” said the hermit, “let us see who it is.”

The King turned round, and saw a bearded man come running out of the wood. The man held his hands pressed against his stomach, and blood was flowing from under them. When he reached the King, he fell fainting on the ground moaning feebly. The King and the hermit unfastened the man’s clothing. There was a large wound in his stomach. The King washed it as best he could, and bandaged it with his handkerchief and with a towel the hermit had. But the blood would not stop flowing, and the King again and again removed the bandage soaked with warm blood, and washed and rebandaged the wound. When at last the blood ceased flowing, the man revived and asked for something to drink. The King brought fresh water and gave it to him. Meanwhile the sun had set, and it had become cool. So the King, with the hermit’s help, carried the wounded man into the hut and laid him on the bed. Lying on the bed the man closed his eyes and was quiet; but the King was so tired with his walk and with the work he had done, that he crouched down on the threshold, and also fell asleep⁠—so soundly that he slept all through the short summer night. When he awoke in the morning, it was long before he could remember where he was, or who was the strange bearded man lying on the bed and gazing intently at him with shining eyes.

“Forgive me!” said the bearded man in a weak voice, when he saw that the King was awake and was looking at him.

“I do not know you, and have nothing to forgive you for,” said the King.

“You do not know me, but I know you. I am that enemy of yours who swore to revenge himself on you, because you executed his brother and seized his property. I knew you had gone alone to see the hermit, and I resolved to kill you on your way back. But the day passed and you did not return. So I came out from my ambush to find you, and I came upon your bodyguard, and they recognized me, and wounded me. I escaped from them, but should have bled to death had you not dressed my wound. I wished to kill you, and you have saved my life. Now, if I live, and if you wish it, I will serve you as your most faithful slave, and will bid my sons do the same. Forgive me!”

The King was very glad to have made peace with his enemy so easily, and to have gained him for a friend, and he not only forgave him, but said he would send his servants and his own physician to attend him, and promised to restore his property.

Having taken leave of the wounded man, the King went out into the porch and looked around for the hermit. Before going away he wished once more to beg an answer to the questions he had put. The hermit was outside, on his knees, sowing seeds in the beds that had been dug the day before.

The King approached him, and said:

“For the last time, I pray you to answer my questions, wise man.”

“You have already been answered!” said the hermit still crouching on his thin legs, and looking up at the King, who stood before him.

“How answered? What do you mean?” asked the King.

“Do you not see,” replied the hermit. “If you had not pitied my weakness yesterday, and had not dug those beds for me, but had gone your way, that man would have attacked you, and you would have repented of not having stayed with me. So the most important time was when you were digging the beds; and I was the most important man; and to do me good was your most important business. Afterwards when that man ran to us, the most important time was when you were attending to him, for if you had not bound up his wounds he would have died without having made peace with you. So he was the most important man, and what you did for him was your most important business. Remember then: there is only one time that is important⁠—Now! It is the most important time because it is the only time when we have any power. The most necessary man is he with whom you are, for no man knows whether he will ever have dealings with anyone else: and the most important affair is, to do him good, because for that purpose alone was man sent into this life!”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

A king decides that he would never fail in anything if he always knew the right time to begin each task, the right people to listen to and avoid, and above all the most important thing to do. He proclaims a reward for anyone who can answer these questions. Learned men come and each says something different. One says to draw up a timetable. Another says you cannot know the right time in advance, so you must pay attention to everything and act when needed. Another says you need a council of wise men, and another says that to know what to do you need priests, or doctors, or soldiers. None of these answers satisfies him, so he decides to consult a hermit who is said to be wise and who receives only simple people.

The king disguises himself, leaves his attendants behind, and walks to the hermit's hut in the woods. The hermit is digging beds in his garden, and he is old and frail. The king asks his questions, but the hermit only nods and keeps digging. Seeing how weak the old man is, the king takes the spade and digs for him. Hours pass, and the king grows impatient and says he will go home without an answer. At that moment a man comes running out of the forest holding his belly, covered in blood, and collapses. The king and the hermit wash and bind the wound, and the king, exhausted, falls asleep.

In the morning the wounded man begs forgiveness. He tells the king that he was his enemy, that the king had executed his brother and taken his property, and that he had been lying in wait to kill him. He was found by the king's bodyguards and wounded, and escaped. Now the king has saved his life. They make peace, and the king promises to send his own doctor and to restore the man's property. Then the king returns to the hermit and asks one more time for his answer. The hermit says he has already been answered, and explains.

The explanation is the heart of the story. If the king had not stopped to dig the beds, the man would have attacked him, so the most important time was when he was digging, the most important man was the hermit, and the most important act was helping him. Later the most important time was when he bandaged the wounded man, because otherwise the man would have died without reconciling. The conclusion is the famous passage. There is only one time that matters, which is now, because it is the only time we have any power. The most necessary person is whoever you are with, and the most important act is to do that person good.

There is a clever layer to this. The king's questions assumed that the answers lay in the future or in special knowledge, in a calendar or a council. The answer turns out to be that the questions are looking in the wrong place. Importance is not in the grand plan but in the person in front of you. Notice too that the king does not learn the lesson by being told it. He learns it by acting, and the explanation only names what he has already done.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The story has a handsome structure. It begins with a puzzle that sounds as if it needs a philosopher, and resolves it with a day of ordinary labor. The structure is that of a mystery, where the king keeps asking and the hermit keeps not answering, and the answer is hidden in plain view. When the hermit finally says you have already been answered, it is a small shock, because the reader too has been waiting for a speech and missed the lesson in the action.

The use of the wounded man is clever. He is not a random victim but the king's enemy, so that a simple act of kindness turns into a reconciliation that saves the king's life, and the plot gives practical weight to the moral. The details feel right as well. The king's irritation at the long day of digging, his desire to leave, and the quick, practical way he and the hermit tend the wound are all well observed. The hermit's refusal to answer at first makes him a believable teacher, since he understands that the king must see for himself.

But there are real problems. The central claim, that the only important time is now, is a fine idea but it can sound like a greeting card. Tolstoy states it with great force, yet in the story's own terms it rests on a coincidence. The king's kindness happens to save his life, and the structure suggests that doing good is also the safe course. A more honest tale might have shown a good deed that cost the king dearly. Here virtue is conveniently rewarded, so the lesson may be less demanding than it sounds.

There is also an odd gap between the lesson and the king's position. A king, after all, has real responsibilities, to a whole kingdom, which cannot be met by attending to the person in front of him. The story brushes this aside. The idea that the most important person is whoever happens to be with you is moving for a private person, but it is awkward for someone who decides the fates of thousands. The king who executed a man's brother and seized his property was not making the mistake of ignoring the present. He had done something worse, and the story treats that as background. A reader might want to hear more about it.

Finally, the prose is flat in places and the dialogue sounds more like exposition than speech, particularly in the hermit's final explanation. But that is part of the form. Tolstoy was writing a fable, and the plainness is chosen.

One more craft point is the pacing of the middle. Tolstoy lets the day drag, mentioning the sun, the king's tired arms, and his growing impatience, so that when the wounded man bursts from the trees the interruption feels like a real intrusion, as it would in life. That small choice makes the lesson land harder, because we, like the king, have been wishing to be elsewhere.

III. The Impact It Had

By 1903 Tolstoy was the most famous private citizen in Russia and a figure of world interest. His excommunication in 1901 had made him even more visible, and he was getting letters from followers around the world. In that setting this little tale was an attempt to distill his message into something anyone could remember. Of all his late tales it is probably the most directly practical, offering a rule for living rather than a warning.

The story has had a long life outside literature. It has been used for generations in sermons and teaching, and in recent decades it has been taken up in mindfulness, leadership, and self-help writing, where its answer, now, here, this person, is easily turned into advice about attention. The American author and illustrator Jon J Muth published a picture-book version in 2002, based on Tolstoy, which moved the tale into a modern children's story with a boy and his animal friends, and brought it to many young readers who had never heard of the original.

It also connects to Tolstoy's lasting influence on thinkers about service and nonviolence. His idea that goodness lives in small acts toward the people near us is one that Gandhi, who corresponded with him, took seriously, and it remains a strand of the ethics of care. I would not claim that this particular story drove those movements, but it states their spirit in memorable form.

It is also worth noting how little direct reception from its own time is recorded in detail. Tolstoy's name guaranteed that his short pieces were printed, translated, and read, but the critics of the day were more interested in his big religious arguments and his quarrel with the church than in a short fable. The story's real success came later, through repetition, as readers, translators, and teachers passed it along because it was short and clear and seemed to hold a whole philosophy in a few pages. That slow, word-of-mouth life is typical of the late tales.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers love most is how usable the story is. Few works of literature give you something so easy to carry. Three questions, three answers, and a day of labor to prove them. People often say that they remember the central lines for years, and that they return to them in moments when they feel distracted or overwhelmed, thinking about the future when the present needs them.

They love its modesty. It is not a story of dramatic triumph. The king's great deed is digging a garden bed and washing a wound, and the story insists that this is enough. Many readers find that a relief in a culture that tells us importance means scale. It suggests that an ordinary afternoon with an ordinary person can be the most important thing we ever do.

Readers also like the shape of the plot, the way the king's journey seems to fail, and then reveals itself as a success. The sense of a puzzle quietly solved is a pleasure in itself, and it helps the lesson feel earned.

And there is gratitude for its generosity. The hermit does not scold the king. He explains, kindly, and the king leaves better, not humiliated. Many readers see in that gentleness the best side of Tolstoy, a man who could be furious and demanding in his essays, but who in his tales could be patient with human weakness.

There is a particular pleasure in the way the story respects its readers' intelligence. It does not tell us the answers at the start. It sets up the puzzle, lets the wise men fail, and trusts us to feel the pull of the king's anxiety, because we share it. Most of us also want to know the right moment, the right person, and the right task, and the story begins by flattering that wish before it gently corrects it.

Many readers also say that the story changed how they think about ordinary interruptions. The wounded man appears at the worst moment, when the king is tired and wants to leave, and the lesson is that the interruption is the task. People who work in caring jobs, such as nurses, teachers, and parents, often say that this is the part that resonates most. It gives dignity to the unplanned, unglamorous help that makes up so much of a life.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The usual criticism is that the story is a sermon. Its message is clear from the start and delivered at the end in a speech that tells us exactly what to think. Readers who prefer fiction to leave questions open find the final explanation heavy, and some critics have called these late tales more tract than literature.

A related criticism is that the advice is too simple to be useful. Live in the present and do good to whoever is near you is hard to argue with, but critics point out that real life involves conflicts between the person in front of us and the people far away, between the immediate and the long-term. Planning, saving, and caution, which the story seems to dismiss, are also forms of care. A government that only attended to whoever stood nearest would be a bad one.

There are also political objections. Because the story is set among kings and hermits, it avoids the structural injustices that Tolstoy attacked in his essays. The king is simply a man who made a mistake, and the point of the story is to improve him, not to change the system that made him a king. Some critics have felt that Tolstoy's moral individualism, his focus on personal kindness, was a way of avoiding politics, even though he was a harsh critic of the state.

Finally, a few readers find the coincidence of the wounded man too convenient, and the hermit a little smug. The hermit never really explains why he would not simply answer in the first place, and some feel that his silence is a teaching trick. It works within the fable, but it may strain belief.

There is also an argument from the other direction, that the story is not simple enough. Tolstoy's claim that the important time is now sits oddly beside the story's own plot, which is built on a decision made long ago, the king's execution of his enemy's brother, and on its consequences in the present. The past, in other words, keeps returning in the story, and the lesson about the present does not fully account for it. That tension is easy to miss but may be the most interesting thing about the tale.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This is a fable, and like all fables its roots are ancient. It belongs with the tales of kings who seek wisdom from a humble sage, which turn up in Eastern and Western traditions alike, and with the Gospel teaching that what we do for the least of people we do for God. Its central idea, that the present moment is the only one in which we can act, echoes Stoic philosophers like Marcus Aurelius, who was himself an emperor, and Buddhist teachings on mindfulness.

Within Tolstoy's work, it belongs with the late tales and with his final religious essays. It is the lightest and clearest statement of an ethic he elaborated at length in works like The Kingdom of God Is Within You and What Then Must We Do? Its descendants are numerous. The picture-book version by Jon Muth is one, and the many self-help books that repeat the formula of now, here, this person are another. The tradition of the wise hermit who answers a seeker with a deed rather than a lecture also appears in later fiction, though it is difficult to draw a straight line.

It is worth reading now because the questions are timeless and the answers are still uncomfortable. We live distracted lives, and the story reminds us how rarely we are fully with the person in front of us. It also reminds us that wisdom may be less a body of knowledge than a habit of attention. At about fifteen hundred words, it takes a few minutes to read, and its last paragraph is among the most quotable in Tolstoy.

A last thing to say is about what kind of reading experience the story offers. It is not a plot-driven thriller or a character study. It is closer to a guided meditation, with a question, a pause, an action, and an answer, and that is perhaps why it has found a home in contemporary writing on attention and presence. Anyone who finds a long book hard to start can read this one in the time it takes to make tea, and may find it harder to forget than most books ten times as long.
