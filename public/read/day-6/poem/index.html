Nightly Poem • Romantic Ode & Lyric

Kubla Khan

Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1816) • 6 min read

Subtitled 'A Vision in a Dream,' this hypnotic fragment maps the walled pleasure-dome of Xanadu above a sunless, measureless abyss of creative imagination.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Composed in the autumn of 1797 at an Exmoor farmhouse near Porlock, Somerset, after Samuel Taylor Coleridge took laudanum while reading Samuel Purchas’s 1613 account of Emperor Kublai Khan’s summer palace at Xanadu, 'Kubla Khan' was published in 1816 at Lord Byron’s urging. Famously interrupted according to Coleridge’s preface by 'a person on business from Porlock,' its fifty-four lines shift from hypnotic iambic tetrameter chant to surging pentameter to map the Romantic imagination itself—contrasting the emperor’s walled, rational pleasure-dome with the savage subterranean chasm of the unconscious and the ecstatic song of the Abyssinian maid.

In Xanadu did Kubla Khan

A stately pleasure-dome decree:

Where Alph, the sacred river, ran

Through caverns measureless to man

Down to a sunless sea.

So twice five miles of fertile ground

With walls and towers were girdled round;

And there were gardens bright with sinuous rills,

Where blossomed many an incense-bearing tree;

And here were forests ancient as the hills,

Enfolding sunny spots of greenery.

But oh! that deep romantic chasm which slanted

Down the green hill athwart a cedarn cover!

A savage place! as holy and enchanted

As e’er beneath a waning moon was haunted

By woman wailing for her demon-lover!

And from this chasm, with ceaseless turmoil seething,

As if this earth in fast thick pants were breathing,

A mighty fountain momently was forced:

Amid whose swift half-intermitted burst

Huge fragments vaulted like rebounding hail,

Or chaffy grain beneath the thresher’s flail:

And mid these dancing rocks at once and ever

It flung up momently the sacred river.

Five miles meandering with a mazy motion

Through wood and dale the sacred river ran,

Then reached the caverns measureless to man,

And sank in tumult to a lifeless ocean;

And ’mid this tumult Kubla heard from far

Ancestral voices prophesying war!

The shadow of the dome of pleasure

Floated midway on the waves;

Where was heard the mingled measure

From the fountain and the caves.

It was a miracle of rare device,

A sunny pleasure-dome with caves of ice!

A damsel with a dulcimer

In a vision once I saw:

It was an Abyssinian maid

And on her dulcimer she played,

Singing of Mount Abora.

Could I revive within me

Her symphony and song,

To such a deep delight ’twould win me,

That with music loud and long,

I would build that dome in air,

That sunny dome! those caves of ice!

And all who heard should see them there,

And all should cry, Beware! Beware!

His flashing eyes, his floating hair!

Weave a circle round him thrice,

And close your eyes with holy dread

For he on honey-dew hath fed,

And drunk the milk of Paradise.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Composed in the autumn of 1797 at a lonely Exmoor farmhouse near Porlock, Somerset, 'Kubla Khan; or, A Vision in a Dream: A Fragment' remained unpublished until 1816, when Lord Byron persuaded Samuel Taylor Coleridge to print it. In his famous preface, Coleridge recounted taking two grains of opium (laudanum) for an indisposition and falling asleep while reading Samuel Purchas's 1613 travelogue Purchas His Pilgrimage: 'In Xamdu did Cublai Can build a stately Palace...' For three hours he experienced vivid visual images that composed themselves into two to three hundred lines of verse; upon waking, he eagerly wrote down the fifty-four lines we possess before being interrupted by 'a person on business from Porlock,' after which the rest of the vision vanished like ripples on a stream.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Far from being an arbitrary opium hallucination, 'Kubla Khan' is the definitive Romantic allegory of the creative imagination and the unconscious mind. In stanza one, Emperor Kubla Khan decrees a walled, geometric 'stately pleasure-dome'—representing conscious human artifice trying to enclose and master nature. Yet in stanza two, the true source of life and art erupts from a 'deep romantic chasm,' a savage, erotic, subterranean fountain (the unconscious) that flings up the sacred river Alph before plunging into 'caverns measureless to man' and a 'sunless sea.' The miracle of great art lies in the reconciled paradox at the end of stanza two: 'A sunny pleasure-dome with caves of ice!'—conscious sunlit form suspended over the freezing, untamable depths of the abyss.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Coleridge's prosody shifts dynamically across the three movements to enact the topography of Xanadu. The opening five lines establish an hypnotic chant in iambic tetrameter (`In XAN-a-DU did KUB-la KHAN`) rich in liquid consonants (`l`, `r`, `m`, `n`) and assonance. When the poem plunges into the chasm in stanza two, the meter expands into surging iambic pentameter with feminine endings ('slanted / enchanted', 'cover / lover', 'seething / breathing') that imitate the earth panting in 'fast thick pants.' Alliteration reaches a tour de force in 'Five miles meandering with a mazy motion,' before shifting in stanza three to brisk trochaic and iambic trimeter/tetrameter as the speaker invokes the Abyssinian maid and the ecstatic bardic trance.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Initially dismissed by rigid neoclassical reviewers like William Hazlitt as 'a musical composition' with little meaning, 'Kubla Khan' became a sacred text for the Pre-Raphaelites, Symbolists, and Surrealists. John Livingston Lowes's landmark 1927 study The Road to Xanadu traced virtually every image in the poem to Coleridge's voracious reading in travel accounts, proving how a well-stocked subconscious fuses disparate books into visionary unity. In pop culture, its opening lines open Orson Welles's Citizen Kane (1941), where newspaper tycoon Charles Foster Kane builds his own doomed Florida 'Xanadu,' and the phrase 'person from Porlock' entered the English lexicon as the universal symbol of mundane interruptions to creative flow.
