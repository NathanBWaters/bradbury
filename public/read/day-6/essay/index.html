Nightly Essay • Lyrical Nature & Philosophical Essay

Walking

Henry David Thoreau (1862) • 12 min read

I wish to speak a word for Nature, for absolute freedom and wildness: Thoreau's late masterpiece on the etymology of sauntering and why in Wildness is the preservation of the World.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Henry David Thoreau gave versions of this essay as lectures through the 1850s, and he was still revising it in the spring of 1862, when tuberculosis was killing him. It appeared in the Atlantic Monthly just weeks after he died at forty-four. That timing matters, because the essay reads like a last will: a plain-spoken argument for how to spend a life. Thoreau lived in Concord, Massachusetts, in a country that was building railroads, factories and cities at astonishing speed, and arguing bitterly with itself about slavery. His answer to all that busyness was radical in its simplicity. Walk, every day, for hours, with no destination and no errand. Along the way he plays with the word sauntering, and then pushes a bigger idea: that nature, and the wildness inside people, is where freedom and creativity come from. Listen for how he moves between jokes, scolding and sudden bursts of beauty. The essay's overall meaning is that a civilization stays alive only by staying in touch with what it has not tamed.

I wish to speak a word for Nature, for absolute freedom and wildness, as contrasted with a freedom and culture merely civil,—to regard man as an inhabitant, or a part and parcel of Nature, rather than a member of society. I wish to make an extreme statement, if so I may make an emphatic one, for there are enough champions of civilization: the minister and the school-committee and every one of you will take care of that.

I have met with but one or two persons in the course of my life who understood the art of Walking, that is, of taking walks,—who had a genius, so to speak, for sauntering: which word is beautifully derived 'from idle people who roved about the country, in the Middle Ages, and asked charity, under pretense of going à la Sainte Terre,' to the Holy Land, till the children exclaimed, 'There goes a Sainte-Terrer,' a Saunterer, a Holy-Lander. They who never go to the Holy Land in their walks, as they pretend, are indeed mere idlers and vagabonds; but they who do go there are saunterers in the good sense, such as I mean. Some, however, would derive the word from sans terre, without land or a home, which, therefore, in the good sense, will mean, having no particular home, but equally at home everywhere. For this is the secret of successful sauntering. He who sits still in a house all the time may be the greatest vagrant of all; but the saunterer, in the good sense, is no more vagrant than the meandering river, which is all the while sedulously seeking the shortest course to the sea.

I think that I cannot preserve my health and spirits, unless I spend four hours a day at least—and it is commonly more than that—sauntering through the woods and over the hills and fields, absolutely free from all worldly engagements. You may safely say, A penny for your thoughts, or a thousand pounds. When sometimes I am reminded that the mechanics and shopkeepers stay in their shops not only all the forenoon, but all the afternoon too, sitting with crossed legs, so many of them,—as if the legs were made to sit upon, and not to stand or walk upon,—I think that they deserve some credit for not having all committed suicide long ago.

Moreover, you must walk like a camel, which is said to be the only beast which ruminates when walking. When a traveler asked Wordsworth's servant to show him her master's study, she answered, 'Here is his library, but his study is out of doors.' Living much out of doors, in the sun and wind, will no doubt produce a certain roughness of character,—will cause a thicker cuticle to grow over some of the finer qualities of our nature, as on the face and hands, or as severe manual labor robs the hands of some of their delicate tactile power. So staying in the house, on the other hand, may produce a softness and smoothness, not to say thinness of skin, accompanied by an increased sensibility to certain impressions. Perhaps we should be more susceptible to some influences important to our intellectual and moral growth, if the sun had shone and the wind blown on us a little less; and no doubt it is a nice matter to proportion rightly the thick and thin skin. But methinks that is a scurf that will fall off fast enough,—that the natural remedy is to be found in the proportion which the night bears to the day, the winter to the year, thought to experience.

I am alarmed when it happens that I have walked a mile into the woods bodily, without getting there in spirit. In my afternoon walk I would fain forget all my morning occupations and my obligations to society. But it sometimes happens that I cannot easily shake off the village. The thought of some work will run in my head and I am not where my body is,—I am out of my senses. In my walks I would fain return to my senses. What business have I in the woods, if I am thinking of something out of the woods?

The West of which I speak is but another name for the Wild; and what I have been preparing to say is, that in Wildness is the preservation of the World. Every tree sends its fibers forth in search of the Wild. The cities import it at any price. Men plow and sail for it. From the forest and wilderness come the tonics and barks which brace mankind. Our ancestors were savages. The story of Romulus and Remus being suckled by a wolf is not a meaningless fable. The founders of every state which has risen to eminence have drawn their nourishment and vigor from a similar wild source. It was because the children of the Empire were not suckled by the wolf that they were conquered and displaced by the children of the northern forests who were.

Life consists with wildness. The most alive is the wildest. Not yet subdued to man, its presence refreshes him. One who pressed forward incessantly and never rested from his labors, who grew fast and made infinite demands on life, would always find himself in a new country or wilderness, and surrounded by the raw material of life. He would be climbing over the prostrate stems of primitive forest-trees. Hope and the future for me are not in lawns and cultivated fields, not in towns and cities, but in the impervious and quaking swamps.

In literature it is only the wild that attracts us. Dullness is but another name for tameness. It is the uncivilized free and wild thinking in Hamlet and The Iliad, in all the Scriptures and Mythologies, not learned in the schools, that delights us. As the wild duck is more swift and beautiful than the tame, so is the wild—the mallard—thought, which 'mid falling dews wings its solitary way above the marshes. A truly good book is something as natural, and as unexpectedly and unaccountably fair and perfect, as a wild flower discovered on the prairies of the West or in the jungles of the East. Genius is a light which makes the darkness visible, like the lightning's flash, which perchance shatters the temple of knowledge itself,—and not a taper lighted at the hearth-stone of the race, which pales before the light of common day.

We have heard of a Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge. It is said that knowledge is power; and the like. Methinks there is equal need of a Society for the Diffusion of Useful Ignorance, what we will call Beautiful Knowledge, a knowledge useful in a higher sense: for what is most of our boasted so-called knowledge but a conceit that we know something, which robs us of the advantage of our actual ignorance? What we call knowledge is often our positive ignorance; ignorance our negative knowledge. By long years of patient industry and reading of the newspapers,—for what are the libraries of science but files of newspapers?—a man accumulates a myriad facts, lays them up in his memory, and then when in some spring of his life he saunters abroad into the Great Fields of thought, he, as it were, goes to grass like a horse and leaves all his harness behind in the stable.

Above all, we cannot afford not to live in the present. He is blessed over all mortals who loses no moment of the passing life in remembering the past. Unless our philosophy hears the cock crow in every barn-yard within our horizon, it is belated. That sound commonly reminds us that we are growing rusty and antique in our employments and habits of thought. His philosophy comes down to a more recent time than ours. There is something suggested by it that is a newer testament,—the gospel according to this moment. So we saunter toward the Holy Land, till one day the sun shall shine more brightly than ever he has done, shall perchance shine into our minds and hearts, and light up our whole lives with a great awakening light, as warm and serene and golden as on a bankside in autumn.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Thoreau opens by announcing that he wants to speak for Nature, for absolute freedom and wildness, as opposed to the kind of freedom that merely comes from living politely in society. He admits he is making an extreme statement on purpose, because there are plenty of people already speaking up for civilization. Notice the joke in that: the minister and the school committee will take care of it. From the first paragraph he is a man picking a fight with the respectable audience in the room.

Then comes the word that gives the essay its heart. He says he has met only one or two people who understood the art of walking, who had a genius for sauntering. He offers two possible origins of the word. One is that medieval wanderers claimed to be heading to the Holy Land, la Sainte Terre, so that children called them Sainte-Terrers, saunterers. The other is that it comes from sans terre, without land or home, which he reads as being at home everywhere. Whether or not the etymology is sound, the point is that a walk can be a pilgrimage. It does not matter whether you ever reach Jerusalem. What matters is that you set out as if something sacred were ahead of you.

This is why Thoreau says that a man who sits still in a house all the time may be the greatest vagrant of all. The restless, busy person who never really inhabits his own life is the true wanderer. The saunterer, by contrast, is like a meandering river, which looks aimless but is steadily finding its way to the sea.

Underneath the charm there is a serious claim. Thoreau thinks human beings are part of nature, not just members of a town or a church or a nation. Health, thought and morals all depend on contact with ground that nobody has fenced, planted or mortgaged. He says he cannot keep his health and spirits unless he spends at least four hours a day wandering the woods and fields, free of worldly engagements. He treats that as a requirement, like food.

Later the essay turns toward direction. He finds that his afternoon walks drift southwest, toward the open country and, symbolically, toward the future. Westward, he says, he goes free, while east means the past and the settled world. This can sound like a mere habit of his feet, but he lifts it into a theory about where the human spirit wants to go. It is also the part of the essay a modern reader has to handle with care, since westward expansion was bound up with the dispossession of Native peoples.

The essay's most quoted idea is that in wildness is the preservation of the world. He does not mean that everyone should live in a forest. He means that cultures grow stale unless something untamed feeds them, whether that is forest, swamp, or the unruly part of the imagination. He even says that hope for him lies not in lawns and cultivated fields but in the quaking swamps. The ending, with the setting sun lighting up the mind and heart, tells you the real subject. The walk is a way of preparing to be awake.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, "Walking" is both dazzling and uneven, and it is worth admitting both. Its best sentences are among the finest in American prose. Thoreau can take a plain observation and turn it into an image that stays in your head for years. The meandering river sentence is a good example: in a single comparison it defends the whole idea of the unhurried life and makes it sound purposeful. His humor is also underrated. He is a scold who knows he is a scold, and the exaggeration, the sly puns and the mock-heroic tone make a demanding argument easy to enjoy.

The essay's structure is its biggest weakness. It was stitched together from lectures, and you can feel the seams. The opening is tightly organized around sauntering, but the middle wanders into long discussions of America, the West, the supposed decline of European animals and plants in the New World, and the poetry of the wild. These sections do not build on one another so much as sit side by side. A listener hearing it aloud may feel the energy drop and rise rather than climb. You could argue that a walking essay should wander, and Thoreau might say so himself, but intentional looseness and plain looseness are not the same, and the essay does not always control the difference.

Some of his rhetoric has also aged badly. The claim that nature grows bigger and better in America, and that people and animals do too, is closer to patriotic boasting than observation, and it sits oddly beside his own scorn for national pride elsewhere. His hostility to nearly everyone who works for a living can read as snobbery. When he complains that most people are not really walking, he forgets that most people did not have four free hours every afternoon. The ideal of idle sauntering depends on leisure that Thoreau had, partly through the help of his family and friends, and partly through a willingness to live on very little.

There is also a gap between the lofty talk about wildness and the landscape he actually walked. The Concord countryside was farmland, old pasture and woodlots, shaped by centuries of human use. His wild was often a tamed place seen with a visionary eye. That is not necessarily a flaw, since the essay is about attention more than geography. But it makes his sharp contrast between wild and civil look a bit too neat.

Still, when the essay works, nothing quite matches it. The sunset ending, with its slow sentence that gathers light until it floods the mind, is a real piece of music. The piece succeeds less as an argument you can diagram than as a mood and a habit of mind you catch from him. It persuades by making you want to put the book down and go outside, and that is a rare test for an essay to pass. On balance it is a flawed masterpiece, brilliant in passages and patchy as a whole.

III. The Impact It Had

The most direct legacy of "Walking" is the modern American conservation movement. Thoreau was not an organizer, and he did not found anything, but his phrase about the preservation of the world became a kind of motto. John Muir, who came a generation later and spent his life defending wild places like Yosemite, drew on the same current of thought, and the Sierra Club has long used Thoreau's line. When the United States passed the Wilderness Act in 1964, creating a legal category for land left essentially untouched, many people saw it as Thoreau's idea turning into law. The writer Wallace Stegner, in his famous letter defending wilderness, argued that we need wild country even if we never visit it, which is very much the Thoreauvian claim that wildness feeds a civilization from a distance.

The essay also shaped how people think about walking itself. It helped create the idea that walking is a form of thinking and a form of resistance. Later writers have followed that thread, from nature writers like Edward Abbey and Gary Snyder to modern cultural historians of walking such as Rebecca Solnit, who treats Thoreau as one of the central figures in the long story of people going on foot to think. The sense that a slow, unplugged walk is good for the mind has become so common that it is almost a cliché, and a good part of the origin of that cliché is here.

In the arts and politics the essay worked more quietly. Thoreau's other writings, especially the essay on civil disobedience, influenced Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr., and "Walking" belongs to the same habit of mind: trust your own conscience and your own legs over the opinions of the crowd. It does not preach political action directly, but it models a refusal to let institutions define what counts as a good life.

In literature classes the essay is taught as a prime example of American Transcendentalism, the movement that Ralph Waldo Emerson led, which held that the individual can know truth directly through intuition and through nature. It is also taught as a short, accessible entry into Thoreau for students who find Walden long. Teachers often pair it with nature writing, with environmental history, and with debates about what we mean when we say wilderness.

The essay has had a long afterlife outside the classroom as well. Outdoor brands, hiking clubs, mindfulness writers and anyone arguing against screens or constant productivity are likely to quote it. Thoreau's name sits on trails, lectures and nature centers. Because he died just as the essay appeared, it has also taken on the glow of a final statement, a last testament delivered by someone who knew time was short. That gave it a force that a casual lecture might not have carried, and it helped fix Thoreau in the public mind as the patron saint of the person who steps away to see clearly.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved "Walking" first for its permission. Thoreau tells you that the aimless afternoon is not wasted time, and that sitting in a room all day is the real waste. For anyone who feels guilty about not being productive, that is a profound relief. Many readers describe the essay as one that gave them language for something they already felt, that a walk without a goal is a way of becoming more fully alive.

They have also loved its voice. Thoreau is cranky, funny and unembarrassed. He is willing to say that most people do not know how to walk, and he says it with enough wit that the reader laughs at themselves rather than bristles. The extravagance is part of the pleasure. He is not careful or balanced, and he tells you so. A reader who is tired of hedged and cautious writing finds this bracing, like a cold swim.

The images are a third source of delight. The saunterer as a Holy-Lander, the river that looks aimless and is not, the swamp as a symbol of hope, the sun that finally shines into the mind and heart and lights the whole life: these are pictures that readers carry away and repeat. People who have never read the full essay often know a line or two of it, because the best phrases seem to float free and become part of the language. Thoreau had a gift for making ideas feel like things you could touch.

Naturalists and writers have admired the essay for its close attention to the actual world. Thoreau was a skilled observer who carried a notebook and spyglass, and some of that precision shows up in the way he names plants, light and weather even in a philosophical essay. For writers, this is a lesson: big ideas land best when anchored in the concrete.

Finally, readers respond to the feeling of urgency under the humor. Thoreau was dying as he finished the piece, and a thoughtful reader can sense that he is trying to get something essential said. There is no padding in the emotional core. When he writes about the evening light and the possibility of a great awakening, it reads as a man giving what he has left. This is part of why the essay is so often recommended to people at turning points in their lives, to students deciding what kind of life to build, and to anyone worn down by work and noise. They find in it a calm, stubborn encouragement to look up and walk out the door.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Criticism of "Walking" has grown louder in recent decades, and much of it is fair. The most serious objection concerns the idea of wilderness itself. When Thoreau praises the untamed West and the unpeopled wild, he is writing as though those lands were empty, when they had been home to Native nations for thousands of years. Scholars of environmental history, most famously William Cronon in his essay on the trouble with wilderness, have argued that the notion of untouched nature is a cultural invention that helped justify pushing Indigenous people off their land and into reservations. Thoreau was not an enthusiastic participant in that, and in other writings he showed real interest in Native life, but "Walking" does lean on a frontier mythology that readers now find hard to ignore.

A second criticism is about privilege. The saunterer needs time and security, and Thoreau's contempt for people who work long hours can come across as disdain. He tells people to stop being slaves to business, but he does not much reckon with the fact that for many workers and for the enslaved people whose cause he elsewhere supported, there was no choice. Critics also point out that the sauntering ideal was mostly open to men. A woman walking alone in the nineteenth century faced dangers and rules that Thoreau never had to consider.

There are objections on the grounds of logic as well. The essay asserts rather than proves. Its claim that animals and men shrink in Europe and flourish in America is not backed by evidence and now looks like wishful nationalism. Even in his own day, friends and readers found him contrary and sometimes unfair. Emerson, in his eulogy, praised Thoreau's character and genius but also admitted a streak of stubborn opposition, a tendency to say no to almost everything, which shows in the essay's scolding side.

Some readers find the piece plain disorganized. Because it grew from lectures, it has digressions that go on a bit long, and repetition that a spoken version might forgive but a page does not. A modern reader may feel that the first third is brilliant and the rest is a lovely but rambling coda.

Finally, there is the charge of hypocrisy that has followed Thoreau all his life. He praises roaming free and condemns civilization, yet he walked home to a village, ate bread made by others, and relied on his family's pencil business and on neighbors for support. Defenders reply that he never claimed to be a hermit, and that the essay's point is about a way of attending, not a rule of life. The criticism is still useful, because it keeps the essay honest and reminds us to take the vision seriously without taking the man as a saint.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"Walking" belongs to American Transcendentalism, a movement of the 1830s and 1840s centered around Concord. Its leader, Ralph Waldo Emerson, wrote that nature is a language through which the spirit speaks, and Thoreau took that idea literally and put on his boots. Transcendentalism itself grew from European Romanticism, in which poets such as Wordsworth and Coleridge turned to wild landscape as a source of truth and feeling. Wordsworth in particular was a famous walker, and it is fair to see Thoreau as an American version of that tradition, with more humor and more suspicion of institutions.

Behind both lies a much older tradition of the pilgrimage, which Thoreau slyly invokes with his Holy Land etymology, and of the philosopher who thinks while moving. Ancient Greek teachers walked and talked, and writers from Rousseau to Nietzsche have praised walking as the best condition for thought. "Walking" turns that long tradition into something both practical and democratic, because anyone with a pair of legs can try it.

Looking forward, the essay is a founding text of American nature writing and environmentalism. It sits in a line that runs through John Muir and John Burroughs, then Aldo Leopold, Rachel Carson, Edward Abbey, Annie Dillard, Gary Snyder and Wendell Berry. Each of them, in different ways, takes up Thoreau's insistence that attention to the natural world is a moral and spiritual act. Contemporary writing about forest bathing, rewilding and the psychology of attention also echoes him, whether or not it names him.

Compared with his other work, "Walking" is looser and more mystical than Walden, which is built around the experiment of living at Walden Pond and has a clearer arc. It is also less directly political than the essay on civil disobedience, though both share a refusal to be managed by the state. "Walking" may be the best place to meet Thoreau's cheerful extremism in a small space. It shows his humor, his flaws and his genius all at once.

It still matters today because the problem it addresses has grown larger. Thoreau worried that railroads and commerce were eating up the day and the mind, and that people were losing their ability to simply be somewhere. Replace the railroad with the phone and the point lands harder. The essay does not give a policy or a program. It gives a practice, and a reason for the practice, in a voice that is hard to resist. Read it with its blind spots in view, particularly on land and who was already living on it, and it remains a bracing reminder that a good life needs open ground, some free time, and a mind willing to wander far enough to discover where it actually wants to go.
