Bridging the Golden Gate: Joseph Strauss, Irving Morrow, and the Birth of International Orange (1933–1937)

San Francisco — How mathematicians Charles Ellis and Leon Moisseiff, deep-sea divers in ripping tides, and a stubborn architect built 'the bridge that couldn't be built' (Great Depression Engineering (1933–1937)) • 20 min read

“At last the mighty task is done; Resplendent in the western sun, The Bridge looms mountain high.” — Joseph B. Strauss, 'The Mighty Task Is Done' (May 1937)

For more than half a century before the first stick of dynamite was lowered into the tide off Fort Point, the consensus of the world’s civil engineers, the U.S. War Department, and the Southern Pacific Railroad—which owned the lucrative Golden Gate Ferry monopoly carrying forty thousand commuters and cars a day—was unanimous: bridging the one-mile-wide mouth of San Francisco Bay was physically and financially impossible. That narrow gap between the northern tip of the San Francisco peninsula and the Marin Headlands is a drowned Pleistocene river gorge carved through Franciscan bedrock by the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers during the last Ice Age. Four times every day, fourteen billion cubic feet of water roar in and out of the strait at speeds reaching seven and a half knots through a submarine canyon 372 feet deep, battered by sixty-mile-per-hour Pacific gales, blinded all summer by dense advection fog, and flanked just seven miles offshore by the submerged trace of the San Andreas Fault.

Into this tempest stepped Joseph Baermann Strauss, a five-foot-tall, Cincinnati-born poet and bridge engineer whose slight physique concealed an almost volcanic ambition. Born in 1870 to a mother who was a concert pianist and a father who painted portraits, Strauss had been hospitalized at the University of Cincinnati after being battered on the college football field, spending his recovery studying structural mechanics while composing romantic verse. After graduating in 1892, he revolutionized railroad and municipal bridge construction by inventing an economical concrete-counterweighted trunnion bascule drawbridge, building more than four hundred movable spans from Chicago to Saint Petersburg. When San Francisco’s City Engineer Michael Maurice O’Shaughnessy—fresh from damming Hetch Hetchy—asked Strauss in 1919 whether the Golden Gate could be spanned for less than the hundred-million-dollar estimates that had paralyzed city planners, Strauss looked across the whitecaps and promised he could build a bridge for under thirty million dollars.

There was, however, a glaring flaw in the initial bridge design Strauss unveiled on June 28, 1921: it was a monstrously awkward structural hybrid combining two gargantuan steel cantilever trusses extending from either shore with a stubby central suspension span slung between them. Architectural critics and newspaper cartoonists immediately ridiculed the drawing as 'two giant mousetraps with an upside-down rat-trap dangling in the middle,' while shipping magnates warned that should an earthquake or warship strike one of the cantilevered arms, the fallen steel would cork the Golden Gate and trap the Pacific Fleet inside the bay. Undeterred by aesthetic mockery or the two thousand lawsuits financed by Southern Pacific Railroad front groups, Strauss spent twelve grueling years barnstorming across six Northern California counties—San Francisco, Marin, Sonoma, Napa, Mendocino, and Del Norte—until the California Legislature incorporated the Golden Gate Bridge and Highway District in December 1928.

By the summer of 1929, recognizing that his cantilever hybrid was both technologically obsolete and visually unforgivable at the portal of the Pacific, Strauss quietly assembled a world-class Board of Consulting Engineers that discarded his 1921 sketch entirely. To conceive a pure, unbroken suspension span stretching 4,200 feet between tower piers—longer by seven hundred feet than New York’s George Washington Bridge and longer than any span ever attempted—Strauss turned to Latvian-born New York bridge theoretician Leon S. Moisseiff. Designer of the Manhattan Bridge, Moisseiff had pioneered 'deflection theory,' a breakthrough in elastic structural mechanics proving that in an ultra-long suspension bridge, the immense dead weight of the steel cables and roadway deck itself resists wind and live traffic deformations, allowing engineers to replace bulky iron cages with a slender, flexible stiffening truss that could bend gracefully with the ocean gales.

While Moisseiff provided the overarching theoretical framework from New York, the heroic day-to-day mathematical architecture and structural design of the Golden Gate Bridge fell to Strauss’s vice president in Chicago, Professor Charles Alton Ellis. Born in Parkman, Maine, in 1876 and educated as a scholar of classical Greek and mathematics at Wesleyan University before teaching civil engineering at the University of Michigan and the University of Illinois, Ellis was a quiet perfectionist who had co-authored the definitive American textbook on frame structures. Working in Chicago long before electronic computers existed, armed only with Keuffel & Esser slide rules, seven-place logarithmic tables, and mechanical calculators, Ellis personally solved tens of thousands of simultaneous differential equations to determine how the 746-foot-tall steel towers and 36.5-inch-diameter cables would behave under sixty-knot lateral winds, fifty-degree temperature swings, and seismic ground accelerations.

It was Charles Ellis who engineered the bridge’s most celebrated structural innovation: the stepped, cellular steel towers. Instead of lacing the twin towers with unsightly external diagonal X-bracing, Ellis designed each tower leg as a honeycomb bundle of more than 600,000 shop-riveted rectangular steel cells—3.5 feet square—that taper inward in graceful setbacks as vertical load diminishes with height, transferring 123 million pounds of downward cable force per tower into the bedrock while allowing the 746-foot spires to flex up to sixteen inches toward the channel or shore. Yet as the Great Depression deepened in late 1931 and Ellis insisted on verifying rock core samples with UC Berkeley geologist Andrew C. Lawson (who had named the San Andreas Fault in 1895), the insecure Strauss grew fearful that Ellis would overshadow his legacy. In late November 1931, three days before Ellis was scheduled to present the final calculations to the Bridge Board, Strauss ordered him to take an 'indefinite unpaid vacation' and erased Charles Alton Ellis’s name completely from the blueprints and the 1937 bronze dedication plaque—an injustice rectified only seventy years later when the Bridge District installed a permanent bronze statue honoring Ellis at the South Plaza.

Even with Ellis’s master blueprints completed, the bridge nearly died in the cradle of the Great Depression. On November 4, 1930, voters across the six counties of the Bridge District had performed an extraordinary act of civic faith, voting overwhelmingly to pledge their private homes, dairy ranches, and redwood timberlands as collateral for a $35 million construction bond issue. Yet when the California Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court dismissed the Southern Pacific’s last legal challenges in 1932, the nation’s banking system was frozen; Wall Street syndicates refused to buy the 5 percent municipal bonds at par. Once again, as in the aftermath of the 1906 earthquake, San Francisco was rescued by Amadeo Peter Giannini, founder of the Bank of Italy and Bank of America. Summoning Strauss and General Manager Alan MacDonald to his San Francisco office in May 1932, Giannini looked at the blueprints and declared: 'San Francisco needs that bridge; we will take the bonds.' With Bank of America purchasing the initial $6 million tranche to put thousands of unemployed Californians to work, construction began on January 5, 1933.

Before the ironworkers could spin a single wire across the water, Charles Ellis and consulting architect Irving F. Morrow solved a delicate crisis of historical preservation at the southern shoreline in the Presidio. Directly beneath the planned southern approach stood Fort Point, a magnificent three-tiered red-brick masonry fortress erected by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers between 1853 and 1861—modeled on Fort Sumter—to guard the harbor during the Civil War. Standard highway engineering called for dynamiting the brick bastion down to sea level to make room for a concrete pylon. Instead, recognizing the irreplaceable beauty of the nineteenth-century brick casemates and spiral granite staircases, Ellis and Morrow designed a soaring 319-foot, four-hinged steel arch that leaps directly over the entire roof of Fort Point like a Gothic cathedral’s flying buttress, sheltering the brick ramparts intact beneath the thundering deck.

Across the strait on the Marin shore beneath the sheer cliffs of Lime Point, crews from the Pacific Bridge Company made rapid progress on the North Tower Pier, blasting a three-sided cofferdam into dense radiolarian chert and diabase basalt and completing the concrete monolith in June 1933. The South Tower Pier, however, proved to be the single most perilous marine engineering campaign of the twentieth century. Its foundation had to rise 1,125 feet offshore in the open ocean swells off Fort Point, where six-knot tidal currents swept across a sloping underwater shelf of sheared, soapy green serpentinite—California’s state rock, notorious among geologists for turning slick and unstable when fractured by water.

Throughout the freezing winter and spring of 1933 and 1934, a small team of veteran deep-sea hardhat divers—led by William Reed, Chrisirk, and Artican—risked their lives every day ninety to one hundred ten feet beneath the churning surface. Encased in 190-pound bolted copper helmets, lead-weighted canvas suits, and twenty-pound iron boots, the divers could descend only during the brief twenty-minute windows of slack water between ebb and flood tides; if a diver remained on the bottom when the seven-knot current resumed, his air hose and lifeline would be snapped against the jagged rock like twine. Working in utter pitch-black darkness where underwater lamps could not penetrate a foot of silt-laden water, the divers crawled across the sea floor, feeling entirely by bruised hand to guide pneumatic drills from surface barges, packing black-powder charges into steel pipes to blast a thirty-five-foot-deep keyway into the serpentinite, and inspecting concrete pours by touch.

Above the divers, the Pacific Ocean fought back with relentless fury. In August 1933, a freighter sheared through the 1,100-foot wooden access trestle extending from Fort Point; on Halloween 1933, twenty-foot groundswells tore away eight hundred feet of the rebuilt trestle. Resident Engineer Russell G. Cone and the Pacific Bridge Company responded by constructing a football-field-sized elliptical concrete fender wall—300 feet long, 155 feet wide, and rising 115 feet from the ocean floor—assembled out of twenty-two interlocking vertical concrete columns guided down steel frames by the divers. In October 1934, when crews floated a 10,000-ton timber-and-steel caisson inside the unfinished fender ring, a violent Pacific storm turned the caisson into a battering ram that threatened to smash the fender wall from within. Cone ordered the four-hundred-thousand-dollar caisson dynamited loose, towed out to sea, and sunk by Coast Guard gunfire; engineers then sealed the concrete fender ring itself into a giant open-ocean cofferdam, poured a sixty-five-foot underwater concrete plug through tremie pipes, pumped out 9.4 million gallons of seawater, and completed the South Tower Pier in January 1935.

With both piers anchored to bedrock, Bethlehem Steel ironworkers drove the twin cellular towers 746 feet into the clouds during 1935, heating over 1.2 million steel rivets to cherry-red incandescence in portable coal forges and tossing them across the void into tin catching cones before hammering them tight with pneumatic guns. Then, in November 1935, John A. Roebling’s Sons Company—the legendary cable-makers of the Brooklyn Bridge—strung twin wooden catwalks from anchorage to anchorage and set giant double spinning wheels shuttling back and forth on overhead tramways. Working twenty-four hours a day for six months and nine days, Roebling’s crews spun 27,572 individual pencil-thick (0.196-inch) galvanized steel wires into sixty-one strands per cable—80,000 miles of wire in all, enough to circle the Earth’s equator more than three times—before hydraulic presses squeezed the strands into two 36.5-inch-diameter cylinders locked into 60,000-ton concrete anchorages.

High in the blinding salt wind, Joseph Strauss enforced the strictest industrial safety code ever seen on a heavy construction site. In an era when bridge contractors routinely budgeted one worker death for every million dollars of steel erected—meaning thirty-five men were statistically expected to die on the Golden Gate—Strauss mandated hard-boiled Bullard safety helmets, anti-glare tinted fog goggles, mobile canteen carts serving hot soup and sauerkraut juice on the catwalks to combat dizziness, and his crowning innovation: a $130,000 manila hemp safety net suspended thirty feet beneath the entire 6,450-foot span. Over four years, nineteen ironworkers who lost their footing plunged into the hemp net and climbed back to work as proud members of the 'Halfway to Hell Club.' For fifty months only a single worker, Kermit Moore, had been killed—until 9:40 AM on February 17, 1937, when a five-ton rolling stripping scaffold beneath the deck near the North Pier snapped its cast-aluminum hanger bracket, tore straight through the hemp safety net with a sound like machine-gun fire, and dragged twelve men 220 feet down into the icy five-knot tide, where ten men perished and only two—Slim Lambert and Fred Dummatzen—survived.

While engineers and ironworkers conquered the physics of the strait, consulting architect Irving F. Morrow—working alongside his wife and fellow UC Berkeley-trained architect Gertrude Comfort Morrow—transformed a utilitarian highway crossing into an Art Deco sculpture of astonishing refinement. Working from their office in the de Young Building at Market and Kearny streets, the Morrows convinced the Bridge District that a structure of such titanic scale required heroic geometry rather than applied ornament. Look closely at the 746-foot towers today: rather than rising as monotonous rectangular shafts, Morrow stepped the cellular steel plates inward at each horizontal portal brace, opening the upper portals so larger rectangles of blue sky shine through as the eye ascends, and beveled the vertical exterior ribs so the afternoon sun carves sharp, alternating bands of highlight and shadow down the legs, flanked at deck level by open vertical railing slats and fluted sodium-vapor streetlamps.

Yet Irving Morrow’s most audacious triumph was the bridge’s color. As the steel segments arrived by barge from Bethlehem Steel’s mills in Pennsylvania via the Panama Canal, a fierce battle erupted over how to paint the finished monument. The U.S. Navy demanded black-and-yellow diagonal stripes like a giant hornet so battleships would not ram the towers in the fog; the Army Air Corps insisted on red-and-white bands to warn biplanes flying from Crissy Field; and traditional engineers assumed the span would be coated in carbon black or aluminum gray like the Bay Bridge. Riding the Golden Gate ferries through the mist as the steel rose in its factory coat of reddish-orange red-lead rust primer, Morrow experienced an epiphany. On April 6, 1935, he submitted a twenty-nine-page treatise titled 'Report on Color and Lighting,' demonstrating that a warm terracotta vermilion—formulated as International Orange (69 percent magenta, 100 percent yellow, 6 percent cyan, and 6 percent black)—harmonized with the golden summer grasses and emerald winter slopes of the Marin Headlands, sang against the ultramarine Pacific and white fog, and offered superior visibility to ships.

At six o’clock on the morning of Thursday, May 27, 1937—Pedestrian Day, twenty-four hours before President Franklin D. Roosevelt pressed a telegraph key in Washington to open the span to automobiles—every foghorn and ship whistle in San Francisco Bay erupted as the gates swung open to 200,000 joyful citizens who walked, ran, and pushed baby carriages across the 1.7-mile span while an aging Joseph Strauss recited his poem 'The Mighty Task Is Done.' Eighty-three years later, in 2020, when engineers replaced the west sidewalk railing slats with thinner aerodynamic profiles to withstand hundred-mile-per-hour storms, the Golden Gate Bridge unexpectedly acquired a literal voice: whenever thirty-knot western gales blow through the new slats, the 4,200-foot span sings a haunting, multi-octave aeolian chord audible across the Marina and the Presidio—a living steel harp in International Orange, held aloft by the quiet equations of Charles Alton Ellis.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Suspension Physics & Why Charles Ellis Was Vindicated

Each of the Golden Gate Bridge's two main cables is 36.5 inches thick, spun in midair over six months by Roebling & Sons from 27,572 individual pencil-thick galvanized steel wires—enough wire to circle the Earth at the equator more than three times—anchored at each end into solid concrete blocks weighing 60,000 tons apiece. Charles Ellis's mathematical genius in designing the stepped cellular steel towers (which contain over 600,000 shop-riveted cells without external diagonal cross-bracing) allowed the towers to flex up to 16 inches toward the channel or shore as temperatures and traffic loads change while transferring 123 million pounds of vertical load per tower cleanly into the bedrock.

2. Irving Morrow's Art Deco Masterwork & The Singing Slats of 2020

Too often people credit only engineers for bridges, but consulting architect Irving Morrow (working alongside his wife, architect Gertrude Comfort Morrow) transformed a utilitarian highway structure into sculpture. Look closely at the towers as they rise 746 feet: Morrow stepped back the steel plates as the load diminishes near the top, opening up the upper portals so sky shines through, and bevelled the vertical ribs to catch morning and evening light. In 2020, when engineers replaced the west sidewalk railing slats with thinner aerodynamic profiles to withstand 100 mph winds, the Golden Gate Bridge literally began singing an eerie, multi-tone aeolian chord audible across the city whenever stiff western gales blow!

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three Golden Gate concepts in mind: first, International Orange, the signature red-lead primer hue championed by Irving Morrow and touched up continuously by ironworkers and painters against salt-fog corrosion; second, the 'Halfway to Hell Club,' the nineteen ironworkers saved from death by Joseph Strauss's innovative under-deck hemp safety net; and third, Charles Alton Ellis (1876–1949), whose statue and plaque at the South Vista Point now restore the erased mathematician to his rightful place in history.
