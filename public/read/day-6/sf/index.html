Bridging the Golden Gate: Joseph Strauss, Irving Morrow, and the Birth of International Orange (1933–1937)

San Francisco — How mathematicians Charles Ellis and Leon Moisseiff, deep-sea divers in ripping tides, and a stubborn architect built 'the bridge that couldn't be built' (Great Depression Engineering (1933–1937)) • 11 min read

“At last the mighty task is done; Resplendent in the western sun, The Bridge looms mountain high.” — Joseph B. Strauss, 'The Mighty Task Is Done' (May 1937)

For decades, the consensus of the world’s civil engineers, the U.S. War Department, and the Southern Pacific Railroad (which owned the lucrative Golden Gate ferry monopoly) was unanimous: bridging the one-mile-wide mouth of San Francisco Bay was physically and financially impossible. Through that narrow submarine canyon between the Presidio’s Fort Point and the Marin Headlands, the entire tidal volume of San Francisco Bay and the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta roars in and out four times a day at speeds reaching seven knots through 372-foot-deep water, battered by sixty-mile-per-hour Pacific gales, blinded by summer fog, and flanked just seven miles offshore by the submarine trace of the San Andreas Fault.

Enter Joseph Baermann Strauss, a five-foot-tall, Cincinnati-born poet and engineer who had designed over four hundred movable trunnion bascule drawbridges across the Midwest. When San Francisco City Engineer Michael O’Shaughnessy challenged Strauss in 1919 to build a span across the Golden Gate for under $30 million (after earlier estimates exceeded $100 million), Strauss spent twelve years campaigning tirelessly from town hall to town hall across six Northern California counties to form the Golden Gate Bridge and Highway District.

There was only one problem with Strauss’s original 1921 bridge design: it was a monstrously ugly hybrid combining two massive steel cantilever trusses with a short central suspension span that critics compared to 'two giant Mousetraps with an upside-down rat-trap in the middle.' Realizing that only a pure, soaring 4,200-foot main suspension span—longer than any bridge ever built on Earth—could conquer the strait with structural grace, Strauss quietly brought onto his team two brilliant structural mathematicians: Leon Moisseiff (designer of the Manhattan Bridge and pioneer of 'deflection theory,' which allowed thin, flexible suspension decks to bend with the wind) and University of Illinois professor Charles Alton Ellis.

Working in Chicago with slide rules and logarithm tables before electronic computers existed, Charles Ellis performed the tens of thousands of simultaneous differential equations required to calculate how the 746-foot-tall cellular steel towers and 36.5-inch-diameter cables would respond to wind loads, temperature changes, and earthquakes. Yet when the Great Depression struck and Ellis refused to stop refining his safety calculations on schedule, the insecure Strauss abruptly fired Ellis in late 1931 and erased his name entirely from the bronze plaque mounted on the finished bridge—an injustice rectified only seventy years later when the Bridge District finally installed a permanent tribute honoring Ellis as the true structural architect of the span.

Even after voters across six counties mortgaged their homes, farms, and orchards in November 1930 to pass a $35 million bond issue, Wall Street banks refused to buy the bonds at the bottom of the Depression in 1932. San Francisco was rescued once again by A. P. Giannini—the founder of the Bank of Italy/Bank of America from our Night 4 story—who sat down with Strauss and announced: 'San Francisco needs that bridge; we will buy the bonds.' Construction finally kicked off on January 5, 1933.

The single most perilous engineering feat of the entire four-year build was the South Tower Pier, located 1,125 feet offshore in the open ocean currents off Fort Point. Deep-sea hardhat divers like Chrisirk and William Reed plunged ninety feet into freezing, pitch-black water where tidal currents ripped at their rubber suits, setting dynamite charges into the slippery green *serpentinite* bedrock and guiding concrete chutes. After a lost barge and a brutal October storm smashed the first access trestle, crews constructed a massive football-field-sized oval concrete fender wall 115 feet deep in the ocean floor, pumped out 9.4 million gallons of seawater, and poured the tower pier inside it.

High above the water, Strauss enforced the strictest industrial safety rules in bridge-building history: mandatory hardhats, anti-glare fog goggles, special diets to prevent dizziness, and—most famously—a $130,000 hemp safety net suspended beneath the entire length of the roadway deck. Nineteen ironworkers who slipped and fell into the net survived, proudly forming the 'Halfway to Hell Club.' For four years the bridge suffered only one fatality—until the tragic morning of February 17, 1937, when a five-ton rolling stripping scaffold broke through its aluminum hangers, tore straight through the safety net, and plunged twelve men into the icy outgoing tide, ten of whom perished.

What about the bridge’s world-famous color? The U.S. Navy demanded the bridge be painted in black-and-yellow stripes like a giant bumblebee so warships wouldn't collide with it in the fog; the Army Air Corps wanted red-and-white stripes; and steel-company engineers assumed it would be painted standard charcoal gray or aluminum silver like the Bay Bridge. When the Bethlehem Steel segments arrived from Pennsylvania coated in a reddish-orange rust-inhibiting red-lead primer, consulting architect Irving F. Morrow—who also designed the Art Deco stepped tower setbacks, fluted light standards, and pedestrian walkways—walked along the Marin Headlands in the fog and had a revelation.

Morrow wrote a passionate twenty-nine-page report to the Bridge District proving that the warm terracotta-vermilion primer hue (*International Orange*: 69% magenta, 100% yellow, 6% cyan, 6% black) complemented both the emerald spring hills and golden summer grasses of the Marin Headlands, contrasted brilliantly with the blue-green Pacific and white fog, and was more visible to ships than any gray or black paint. When 200,000 pedestrians walked across the newly opened Golden Gate Bridge on Pedestrian Day, May 27, 1937, they walked into an Art Deco symphony in International Orange—a 1.7-mile steel harp strung with 80,000 miles of galvanized steel wire.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Suspension Physics & Why Charles Ellis Was Vindicated

Each of the Golden Gate Bridge's two main cables is 36.5 inches thick, spun in midair over six months by Roebling & Sons from 27,572 individual pencil-thick galvanized steel wires—enough wire to circle the Earth at the equator more than three times—anchored at each end into solid concrete blocks weighing 60,000 tons apiece. Charles Ellis's mathematical genius in designing the stepped cellular steel towers (which contain over 600,000 shop-riveted cells without external diagonal cross-bracing) allowed the towers to flex up to 16 inches toward the channel or shore as temperatures and traffic loads change while transferring 123 million pounds of vertical load per tower cleanly into the bedrock.

2. Irving Morrow's Art Deco Masterwork & The Singing Slats of 2020

Too often people credit only engineers for bridges, but consulting architect Irving Morrow (working alongside his wife, architect Gertrude Comfort Morrow) transformed a utilitarian highway structure into sculpture. Look closely at the towers as they rise 746 feet: Morrow stepped back the steel plates as the load diminishes near the top, opening up the upper portals so sky shines through, and bevelled the vertical ribs to catch morning and evening light. In 2020, when engineers replaced the west sidewalk railing slats with thinner aerodynamic profiles to withstand 100 mph winds, the Golden Gate Bridge literally began singing an eerie, multi-tone aeolian chord audible across the city whenever stiff western gales blow!

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three Golden Gate concepts in mind: first, International Orange, the signature red-lead primer hue championed by Irving Morrow and touched up continuously by ironworkers and painters against salt-fog corrosion; second, the 'Halfway to Hell Club,' the nineteen ironworkers saved from death by Joseph Strauss's innovative under-deck hemp safety net; and third, Charles Alton Ellis (1876–1949), whose statue and plaque at the South Vista Point now restore the erased mathematician to his rightful place in history.
