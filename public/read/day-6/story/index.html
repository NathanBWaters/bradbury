Bradbury 1000 • Night 6 • Nightly Short Story

The Star

H. G. Wells (1897) • 19 min read

“Which only shows how small the vastest of human catastrophes may seem, at a distance of a few million miles.”

It was on the first day of the new year that the announcement was made, almost simultaneously from three observatories, that the motion of the planet Neptune, the outermost of all the planets that wheel about the sun, had become very erratic. Ogilvy had already called attention to a suspected retardation in its velocity in December. Such a piece of news was scarcely calculated to interest a world the greater portion of whose inhabitants were unaware of the existence of the planet Neptune, nor outside the astronomical profession did the subsequent discovery of a faint remote speck of light in the region of the perturbed planet cause any very great excitement. Scientific people, however, found the intelligence remarkable enough, even before it became known that the new body was rapidly growing larger and brighter, and that its motion was quite different from the orderly progress of the planets, and that the deflection of Neptune and its satellite was becoming now of an unprecedented kind.

Few people without a training in science can realise the huge isolation of the solar system. The sun with its specks of planets, its dust of planetoids, and its impalpable comets, swims in a vacant immensity that almost defeats the imagination. Beyond the orbit of Neptune there is space, vacant so far as human observation has penetrated, without warmth or light or sound, blank emptiness, for twenty million times a million miles. That is the smallest estimate of the distance to be traversed before the very nearest of the stars is attained. And, saving a few comets more unsubstantial than the thistledown, no matter had ever to human knowledge crossed this gulf of space, until early in the twentieth century this strange wanderer appeared. A vast mass of matter it was, bulky, heavy, rushing without warning out of the black mystery of the sky into the radiance of the sun. By the second day it was clearly visible to any decent instrument, as a speck with a barely sensible diameter, in the constellation Leo near Regulus. In a little while an opera glass could attain it.

On the third day of the new year the newspaper readers of two hemispheres were made aware for the first time of the real importance of this unusual apparition in the heavens. “A Planetary Collision,” one London paper headed the news, and proclaimed Duchaine’s opinion that this strange new planet would probably collide with Neptune. The leader writers enlarged upon the topic. So that in most of the capitals of the world, on January 3rd, there was an expectation, however vague, of some imminent phenomenon in the sky; and as the night followed the sunset round the globe, thousands of men turned their eyes skyward to see—the old familiar stars just as they had always been.

Until it was dawn in London and Pollux setting and the stars overhead grown pale. The Winter’s dawn it was, a sickly filtering accumulation of daylight, and the light of gas and candles shone yellow in the windows to show where people were astir. But the yawning policeman saw the thing, the busy crowds in the markets stopped agape, workmen going to their work betimes, milkmen, the drivers of news-carts, dissipation going home jaded and pale, homeless wanderers, sentinels on their beats, and in the country, labourers trudging afield, poachers slinking home, all over the dusky quickening country it could be seen—and out at sea by seamen watching for the day—a great white star, come suddenly into the westward sky! Brighter it was than any star in our skies; brighter than the evening star at its brightest. It still glowed out white and large, no mere twinkling spot of light, but a small round clear shining disc, an hour after the day had come. And where science has not reached, men stared and feared, telling one another of the wars and pestilences that are foreshadowed by these fiery signs in the Heavens.

In a hundred observatories there had been suppressed excitement, rising almost to shouting pitch, as the two remote bodies had rushed together, and a hurrying to and fro, to gather photographic apparatus and spectroscope, and this appliance and that, to record this novel astonishing sight, the destruction of a world. For it was a world, a sister planet of our earth, far greater than our earth indeed, that had so suddenly flashed into white death. Neptune it was, had been struck, fairly and squarely, by the strange planet from outer space and the heat of the concussion had incontinently turned two solid globes into one glowing mass of incandescence. Round the world that day, two hours before the dawn, went the pallid great white star, fading only as it sank westward and the sun mounted above it. Everywhere men marvelled at it, but of all those who saw it none could have marvelled more than those sailors, habitual watchers of the stars, who far away at sea had heard nothing of its advent and saw it now rise like a pigmy moon and climb zenithward and hang overhead and sink westward with the passing of the night.

And when next it rose over Europe everywhere were crowds of watchers on hilly slopes, on house-roofs, in open spaces, staring eastward for the rising of the great new star. It rose with a white glow in front of it, like the glare of a white fire, and those who had seen it come into existence the night before cried out at the sight of it. “It is larger,” they cried. “It is brighter!” And, indeed, the moon a quarter full and sinking in the west was in its apparent size beyond comparison, but scarcely in all its breadth had it as much brightness now as the little circle of the strange new star.

“It is brighter!” cried the people clustering in the streets. But in the dim observatories the watchers held their breath and peered at one another. “It is nearer,” they said. “Nearer!” And voice after voice repeated, “Nearer!” and the clicking telegraph took that up, and it trembled along telephone wires, and in a thousand cities grimy compositors fingered the type. “It is nearer.” Men writing in offices, struck with a strange realization, flung down their pens; men talking in a thousand places suddenly came upon a grotesque possibility in those words, “It is nearer.” It hurried along awakening streets, it was shouted down the frost-stiffened intervals of quiet villages; men who had read these things from the throbbing tape stood in yellow-lit doorways shouting the news to the passers-by. “It is nearer.” Pretty women, flushed and glittering, heard the news told jestingly between the dances, and feigned an intelligent interest they did not feel. “Nearer! Indeed. How curious! How very, very clever people must be to find out things like that!”

That day the master mathematician sat in his private room and pushed the papers from him. His calculations were already finished. In a small white phial there still remained a little of the drug that had kept him awake and active for four long nights. Each day, serene, explicit, patient as ever, he had given his lecture to his students, and then had come back with a quickening step to this momentous calculation. His face was grave, a little drawn and hectic from his drugged activity. For some time he seemed lost in thought. Then he went to the window, and the blind went up with a click. Half way up the sky, over the clustering roofs, chimneys and steeples of the city, hung the star. He looked at it as one might look into the eyes of a brave enemy. “You may kill me,” he said after a silence. “But I can hold you—and all the universe for that matter—in the grip of this little brain. I would not change. Even now.” He looked at the little phial. “There will be no need of sleep again,” he said. Then he turned into his laboratory and took up his note-book, and went carefully through his calculation once more.

All down the long street the shutters were being put up, the gas-lamps were being lighted, and people were hurrying hither and thither in the gathering twilight. And high above the haze of the town, the star grew brighter as the sky darkened. For five nights now it had been rising and growing bigger. Now it had reached a third the size of the moon, and its light was blue-white and intensely bright, so that it cast sharp black shadows upon the ground even while the sunset lingered in the west. From his lecture-room the master mathematician dispatched warnings to every capital of the globe: the fused mass of Neptune and the dark wanderer was falling directly toward the Sun, and its path would pass within grazing distance of the Earth, unleashing gravitational tides, atmospheric superheating, and continental deluges beyond anything in human annals.

And then the fifth night came. The frost was gone. In the middle of the night the snow began to melt and the rivers to rise, and before dawn a hot wind came roaring out of the south. In the cities, bells rang incessantly, trains were choked with refugees fleeing toward the high mountains, and along every coast the ocean began to heave in monstrous, leaden swells. The star rose no longer as a point or a small disc, but as a blinding, livid furnace in the sky, accompanied by a vast tail of incandescent vapour that stretched across forty degrees of the firmament. Sleep had vanished from the Earth; millions stood on rooftops and hills watching the sky turn the colour of molten silver.

At the peril of its life humanity fled upward. The Thames and the Seine and the Rhine and the Yang-tse and the Mississippi came roaring out of their banks as the great ocean tides, pulled by the double mass of the passing monster, surged fifty feet above the highest equinoctial marks. Low-lying cities vanished beneath foaming walls of water in the glare of a night brighter than noon. Volcanoes long extinct along the Andes, the Mediterranean, and the Pacific ring of fire split open with thunderous concussions, belching pillars of black ash that met the blinding white light of the sky.

And then the Star and the Earth reached their point of closest approach. To the survivors clinging to the crags of the Alps, the Rockies, and the Himalayas, the Star filled a tenth of the heavens—a spinning maelstrom of white-hot gasses, trailed by the Moon, which had been wrenched outward from its ancient orbit. The air grew hot as a kiln; the mountain snows flashed directly into steam and roaring mudslides; sheets of lightning played continuously from horizon to horizon without a pause in the deafening thunder. Men and women lay face downward in the boiling mud, blinded and gasping, waiting for the crust of the Earth itself to tear apart.

Yet the Earth did not strike the Star. Slowly, by an imperceptible hair's-breadth of celestial mechanics, the blazing mass swept past the Earth's orbit and plunged onward toward its final fiery engulfment in the Sun. After five days of suffocating steam, impenetrable ash-clouds, and subsiding earthquakes, the veil of vapour parted. A dimmer, larger sun rose over a transformed planet whose polar ice caps had melted, whose coastlines were redrawn, and whose surviving remnant of humanity crept down from the mountain ridges to begin the work of brotherhood and reconstruction.

The Martian astronomers—for there are astronomers on Mars, although they are very different beings from men—were naturally profoundly interested by these things. They saw them from their own standpoint of course. “Considering the mass and temperature of the missile that was flung through our solar system into the sun,” one wrote, “it is astonishing what a little damage the earth, which it missed so narrowly, has sustained. All the familiar continental markings and the masses of the seas remain intact, and indeed the only difference seems to be a shrinkage of the white discoloration (supposed to be frozen water) round either pole.” Which only shows how small the vastest of human catastrophes may seem, at a distance of a few million miles.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

Published in the Christmas 1897 issue of The Graphic, 'The Star' was written at the peak of H. G. Wells's unmatched creative streak between The Time Machine (1895) and The War of the Worlds (1898). Trained in biology under Thomas Henry Huxley—Darwin's fiercest champion—Wells rejected the comforting Victorian belief that the universe was designed around human progress. By placing a planetary cataclysm in a Christmas periodical alongside festive illustrations, Wells subverted the Star of Bethlehem into a blind, indifferent astrophysical projectile hurtling out of the dark gulf beyond Neptune. Written at the dawn of modern astrophysics and mass daily journalism, the story captures the exact historical moment when humanity first realized that Earth is not a sheltered stage at the center of creation, but a fragile pebble orbiting an indifferent sun.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

The central theme of 'The Star' is cosmic scale versus human parochialism. For days, newspaper readers, dancers at balls, and busy city-dwellers treat the brightening point of light as a mild curiosity—'How very, very clever people must be to find out things like that!'—unable to grasp that nature obeys gravitation rather than human schedules. Against this collective blindness stands the Master Mathematician, who stares at the approaching destroyer and declares, 'You may kill me... But I can hold you—and all the universe for that matter—in the grip of this little brain,' embodying both the fragility of human flesh and the nobility of scientific consciousness.

3. Narrative Craft, Pacing & Symbolism

Wells pioneers the 'mosaic catastrophe' technique here: instead of following a single protagonist with a name and backstory, the camera pans across the entire planet—from London policemen, milkmen, and poachers to sailors at sea, compositors setting type, and refugees scrambling up mountain ranges. The pacing mirrors gravitational acceleration itself: slow, technical astronomical observations in the opening paragraphs speed up day by day as the refrain 'It is nearer!' ripples through telegraph wires, culminating in elemental fury before zooming abruptly outward to the cold telescopes of Mars. By cutting rapidly between continents, social classes, and ocean latitudes as the incandescent disc swells in the heavens, Wells creates a planetary panorama in which individual biography dissolves into the collective struggle of the human species to survive.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

'The Star' literally invented the modern asteroid/comet-impact genre. Every subsequent work of impact fiction—from Arthur C. Clarke's Rendezvous with Rama and Larry Niven's Lucifer's Hammer to films like Deep Impact, Melancholia, and Don't Look Up—follows the blueprint Wells laid down in 1897: the initial orbital perturbation, the public denial and media trivialization, the solitary scientist's calculations, and the humbling of human vanity. Its closing zoom-out to the Martian astronomers remains one of the most celebrated final paragraphs in science fiction. Generations of speculative writers, including Arthur C. Clarke, Isaac Asimov, and Liu Cixin, have drawn directly on Wells's blend of rigorous astronomical plausibility and philosophical detachment to explore humanity's precarious place in deep time.

5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

Welcome to your first Weekend Boss Night! Ray Bradbury grew up intoxicated by H. G. Wells, and in stories like 'Kaleidoscope' and 'The Last Night of the World' Bradbury explored the exact question Wells poses here: how do ordinary human beings behave when the sky itself announces an ending? After five nights of intimate domestic rooms—a garden lodge, an upstairs armchair, a bedroom floorboard, a Parisian garret, an $8 flat—Wells blows the roof off the planet. Tonight, hold the Master Mathematician's defiance alongside the Martian astronomer's postscript. Pairing Wells's cosmic perspective with our intimate human stories reminds us that both scales of vision are simultaneously true: from Mars our greatest catastrophes barely shrink the polar ice, yet inside a single human skull the entire universe is comprehended.
