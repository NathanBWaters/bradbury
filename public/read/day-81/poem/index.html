Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

Danse Russe

William Carlos Williams (1917) • 3 min read

While his wife, baby, and household sleep at dawn, the suburban doctor dances naked before his mirror in the north room, declaring himself the happy genius of his household.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Danse Russe is a nineteen-line poem by William Carlos Williams, and it is one of the funniest and most surprising short poems in American writing. Williams was a working doctor in Rutherford, New Jersey, who wrote poems between patients. He published this one in 1916 and collected it in his 1917 book Al Que Quiere. The title nods to the Ballets Russes, the Russian dance company whose star, Vaslav Nijinsky, had thrilled and scandalized audiences, including American ones, around that time. But Williams puts the dancing in a very different place: his own bedroom, early in the morning, while the household sleeps. At the time, poetry was often grand, hushed and full of suffering artists. Williams wanted something plain, American and a little ridiculous. The poem is about private joy, the kind you only dare feel when nobody is watching, and about the strange way loneliness and happiness can sit in the same body. Read it as a joke that turns out to be sincere.

If I when my wife is sleeping

and the baby and Kathleen

are sleeping

and the sun is a flame-white disc

in silken mists

above shining trees,—

if I in my north room

danse naked, grotesquely

before my mirror

waving my shirt round my head

and singing softly to myself:

“I am lonely, lonely.

I was born to be lonely.

I am best so!”

If I admire my arms, my face

my shoulders, flanks, buttocks

against the yellow drawn shades,—

who shall say I am not

the happy genius of my household?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is one long sentence built on the word "if." If a man, while his wife and baby and a woman named Kathleen are asleep, dances naked in front of his mirror, waving his shirt over his head and singing to himself, then what? The answer arrives in the last two lines as a challenge: "who shall say I am not the happy genius of my household?" So the whole poem is a kind of defense argument, delivered by a man who has just described himself doing something he would be mortified to be caught doing.

Start with the scene. The sun is "a flame-white disc in silken mists above shining trees." It is dawn, or very early morning, and the world outside is lovely and still. Inside, in the "north room," the speaker has found a pocket of time that belongs to nobody but himself. A married father with a baby and a household to support almost never gets this. The dance is not for anyone. It is not a performance, and that is exactly why it is free.

Then comes the song he sings under his breath: "I am lonely, lonely. I was born to be lonely. I am best so!" This is the heart of the poem and the part a first-time reader may find confusing. Is he sad or joyful? The answer is both, and the poem sees no contradiction. Loneliness here is not misery. It is the condition of being a separate self with a private inner life, and he is celebrating it. The cry "I am best so!" is an exclamation of delight, almost swagger. He is saying that the part of him that is alone, that nobody in the house can reach, is the part that is most alive.

Notice the details of the body. He admires "my arms, my face, my shoulders, flanks, buttocks" against yellow drawn shades. The word "grotesquely" matters too. He knows he looks absurd. Williams is quietly poking fun at the solemn poet who stands apart from ordinary life. Here the poet is not on a mountaintop or in a cathedral. He is a naked man in a bedroom with a shirt over his head.

A layer people often miss is the gentle comedy of the ending. "The happy genius of my household" is a joke about a man with a respectable, dutiful public role, the family doctor and husband. The word "genius" can mean a brilliant person, but it also carries the old sense of a guardian spirit of a place, as in the Roman idea of a household's protecting spirit. So he is both claiming brilliance and claiming to be the quiet spirit who keeps the house in order. The secret dance, the poem hints, may be what keeps him going.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of craft, Danse Russe is a small triumph of tone. The hardest thing to do in a poem is to be funny and moving at once without letting either spoil the other, and Williams does it in under twenty lines. The plain diction helps. Nothing here is ornamental. "Wife," "baby," "mirror," "shirt," "shades." The one lofty word, "genius," arrives at the end and lands as a joke and a sincere claim together.

The structure is also clever. The poem keeps you waiting. "If I when my wife is sleeping" begins in suspense, then piles up clause after clause, scene-setting first, then the dance, then the song, then the vanity, and only at the very end gives you the question the whole thing has been building to. That long delayed finish gives the last two lines real force. The short line breaks slow you down and make you look at each small piece of the picture, the sun, the mists, the trees, almost as though you were moving through the room.

The tonal shift in the middle is the boldest move. After the physical comedy, the song about loneliness drops in with surprising directness. It could have wrecked the poem by turning melodramatic. Instead the repetition, "lonely, lonely," and the cheerful "I am best so!" keep it balanced on a knife edge. A reader can hear it as pathos or as bravado, and the ambiguity is the point.

Now the weaknesses. The poem is slight, and some readers will feel that it is more a clever gesture than a deep one. Its idea, that a private self lives under the dutiful surface, is not new, and the poem does not push it very far. The "if" structure is a neat trick, but it means the speaker never commits to the claim directly. He asks a rhetorical question and leaves the room, which can feel like a wink where a fuller statement might have been braver. And the line "I was born to be lonely" sits in tension with the warm household described around it. The poem does not look closely at what this loneliness costs the people sleeping nearby, or him. A more demanding poem would have let that tension hurt a little.

There is also a dated quality in the tidy domestic setup. The wife is asleep, the baby is asleep, and the man's freedom depends on everyone else being unconscious. He is free only when others are not looking, which is both the poem's truth and its limit. Still, these are small complaints. It is the rare poem that is memorable because it is so unguarded, and it has remained in anthologies for more than a century for good reason.

III. The Impact It Had

The immediate impact of Danse Russe was modest. Al Que Quiere was a small book from a young poet who was still better known among other poets than among the public. But the poem has had a long afterlife, and it helped define what Williams stood for: poetry rooted in an American speaker, in ordinary rooms and occupations, and in everyday speech rather than inherited poetic language.

For Williams himself, this was a turning point. In his earlier work he had been imitating Keats and the English tradition. By 1917 he was finding a voice that sounded like a man talking, with a doctor's eye for the body and a neighbor's eye for the street. The poem anticipates the famous approach that he later summed up as "no ideas but in things," meaning that feeling should be carried by concrete objects rather than abstract talk. Here the feeling, loneliness and pleasure, is carried by a mirror, a shirt, and drawn yellow shades.

Beyond Williams, the poem became a quiet landmark for later American poets who wanted to write about private life without solemnity. Poets in the mid-century, including the Beats and the confessional writers, admired his directness. Allen Ginsberg and others looked to Williams as a model of an American idiom, and the poem's naked dancing and plain confession seem like distant ancestors of more openly personal poetry. It is fair to say the poem is part of the long lineage that makes it possible to write a poem about a man looking at his own body in a mirror and have it count as serious art.

The poem has also become a favorite for teachers because it is short, memorable and approachable. It is often used to show students that a modernist poem need not be difficult or obscure. A beginner can understand what is happening and still be left with questions about whether the speaker is happy or sad. It is a classroom example of how an ambiguous tone can make a very short poem rich.

In popular culture the poem has not had the reach of Williams's The Red Wheelbarrow or This Is Just To Say. It is not widely adapted. But among poets and readers it has a quiet reputation as one of his most charming and most human pieces, and its title has been borrowed or echoed in other writing about dance and solitude. Its biggest impact is probably indirect: it modeled a way of being honest and comic about the inner life that later poets took for granted.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved about Danse Russe is first of all its nerve. It tells a secret that most people would never admit, that they sometimes behave ridiculously when alone, and it does so cheerfully. There is a warm shock of recognition in it. Almost everyone has danced in a room when nobody was home, sung loudly in the car, or felt a spell of happiness that only existed because no one could see it. The poem gives permission for that.

Readers also love its humor. The self-satisfied way the speaker admires his own body, and then asks who could possibly deny that he is a genius, is genuinely funny. It is gentle comedy, aimed at himself rather than at others. The man is not a monster of vanity. He is a tired family man enjoying the one hour in which he is nobody's husband, father or doctor. Many readers feel affection for him straight away.

Then there is the surprise of the middle. People often say that the song, "I am lonely, lonely," catches them off guard. It adds depth and a kind of truth. Readers who first took the poem as a joke find themselves thinking about the loneliness of ordinary life and how a person can be surrounded by family and yet carry an inner space nobody else sees. The poem's refusal to say whether this is a happy or a sad thing feels honest.

Fellow poets have admired it for its economy. Williams made a whole scene, a whole mood and a whole character in a few lines, without any ornament. For writers trying to learn how to say a lot simply, the poem is a lesson. It shows how to use one sentence, one image at a time, and one sharp turn.

Finally, readers like that it feels American. It has no European melancholy and no heroic posturing. Its setting is a house in New Jersey, its hero is a doctor, and its mood is cheerful self-mockery. There is a kind of democratic joy in the idea that genius might live in an ordinary household, in somebody who dances in his bedroom, and that nobody has the right to say otherwise. That generous spirit is what many readers carry away.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Danse Russe has not attracted fierce controversy, partly because it is so short and so good-humored. But there are criticisms, some made in general terms by readers and critics of Williams's work, and some that arise naturally from reading the poem closely.

One is that it is lightweight. Next to the grand achievements of modernism of the same decade, such as Eliot's Prufrock or Pound's early Cantos, a nineteen-line joke about dancing in a bedroom can seem minor. Some critics have preferred Williams's later and larger work, such as Paterson, to his early short lyrics, and treat poems like this as charming warm-ups. Williams was never short of defenders, but his critics sometimes argued that his plainness could slip into thinness.

Another objection concerns the speaker's point of view. The poem is the confession of a man in a secure position, with a household that supports his private joy. Some readers, especially more recent ones, note that the wife and the woman named Kathleen are simply asleep in the background, and that the man's freedom depends on their silence and on the unseen domestic work that surrounds him. The poem does not ask who gets time to be alone and who does not. That is not a flaw Williams could have been expected to address in 1916, but a modern reader may feel the absence.

There is also the old problem of sincerity versus performance. Is the loneliness real, or is it a pose? If the speaker is truly lonely, the poem's cheerfulness may seem like denial. If he is not, the song is a mere joke and the poem loses its depth. Some readers feel that Williams has it both ways and never settles the question. Others see that as the poem's strength.

The nakedness itself, along with the admiring look at the body, drew some uneasy reactions from readers in conservative times. Williams's early work sometimes struck readers as frank or odd, and a poem about a naked man admiring his own flanks and buttocks was unusual for the period. Specific hostile reviews of this particular poem are not well documented, so it is safest to say that its frankness was part of what made it feel new rather than to claim any scandal.

Lastly, some critics have argued that the poem is more about a stance than about craft, that its charm comes from its attitude rather than from sound or form. This is a fair point. The poem is not especially musical, and it relies on situation more than on language. Whether that counts against it depends on your taste.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Danse Russe belongs to American modernism, the early twentieth-century push to make poetry new. It is closely related to Imagism, the movement associated with Ezra Pound, who was Williams's friend from their student days. Imagism asked for direct treatment of things, no wasted words, and rhythm that follows speech instead of a drum beat. This poem follows that advice but adds something the early Imagists often lacked, a living human speaker with a wife, a baby and a mirror.

Its ancestors are interesting. Walt Whitman stands behind it, with his open celebration of the body and the self. Whitman wrote, in effect, that he was large and contained multitudes, and Williams brings that expansive spirit down to the size of a single bedroom. There is also a trace of the English Romantics, who prized solitude, though Williams turns their grand lonely walks into a dance in front of a mirror. And the title points to the world of the Ballets Russes, the newest, most daring art form of its day, which had made dance a modern art and shocked the public with its energy and sensuality.

Its descendants are easy to spot. Poets like Frank O'Hara, with his chatty poems of daily life, and many American poets who write about family and body in plain speech, owe a debt to Williams's example. The tradition of the funny, confessional domestic poem, in which a speaker reveals an embarrassing private moment and thereby tells a larger truth, runs through much American verse.

Put it beside Eliot's The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock, published only a year or two earlier. Prufrock is also about a private self who cannot connect, but it is anxious, paralyzed and haunted by embarrassment. Williams's speaker is embarrassed too, but he turns embarrassment into liberation. Where Prufrock cannot dare to disturb the universe, this man dares to dance naked in his north room. It makes a good pair, comic where Eliot is tragic and hopeful where Eliot is brittle.

Why does it still matter? Because the experience it describes has not gone away. We still live in crowded, busy lives and still need small private spaces where we can be foolish and unobserved. The poem is a reminder that such moments are not trivial, that they may be where a person is most himself. It is short enough to read in half a minute and strange enough to think about for a long while, which is a good reason to keep it close.
