The Secret Laboratory of King Sejong & the Invention of Hangul (1443)

Korea — How a Joseon monarch defied his own aristocratic court to engineer the world's most scientific alphabet for the common people (Joseon Dynasty (1443–1446)) • 10 min read

“A wise man can acquaint himself with them before the morning is over; a stupid man can learn them in the space of ten days.” — Jeong In-ji, Postscript to the Hunminjeongeum Haerye (1446)

In the winter of 1443, behind the vermilion pillars and curved slate eaves of Gyeongbokgung Palace in Hanyang (modern-day Seoul), forty-six-year-old King Sejong the Great was going blind. Suffering from diabetes and severe eye inflammation thickened by decades of reading by candlelight, the fourth monarch of the Joseon Dynasty was conducting a clandestine project inside the palace’s inner quarters. Not even the highest ministers of the State Council were permitted to see the diagrams spread across his low lacquered desk—diagrams showing cross-sections of the human throat, tongue, lips, and teeth.

For over a millennium, Koreans had spoken their own rich, agglutinative language while writing exclusively in Classical Chinese characters (*Hanja*). Mastering thousands of ideographs required decades of expensive private tutoring, effectively locking farmers, artisans, women, and merchants out of literacy and the law. A peasant accused of a crime could not read the transcript of his own confession; a farmer could not read royal manuals on crop rotation or drought survival. To the Neo-Confucian aristocratic elite—the *yangban*—this linguistic wall was not a flaw but the very foundation of social hierarchy.

Working with a select circle of young scholars from the *Jiphyeonjeon* (Hall of Worthies)—including Shin Suk-ju, Seong Sam-mun, and Choe Hang—Sejong asked a question no ruler in human history had ever posed: instead of adapting an existing script through centuries of accidental evolution, could a writing system be engineered from first principles to mirror both human vocal anatomy and cosmic philosophy? Rather than representing whole words or syllables, Sejong isolated the fundamental phonemes of Korean speech and designed a *featural alphabet* in which the shape of each letter visually depicts how the mouth produces the sound.

Look closely at the five basic consonants of the *Hunminjeongeum* ('The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People'). The velar consonant ㄱ (*giyeok*, a 'k/g' sound) is a side-view diagram of the back of the tongue rising to touch the soft palate. The coronal consonant ㄴ (*nieun*, an 'n' sound) shows the tip of the tongue curving up to press behind the upper front teeth. The bilabial ㅁ (*mieum*, an 'm' sound) depicts the square outline of closed lips; the sibilant ㅅ (*siot*, an 's' sound) sketches the sharp edge of a tooth; and the glottal ㅇ (*ieung*) traces the round, open throat. To indicate stronger aspiration—a burst of breath—Sejong simply added a single horizontal stroke: ㄱ (*g/k*) becomes ㅋ (*kh*); ㄴ (*n*) gains a stroke to become ㄷ (*d/t*) and another to become ㅌ (*th*).

For the vowels, Sejong turned from physiological anatomy to Neo-Confucian cosmology, reducing the entire vowel space of human speech to three elemental strokes: a round dot (ㆍ, later simplified to a short dash) representing the round Heaven (*Cheon*); a flat horizontal line (ㅡ) representing the flat Earth (*Ji*); and a standing vertical line (ㅣ) representing the upright Human Being (*In*) standing between sky and soil. By combining these three strokes according to yin-yang vowel harmony—bright yang vowels pointing upward or right (ㅏ, ㅗ) and dark yin vowels pointing downward or left (ㅓ, ㅜ)—any vowel sound could be constructed with geometric clarity.

Most ingeniously of all, Sejong refused to string these letters out in a single linear row like Latin or Greek. Instead, he grouped initial consonants, medial vowels, and final consonants (*batchim*) into compact, balanced syllabic blocks—so that *h-a-n* becomes 한 and *g-u-l* becomes 글 (한글). Each syllable occupies the exact square footprint of a traditional Chinese character, allowing the new script to be printed alongside or instead of *Hanja* without disrupting vertical or horizontal page typography.

When Sejong finally unveiled the twenty-eight letters of the *Hunminjeongeum* in the twelfth lunar month of 1443, a firestorm erupted inside the palace. In February 1444, Choe Man-ri, the Deputy Director of the Hall of Worthies, submitted a furious seven-point memorial signed by conservative mandarins. Abandoning Chinese characters for a phonetic vernacular, Choe warned, would sever Joseon from the civilized world, reduce Koreans to the level of 'barbarians' like the Mongols and Jurchens, and allow uneducated clerks and commoners to interpret the law without scholarly mediation.

Usually a patient, consensus-building ruler, Sejong exploded in rare royal fury. Looking down at Choe Man-ri and his fellow scholars, the half-blind king thundered: 'Do you even understand what rhymes and phonemes are? You believe that using twenty-eight letters to help the common people is a trivial, vulgar matter—how can you call yourselves Confucian scholars of the Hall of Worthies while despising the people?' He briefly imprisoned the ringleaders of the protest for insubordination and pressed ahead with testing the script by commissioning *Yongbieocheonga* ('Songs of the Dragons Flying to Heaven'), a 125-canto dynastic epic that proved the new alphabet could capture both courtly poetry and native Korean idioms.

In autumn 1446, Sejong promulgated the *Hunminjeongeum Haerye*—an explanatory manual written with Jeong In-ji that opened with sixty-four characters now etched on the pedestal of King Sejong’s statue in Gwanghwamun Square: 'Because the speech of this country is different from that of China, it does not match the Chinese letters. Therefore, even if the ignorant people wish to communicate, many of them in the end cannot state their concerns. Saddened by this, I have newly made twenty-eight letters.' Though aristocrats derisively nicknamed it *Eonmun* ('vulgar script') or *Amkeul* ('women's script') for centuries, women, novelists, and commoners kept it alive until it blossomed into *Hangul*—celebrated by modern linguists from Geoffrey Sampson to Jared Diamond as the most logical writing system ever devised.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Linguistic Engineering: What Makes Hangul a 'Featural Alphabet'

In 1985, British linguist Geoffrey Sampson coined the term 'featural writing system' specifically to classify Hangul, because no other script in world history operates like it. In the Latin or Cyrillic alphabets, the letters 'p', 'b', and 'm' share no visual similarity even though all three are produced with both lips (bilabial). In Hangul, every sound produced at the same point of articulation shares a common base glyph: ㅁ (m), ㅂ (b/p), and ㅍ (ph) all build visually upon the square shape of the lips. Consequently, a reader is not merely memorizing arbitrary symbols, but reading a real-time phonetic diagram of vocal tract mechanics.

2. The Lost 1940 Andong Discovery: Hunminjeongeum Haerye

For nearly five centuries, historians knew King Sejong had invented Hangul, but the exact anatomical and philosophical rationale behind the letter shapes had been lost during the purge of Hangul books by the tyrannical King Yeonsangun in 1504. Then, in 1940, an original 1446 woodblock edition of the Hunminjeongeum Haerye ('Explanations and Examples of the Correct Sounds') was discovered in the possession of an old clan family in Andong. Smuggled past Japanese colonial censors by Korean linguist Jeon Hyeong-pil, who paid the price of a large Seoul mansion to protect it, the manuscript is now South Korea's National Treasure No. 70 and a UNESCO Memory of the World register item.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Three essential terms anchor this story: first, Hunminjeongeum (훈민정음), the original 1443 name meaning 'The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People,' celebrated every October 9 on Hangul Day; second, Jiphyeonjeon (집현전, Hall of Worthies), the royal research institute inside Gyeongbokgung Palace where Sejong also sponsored rain gauges (Cheugugi), celestial globes, and movable metal type; and third, Cheon-Ji-In (천지인, Heaven-Earth-Human), the three-stroke vowel system that allowed 1990s Korean engineers to design the fastest mobile-phone keypad in the world using just three vowel buttons. 4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Begin at Gwanghwamun Square in central Seoul, where a secret subterranean passageway beneath the giant bronze statue of King Sejong leads into 'The Story of King Sejong' exhibition hall. From there, walk north through the main gate of Gyeongbokgung Palace to Sujeongjeon Hall, which stands on the exact footprint of the fifteenth-century Jiphyeonjeon where the alphabet was tested. For the original 1446 Haerye manuscript itself, visit the Kansong Art Museum or the National Hangeul Museum in Yongsan-gu, where interactive vocal-tract projections show your own tongue forming ㄱ, ㄴ, and ㅁ.
