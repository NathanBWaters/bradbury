The Bet

Anton Chekhov (1889) • 13 min read

“To live anyhow is better than not at all.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Anton Chekhov wrote The Bet in 1889, when he was in his late twenties and already becoming Russia's great master of the short story. It is one of his more openly philosophical tales, and it grew out of a debate people were having everywhere in his day, about whether it is better to be put to death or to be locked up for life. Chekhov was also wrestling with the ideas of Leo Tolstoy, the towering moral teacher of the age, who urged people to renounce wealth and worldly comforts. Chekhov admired Tolstoy but never fully believed him. The story begins at a dinner party, where a rich banker and a young lawyer make a wager that sounds like a parlor game. Fifteen years of solitary confinement, for a fortune. As you listen, pay attention to what each man believes at the start, and who each man has become by the end. The tale asks what money, books, and freedom are actually worth, and answers in a way that surprises both of its characters.

It was a dark autumn night. The old banker was walking up and down his study and remembering how, fifteen years before, he had given a party one autumn evening. There had been many clever men there, and there had been interesting conversations. Among other things they had talked of capital punishment. The majority of the guests, among whom were many journalists and intellectual men, disapproved of the death penalty. They considered that form of punishment out of date, immoral, and unsuitable for Christian States. In the opinion of some of them the death penalty ought to be replaced everywhere by imprisonment for life.

“I don’t agree with you,” said their host the banker. “I have not tried either the death penalty or imprisonment for life, but if one may judge a priori, the death penalty is more moral and more humane than imprisonment for life. Capital punishment kills a man at once, but lifelong imprisonment kills him slowly. Which executioner is the more humane, he who kills you in a few minutes or he who drags the life out of you in the course of many years?”

“Both are equally immoral,” observed one of the guests, “for they both have the same object—to take away life. The State is not God. It has not the right to take away what it cannot restore when it wants to.”

Among the guests was a young lawyer, a young man of five-and-twenty. When he was asked his opinion, he said: “The death sentence and the life sentence are equally immoral, but if I had to choose between the death penalty and imprisonment for life, I would certainly choose the second. To live anyhow is better than not at all.”

A lively discussion arose. The banker, who was younger and more nervous in those days, was suddenly carried away by excitement; he struck the table with his fist and shouted at the young man: “It’s not true! I’ll bet you two million you wouldn’t stick in solitary confinement for five years.”

“If you mean that in earnest,” said the young man, “I’ll take the bet, but I would stay not five but fifteen years.” “Fifteen? Done!” cried the banker. “Gentlemen, I stake two million!” “Agreed! You stake your millions and I stake my freedom!” said the young man. And this wild, senseless bet was carried out! The banker, spoilt and frivolous, with millions beyond his reckoning, was delighted at the bet. At supper he made fun of the young man, and said: “Think better of it, young man, while there is still time. To me two million is a trifle, but you are losing three or four of the best years of your life. I say three or four, because you’ll never stick it out any longer. Don’t forget either, you unhappy man, that voluntary confinement is a great deal harder to bear than compulsory. The thought that you have the right to step out in liberty at any moment will poison your whole existence in prison. I am sorry for you.”

And now the banker, walking to and fro, remembered all this, and asked himself: “Why did I make that bet? What is the good of that man’s losing fifteen years of his life and my throwing away two million? Can it prove that the death penalty is better or worse than imprisonment for life? No, no. It was all nonsensical and meaningless. On my part it was the caprice of a pampered man, and on his part simple greed for money...” Then he remembered what followed that evening. It was decided that the young man should spend the years of his captivity under the strictest supervision in one of the lodges in the banker’s garden. It was agreed that for fifteen years he should not be free to cross the threshold of the lodge, to see human beings, to hear the human voice, or to receive letters and newspapers. He was allowed to have a musical instrument and books, and was allowed to write letters, to drink wine, and to smoke. By the terms of the agreement, the only relations he could have with the outer world were through a little window made purposely for this; he might have anything he wanted—books, music, wine, and so on—in any quantity he desired by writing an order, but could only receive them through the window.

During the first year of his confinement, as far as one could judge from his brief notes, the prisoner suffered severely from loneliness and depression. The sounds of the piano could be heard continually day and night from his lodge. He refused wine and tobacco. Wine, he wrote, excites the desires, and desires are the worst foes of the prisoner; and besides, nothing could be more dreary than drinking good wine and seeing no one. And tobacco spoilt the air of his room. In the first year the books he sent for were principally of a light character: novels with a complicated love plot, sensational and fantastic stories, and so on.

In the second year the piano was silent in the lodge, and the prisoner asked only for the classics. In the fifth year music was audible again, and the prisoner asked for wine. Those who watched him through the window said that all that year he spent doing nothing but eating and drinking and lying on his bed, frequently yawning and angrily talking to himself. He did not read books. Sometimes at night he would sit down to write; he would spend hours writing, and in the morning tear up all that he had written. More than once he could be heard crying.

In the second half of the sixth year the prisoner began zealously studying languages, philosophy, and history. He threw himself eagerly into these studies—so much so that the banker had enough to do to get him the books he ordered. In the course of four years some six hundred volumes were procured at his request. It was during this period that the banker received the following letter from his prisoner: “My dear Jailer, I write these lines in six languages. Show them to people who know the languages. Let them read them. If they find not one single mistake I implore you to fire a shot in the garden. That shot will show me that my efforts have not been thrown away. The geniuses of all ages and of all lands speak different languages, but the same flame burns in them all. Oh, if you only knew what unearthly happiness my soul feels now from being able to understand them!” The prisoner’s desire was fulfilled. The banker ordered two shots to be fired in the garden.

Then after the tenth year, the prisoner sat immovably at the table and read nothing but the Gospel. It seemed strange to the banker that a man who in four years had mastered six hundred learned volumes should waste nearly a year over one thin book easy of comprehension. Theology and histories of religion followed the Gospels. In the last two years of his confinement the prisoner read an immense quantity of books quite indiscriminately. At one time he was busy with the natural sciences, then he would ask for Byron or Shakespeare. There were notes of his demanding at the same time books on chemistry, a manual of medicine, a novel, and some treatise on philosophy or theology. His reading suggested a man swimming in the sea among the wreckage of his ship, and trying to save his life by greedily clutching first at one spar and then at another.

The old banker remembered all this, and thought: “To-morrow at twelve o’clock he will regain his freedom. By our agreement I ought to pay him two million. If I pay him, it is all over with me: I am utterly ruined.” Fifteen years before, his millions had been beyond his reckoning; now he was afraid to ask himself which were greater, his debts or his assets. Desperate gambling on the Stock Exchange, wild speculation and the excitability which he could not get over even in advancing years, had by degrees led to the decline of his fortune and the proud, fearless, self-confident millionaire had become a banker of middling rank, trembling at every rise and fall in his investments. “Cursed bet!” muttered the old man, clutching his head in despair. “Why didn’t the man die? He is only forty now. He will take my last penny from me, he will marry, will enjoy life, will gamble on the Exchange; while I shall look at him with envy like a beggar, and hear from him every day the same sentence: ‘I am indebted to you for the happiness of my life, let me help you!’ No, it is too much! The one means of being saved from bankruptcy and disgrace is the death of that man!”

It struck three o’clock, the banker listened; everyone was asleep in the house and nothing could be heard outside but the rustling of the chilled trees. Trying to make no noise, he took from a fireproof safe the key of the door which had not been opened for fifteen years, put on his overcoat, and went out of the house. It was dark and cold in the garden. Rain was falling. A damp cutting wind was racing about the garden, howling and giving the trees no rest. The banker strained his eyes, but could see neither the earth nor the white statues, nor the lodge, nor the trees. Going to the spot where the lodge stood, he twice called the watchman. No answer followed. Evidently the watchman had sought shelter from the weather, and was now asleep somewhere in the kitchen or the greenhouse.

The banker felt his way in the dark up the steps and into the entry of the lodge, then fumbled for the passage and struck a match. Not a soul was there. Someone’s bedstead with no bedding on it stood there, and in the corner there was a dark cast-iron stove. The seals on the door leading to the prisoner’s rooms were intact. When the match went out the old man, trembling with emotion, peeped through the little window. A candle was burning dimly in the prisoner’s room. He was sitting at the table. Nothing could be seen of him but his back, the hair on his head, and his hands. Open books were lying on the table, on the two easy-chairs, and on the carpet near the table. Five minutes passed and the prisoner did not once stir. Fifteen years’ imprisonment had taught him to sit still. The banker tapped at the window with his finger, and the prisoner made no movement whatever in response. Then the banker cautiously broke the seals off the door and put the key in the keyhole. The rusty lock gave a grating sound and the door creaked. Sitting at the table was a man unlike ordinary people. He was a skeleton with the skin drawn tight over his bones, with long curls like a woman’s and a shaggy beard. His face was yellow with an earthy tint in it, his cheeks were hollow, his back long and narrow, and the hand on which his shaggy head was propped was so thin and delicate that it was dreadful to look at it. His hair was already streaked with silver, and seeing his emaciated, aged-looking face, no one would have believed that he was only forty. He was asleep. In front of his bowed head there lay on the table a sheet of paper on which there was something written in fine handwriting.

“Poor creature!” thought the banker, “he is asleep and most likely dreaming of the millions! And I have only to take this half-dead man, throw him on the bed, stifle him a little with the pillow, and the most conscientious expert would find no sign of a violent death. But let us first read what he has written here...” The banker took the page from the table and read as follows: “To-morrow at twelve o’clock I regain my freedom and the right to associate with other men, but before I leave this room and see the sunshine, I think it necessary to say a few words to you. With a clear conscience I tell you, as before God, who beholds me, that I despise freedom and life and health, and all that in your books is called the good things of the world. For fifteen years I have intently studied earthly life. It is true I neither saw the earth nor men, but in your books I drank fragrant wine, I sang songs, I hunted stags and wild boars in the forests, loved women... Beauties as ethereal as clouds, created by the magic of your poets and geniuses, visited me at night, and whispered in my ears wonderful tales that intoxicated my brain. In your books I climbed to the peaks of Elbruz and Mont Blanc, and from there I saw the sun rise and in the evening watched it flood the sky, the ocean, and the mountaintops with gold and crimson. In your books I have flung myself into the bottomless pit, performed miracles, slain, burned towns, preached new religions, conquered whole kingdoms... Your books gave me wisdom. All that the unresting thought of man has created in the ages is compressed into a small compass in my brain. I know that I am wiser than all of you. And I despise your books, I despise wisdom and the blessings of this world. It is all worthless, fleeting, illusory, and deceptive, like a mirage. You may be proud, wise, and fine, but death will wipe you off the face of the earth as though you were no more than mice burrowing under the floor, and your posterity, your history, your immortal geniuses will burn or freeze together with the earthly globe. You have lost your reason and taken the wrong path. You have taken lies for truth, and hideousness for beauty. To prove to you in action how I despise all that you live by, I renounce the two million of which I once dreamed as of paradise and which now I despise. To deprive myself of the right to the money I shall go out from here five hours before the time fixed, and so break the compact...”

When the banker had read this he laid the page on the table, kissed the strange man on the head, and went out of the lodge, weeping. At no other time, even when he had lost heavily on the Stock Exchange, had he felt so great a contempt for himself. When he got home he lay on his bed, but his tears and emotion kept him for hours from sleeping. Next morning the watchmen ran in with pale faces, and told him they had seen the man who lived in the lodge climb out of the window into the garden, go to the gate, and disappear. The banker went at once with his servants to the lodge and made sure of the flight of his prisoner. To avoid arousing unnecessary talk, he took from the table the writing in which the millions were renounced, and when he got home locked it up in the fireproof safe.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, The Bet is a story about a wager. At a party fifteen years before the main action, a wealthy banker and a young lawyer argue about capital punishment. The banker thinks death is quicker and more humane than life imprisonment. The lawyer, who is twenty-five, says that to live in any way is better than not to live at all. The banker, half joking, offers two million rubles if the lawyer can stay locked in a lodge in the garden for fifteen years without speaking to anyone. The lawyer ups the stakes from a shorter term, and the bet is made.

The story then jumps to the present, a dark autumn night. The banker is no longer rich. His speculations and his reckless temper have eaten away his fortune, and the two million he once offered so carelessly would now ruin him. He decides he has to kill the prisoner before the deadline, and he creeps into the lodge to do it. This is the real engine of the story. A bet about ideas has turned into a temptation to murder, and the banker's own moral decline is laid bare in the process.

What he finds is a transformed man. The lawyer, now forty, is thin, ragged and asleep over a letter. The banker reads it, and this letter is the heart of the story. Over the fifteen years the prisoner has read everything: languages, philosophy, history, poetry, and finally the Gospels. He has lived whole lives inside books. He writes that in them he drank wine, hunted stags, climbed Mont Blanc and saw the sun rise from its peaks. Then comes the shock. He says he has become wiser than everyone outside, and for that very reason he despises it all. He calls the blessings of the world worthless and fleeting, like a mirage, and says death will wipe people away like mice under a floor.

To prove it, he will walk out five hours before the deadline and forfeit the money. That is the meaning of the ending, and it is an irony with several edges. The man who once believed that any life is better than none has discovered, through the very experience of living only in his mind, that the things most people chase are empty. The banker, meanwhile, who thought himself the wise and practical one, reads the letter and weeps, kissing the sleeping man on the head. At no other time, Chekhov tells us, had he felt so great a contempt for himself.

A first-time reader can miss one more layer. The banker locks the renunciation note in the safe, and he does so to avoid gossip. Even in his moment of shame, his instinct is to protect appearances. Nothing has really changed in him. The story offers a flash of grace and then shows how little it may last.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Bet is very economical, and its structure is close to flawless. It is built like a trap with two jaws. The first jaw is the dinner-party argument, the second is the night in the lodge, and the long middle is told in a few quick summaries of what the prisoner reads year by year. Chekhov compresses fifteen years into a few paragraphs, and we feel both the length of the time and the speed of the change. The prisoner begins with light novels and music, moves to the classics and languages, then to philosophy and the Gospels, and this trajectory is the story's argument in miniature. It shows a mind searching for something and, we are told, not finding it where the world says to look.

The strongest craft decision is to make the banker our point of view for the climax. We do not get inside the lawyer. We see him through the eyes of a man who came to murder him, and whose own wretchedness colors everything. That lets the letter hit with real force, because we receive it as the banker does, as an accusation. The physical details, the long curls like a woman's, the yellow face, the hand so thin it is dreadful to look at, make the prisoner almost ghostly, a figure from another order of being.

But the story has weaknesses, and they are worth stating plainly. The letter is a speech, and speeches can feel like the author stepping forward. The prisoner's praise of books, followed by his contempt for them, is stirring but somewhat abstract. We are told he is wise rather than shown any wisdom beyond a rhetorical rejection of the world. A skeptical reader might ask why fifteen years of reading would lead to a flat renunciation of life rather than a deeper engagement with it. The lawyer's conclusion, that all earthly things are illusion, sounds suspiciously like the sort of thing the story elsewhere seems to doubt, and Chekhov, who preferred ambiguity, may have wanted exactly that tension. Still, the effect is somewhat schematic.

The framing device is also a little artificial. A wager over two million rubles is a fairy-tale premise, closer to a fable than to the slice-of-life realism Chekhov is known for. The banker's turn from jovial host to would-be murderer is quick, and his financial ruin is mentioned rather than dramatized. These are the conventions of a parable, and parables ask for some indulgence.

What redeems all this is the ending. The lawyer leaves, the money stays, and nothing is neatly resolved. The banker has been shamed but is not shown reformed. The prisoner has escaped the cell but has also, in a sense, escaped the whole game of the world. Chekhov refuses to tell us who won. That open, ironic finish is what keeps the story alive, and it is the mark of a writer who would sooner pose a question than hand down a verdict.

III. The Impact It Had

The Bet did not change literature the way some of Chekhov's plays did, but it has had a steady, quiet life. It appeared in 1889, a period in which Chekhov was moving from the comic sketches that made his name toward the longer, more somber work of his maturity. Within a year he made his famous journey across Siberia to the prison island of Sakhalin, an experience that deepened his interest in confinement, suffering and the question of what a society owes its prisoners. The Bet, written just before, can be read as an early imaginative version of that preoccupation.

Its influence has been mostly through the idea. The notion of a bet between a rich man and a poor one, with a human life as the stake, has been echoed in countless later stories, films and television episodes. Anyone who has seen a plot in which someone is locked away for a long stretch to win a prize, only to emerge changed in ways the wager never anticipated, is working in the shadow of this premise, whether or not they know the source. The story also lent itself to the stage, and has been adapted for radio, theater, opera and film in several countries, including productions in Russia and elsewhere that treat it as a compact moral drama.

In classrooms, it has become a standard. It is short, it is clear, and it raises questions that students can argue about for an hour. Is solitary confinement a punishment worse than death? What is education for? What would you give up for money, and what would you give up money for? Teachers also use it to show how a story can turn on irony, since the outcome is the reverse of what both men expected at the start.

For Chekhov himself, the story sits among the tales in which he engaged with Tolstoy's moral philosophy. Tolstoy had urged renunciation of property and worldly ambition, and for a time many Russian intellectuals followed. Chekhov was drawn to it and uneasy with it. The prisoner's contempt for the world looks, at first, like a Tolstoyan conclusion. But the story's overall mood is more skeptical. The renunciation costs the lawyer his youth, his health and his place among other people, and the banker's moved reaction fades into self-protective silence. Chekhov seems to be testing the doctrine, not endorsing it.

Perhaps the most lasting effect is on how readers think about the death penalty and imprisonment. The opening argument is not a dated curiosity. People still debate whether a long life behind bars is a mercy or a cruelty, and the story places that debate inside a human drama rather than a legal one. It shows that what a punishment does to a person's mind may be something no law-giver can predict.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Bet first of all for its sheer narrative pull. Few stories of this length set up a premise so cleanly and pay it off so satisfyingly. The dinner-party argument is lively and believable, with its tobacco smoke and its tipsy talk. The banker's night walk through the dark garden, in the cold and the rain, has the tension of a thriller. Even readers who care nothing for philosophy tend to feel the suspense of the banker standing over a sleeping man with murder in his mind.

They have also loved the way the story honors books. The prisoner's letter is a hymn to reading as much as a rejection of it. He describes flying over mountains, hunting in forests, being visited by ethereal beauties, all without leaving his room. For anyone who has ever disappeared into a novel, this passage is a flash of recognition. The story seems to say that a life of the mind is a real life, rich and strange, and that someone could genuinely come out the other side having lived more than the people who stayed outside.

Another source of affection is the ending's moral shape. The man with all the advantages, the wealthy banker, ends up the smaller figure. The poor lawyer, who took the bet because he needed money and had nothing else, walks away with his dignity. Readers who dislike the idea that wealth makes a person wise or important find this deeply satisfying. There is a quiet justice in the way the banker's contempt for himself, in the final lines, is more honest than anything he said at the party.

Writers have admired the story as a lesson in compression. Chekhov was famous for the advice, reported by those who knew him, that one should cut away everything that does not matter, and The Bet is a good demonstration. We get fifteen years in a handful of paragraphs, a whole philosophy in a single letter, and a moral reversal in a few lines. The prose in English translation, which varies depending on the translator, is plain and clear, and the story is often one of the first pieces of Russian literature that English-speaking readers meet.

Finally, people love it because it asks a question that never goes out of date. We all make bargains with our own lives, trading years for something we think we want. The lawyer trades fifteen years for money and discovers he no longer wants the money. The banker keeps his money and loses something harder to name. Readers return to the story, long after finishing it, to ask what they themselves would have written in that letter.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Not everyone has been equally persuaded by The Bet, and the criticisms are worth hearing. The most common complaint is that it is more a parable than a story. The characters are types, the banker and the lawyer, rather than the fully textured individuals that fill Chekhov's greatest work. We learn little about either man's family, past or private life. The banker is greedy and proud, the lawyer is idealistic and then ascetic, and they serve the argument more than they live in their own right. Readers who prize psychological depth, which is what Chekhov does best elsewhere, sometimes find this one a little thin.

A related objection concerns the prisoner's transformation. Critics have asked whether the lawyer's renunciation is truly earned. His letter claims wisdom, but the evidence on the page is mostly his own say-so. A reader might wonder whether fifteen years of isolation have produced insight or simply a kind of bitterness, even a mild madness. Chekhov leaves this deliberately open, but some find that the story leans too heavily on the prisoner's grand pronouncements without testing them.

Others have pointed to the story's resemblance to the tradition of moral fables, and some have felt it lacks the subtlety of Chekhov's later short fiction, such as stories where nothing much seems to happen and yet everything shifts. The Bet, with its dramatic wager and its stark ending, can feel closer to the work of other storytellers who loved a strong premise. Chekhov himself was wary of preaching, and a few readers have worried that this story, for all its irony, comes close to a sermon on the vanity of worldly things.

There is also the question of plausibility. A man kept in solitary confinement for fifteen years, in a comfortable lodge but without any human contact, would, by most modern accounts, suffer severe psychological damage. The story treats his isolation as a kind of retreat or school. Modern readers, aware of what research has since said about prolonged isolation, may find that the story is too kind to the experience, or that its drama requires us to ignore the real harm. That is an anachronistic complaint, but a fair one for contemporary classrooms.

Finally, there is the matter of the banker. Some readers feel his sudden shame is too quick and too complete, and that the closing detail of locking the note away to avoid gossip is almost too cynical a twist. Others find it exactly right. As with much of Chekhov, the disagreement itself is a sign that the story is doing real work, even where readers part company on whether it succeeds.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Bet belongs to the great tradition of nineteenth-century Russian realism, but it leans toward the fable. Its ancestors include the moral tales of Tolstoy, who wrote short, plain stories about how people should live, and the philosophical dramas of Dostoevsky, who was fascinated by murder and conscience. Chekhov borrows the setting of a moral test and the question of what makes life worth having, but he removes the certainty. Where Tolstoy tells you the answer, Chekhov tells you that the answer is not as simple as anyone thinks.

It also belongs to a long line of stories about wagers and the devil's bargain. The idea that a man might stake years of his life for a prize goes back to folk tales and to Goethe's Faust, where a scholar bets his soul against the devil. In Chekhov's version, there is no devil, only human greed and human pride, and the result is quieter and more ambiguous.

Later literature has picked up the theme of imprisonment as a school of the mind. Stories and novels about prisoners who read their way to wisdom, from the long letters of political prisoners to modern tales of solitary confinement, trace some of their lineage here. Writers like Jorge Luis Borges, who loved philosophical premises delivered in the shape of a brief tale, would recognize the form. The story also anticipates the literature of the twentieth century in which characters are tested by arbitrary conditions, as in the work of Franz Kafka, though Chekhov is warmer and more humane than Kafka.

Within Chekhov's own work, The Bet sits beside stories that look squarely at self-deception, such as those about doctors, clerks and landowners who discover their lives are not what they believed. It is more compact and more openly argued than most, and so it makes a fine door into his world. A reader who starts here may then move to the longer, quieter stories, and to the plays like The Seagull and The Cherry Orchard, where the philosophy goes underground.

Why does it still matter? Because its central questions have not been settled. How should a society treat those it locks away? What is the value of a life lived mostly in thought? Do money and success actually make us wise? And what do we do when we see, for one clear moment, that we have been foolish? The Bet does not answer these. It lets two men fail to answer them, one by walking out into the dawn and the other by locking a note in a safe. It takes ten minutes to read, and it is a story that tends to stay with a reader far longer than that.
