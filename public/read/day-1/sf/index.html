Bradbury 1000 • Night 1 • San Francisco Deep-Dive Story

The Buried Ghost Fleet Beneath Yerba Buena Cove and Jackson Square (1849–1851)

San Francisco — How 500 abandoned Gold Rush sailing ships became the subterranean salons, warehouses, and bedrock of downtown San Francisco (California Gold Rush (1848–1855)) • 20 min read

“Six hundred ships lie anchored in the stream, their sails furled, their yards cockbilled, and not a living soul aboard save the harbor rats.” — Bayard Taylor, Eldorado (1850)

Every weekday morning, thousands of commuters stepping off the Muni Metro light-rail cars at Embarcadero Station or striding past the white quartz wings of the Transamerica Pyramid at Montgomery and Clay streets walk across twenty feet of compacted dune sand, quarried graywacke rubble, and dense blue estuarine clay—directly over the sheared masts, white-oak ribs, and copper-sheathed hulls of more than forty buried nineteenth-century sailing vessels. Today’s Financial District shoreline sits nearly half a mile east of where the cold green waters of San Francisco Bay lapped in the spring of 1848. Back then, before steam shovels and black powder reshaped the peninsula, a shallow crescent of tidal water called *Yerba Buena Cove* curved inland all the way to Montgomery Street, bounded by the rocky promontory of Clark’s Point at the foot of Telegraph Hill to the north and windswept Rincon Point to the south. Along the sandy bluff above Montgomery grew thick mats of the fragrant wild mint vine (*Clinopodium douglasii*) that Spanish missionaries and Mexican rancheros called *yerba buena*.

For decades after Captain Juan Bautista de Anza and Father Pedro Font surveyed the peninsula in 1776, Yerba Buena Cove remained one of the quietest anchorages on the Pacific rim. While Franciscan friars tended Mission San Francisco de Asís two miles inland beside Mission Dolores Lagoon and Mexican soldiers garrisoned the adobe quadrangle of the Presidio, only an occasional New England hide-and-tallow brig or Russian fur bark dropped anchor in the cove—as Richard Henry Dana Jr. chronicled in *Two Years Before the Mast* after visiting aboard the brig *Pilgrim* in December 1835. Even when Alcalde Washington Allon Bartlett officially rechristened the muddy village of Yerba Buena as *San Francisco* on January 30, 1847, during the Mexican-American War, the entire settlement counted barely eight hundred souls, a cluster of frame shanties around the dusty plaza of Portsmouth Square, and a single weekly newspaper printed by Mormon elder Samuel Brannan on a wooden hand press.

Everything changed on the frosty morning of January 24, 1848, up in the Sierra Nevada foothills along the South Fork of the American River, when carpenter James W. Marshall spotted malleable flakes of yellow metal glinting in the granite tailrace of John Sutter’s sawmill at Coloma. For three months, skeptical townspeople dismissed rumors of the strike—until May 12, 1848, when Sam Brannan stepped off the Sacramento launch after quietly buying up every pan, pickaxe, and shovel in the region, strode down Montgomery Street waving his beaver hat in one hand and a quinine vial brimming with coarse gold dust in the other, and bellowed: *'Gold! Gold! Gold from the American River!'* Within forty-eight hours, San Francisco emptied like a breached reservoir. Blacksmiths dropped their tongs, carpenters abandoned half-framed roofs, soldiers deserted the Presidio by moonlight, and both local newspapers suspended publication for lack of readers and typesetters.

When President James K. Polk confirmed the strike to the United States Congress in his annual message on December 5, 1848—displaying a tin tea caddy packed with two hundred and thirty ounces of California placer gold at the War Department—gold fever leaped across every ocean on Earth. Between April 1849 and December 1850, more than seven hundred and fifty wooden sailing vessels—blunt-bowed New Bedford whalers, sleek Baltimore clippers, three-masted Maine merchantmen, Chilean brigs, British snows, French barks, and Chinese junks—surged through the mile-wide narrows of the Golden Gate. They carried over ninety thousand *Argonauts* who had gambled their lives on one of two grueling maritime gauntlets: either the eighteen-thousand-mile, six-month voyage around the freezing icebergs of Cape Horn, or the malarial portage in dugout canoes up the Chagres River and across the mule trails of Panama to catch overcrowded Pacific steamers.

The instant a vessel rounded Clark’s Point and dropped her iron anchor into the mud of Yerba Buena Cove, an unstoppable maritime mutiny unfolded. Not only did every passenger scramble into Whitehall rowboats bound for the Sacramento and Stockton river steamers, but the ship’s own sailors, boatswains, carpenters, cooks, first mates, and frequently the captains themselves threw down their marlinspikes and bolted for the Sierra mines. Who would scrub holystoned decks for merchant-marine wages of twelve dollars a month when a man with a tin washpan and a butcher knife could pry sixteen dollars’ worth of gold dust out of a gravel bar at Mormon Island in a single afternoon? Desperate captains tried clapping their crews in irons belowdecks or anchoring beside the warships of the U.S. Pacific Squadron, only to watch the naval sentries row ashore with the deserters before dawn.

By the winter of 1849–1850, more than five hundred abandoned three-masted ships, brigs, and barks swung idly on their anchor chains across Yerba Buena Cove, their canvas sails rotting in the salt fog and their yardarms tangled like dead branches in a flooded forest. When *New-York Tribune* correspondent Bayard Taylor looked out over the waterfront from the slopes of Telegraph Hill for his classic book *Eldorado* (1850), he beheld a ghost armada unlike anything in naval history: six hundred hulls riding at anchor with no return cargo to carry and not a living soul aboard save the harbor rats. So tightly packed were the deserted vessels between Rincon Point and Clark’s Point that when winter southeasters swept the bay, hulls ground their oak gunwales together with a groaning roar that echoed all the way up Clay Street to Portsmouth Square.

Onshore, meanwhile, San Francisco was choking on its own growth amid a desperate shortage of timber, shelter, and dry land. Thirty thousand newcomers were camped in flapping canvas tents and scrap-wood lean-tos pitched across mud knee-deep with rainwater, where cholera and dysentery raged through the winter. Because the northern San Francisco peninsula was virtually treeless, rough-sawn pine boards imported from Oregon or Maine sold for a staggering one dollar per square foot—five hundred dollars per thousand board feet—while a single brick cost a dollar and a damp bunk in a communal rooming house rented for thirty dollars a month in gold dust. Gazing out across the cove at the forest of idle ships framed from seasoned New England white oak, hackmatack knees, copper bolts, and Malabar teak, pragmatic speculators realized a stunning economic truth: those ships were worth ten times more sitting in the mud as instant buildings than sailing the high seas.

This architectural alchemy was supercharged by a uniquely San Franciscan real-estate invention: the *water-lot market*. Under the March 1847 decree of Brigadier General Stephen Watts Kearny and the municipal surveys of Irish civil engineer Jasper O’Farrell, San Francisco had platted its rectangular street grid straight out into the waters of Yerba Buena Cove, auctioning off thousands of submerged 'water lots' that lay beneath six to fifteen feet of tide between Montgomery Street and modern-day Spear Street. Under frontier legal custom, paper deeds were constantly challenged by squatters; the surest way to establish indisputable legal possession of a submerged water lot was to occupy it physically. Enterprising syndicates bought condemned or crewless ships at U.S. Marshal and Admiralty auctions for a few thousand dollars, waited for the highest spring tide of the month, and used draft horses, capstans, and steam tugs to drag the hulls directly onto their surveyed street corners in the mud.

Once a hull grounded firmly on the tidal flat at the intersection of two future streets, carpenters drove tall redwood pilings around her bilges so the next high tide could never float her away, pumped water into her hold to settle her keel deep into the blue clay, and took cross-cut saws to her hull. No ship embodied this metamorphosis more famously than the 341-ton full-rigged ship *Niantic*. Built in Waldoboro, Maine, in 1835 for the Canton tea and silk trade and later converted into a Rhode Island whaler, the *Niantic* was cruising the Pacific when her captain learned of the Gold Rush, sailed into Panama City in May 1849, replaced his whale-oil casks with wooden bunks, and carried two hundred and forty-five cheering Argonauts through the Golden Gate on July 5, 1849. The next morning, her crew deserted for the mines.

Unable to sail her out of the bay, Captain Henry Cleaveland sold the *Niantic* to the mercantile firm of Godeffroy, Sillem & Co. On a high August tide, crews hauled the *Niantic* right up to the muddy shore at the northwest corner of Clay and Sansome streets—just steps from where the Transamerica Pyramid rises today. Shipwrights sawed off her three masts flush with the deck, enclosed her weather deck beneath a shingled gable roof topped by a flagpole, cantilevered a two-story wooden veranda around her sides, and cut a wide rectangular doorway directly into her oak hull at boardwalk level beneath the painted sign *'Restorer of Health / Storage for 20,000 Barrels.'* Inside her cavernous hold—divided into dry warehousing decks below and sunny offices above—merchants stored pork, flour, and French champagne while commission brokers transacted business inside her ribs.

Within months, the *Niantic* was joined by an entire fleet of permanently beached *storeships* that formed the commercial spine of Gold Rush San Francisco. One block south, at Sacramento and Battery streets, Captain J. H. Peirce moored the 412-ton New York packet *Apollo*, cutting doorways into her stern and converting her between-decks into the *Apollo Warehouses* below and a chandelier-lit saloon and lodging house above. One block north, at Clay and Battery streets, the 409-ton ship *General Harrison* was pinned in place with pilings as a wholesale dry-goods emporium. At Battery and Washington streets, the Town Council paid thirty-five hundred dollars in October 1849 for the 150-ton brig *Euphemia*, riveting iron bars across her portholes to turn her into San Francisco’s first municipal prison hulk. Further north along Pacific Street near Battery, the three-masted ship *Arkansas* was engulfed by landfill while her wooden bowsprit still jutted straight through the front wall of the *Old Ship Ale House*.

To connect these beached wooden arks to dry land at Montgomery Street and to the deep-water clippers unloading out in the bay, San Francisco’s wharf builders launched an astonishing feat of timber engineering. Led by promoter Henry Meiggs and the Central Wharf Joint Stock Company, pile-driving barges steamed out into Yerba Buena Cove and hammered thousands of eighty-foot redwood and Douglas fir trunks deep into the mud. Starting in May 1849, Central Wharf (popularly known as *Long Wharf*, now Commercial Street) pushed two thousand feet straight out into the bay, quickly paralleled by Market Street Wharf, California Street Wharf, and Sacramento Street Wharf. Soon, cross-streets of heavy pine planks—Sansome, Battery, Front, and Davis—were spiked across pilings perpendicular to the wharves, weaving an elevated wooden checkerboard fifteen feet above the swirling tide.

To turn that treacherous wooden grid into solid ground, contractor David Hewes imported a rail-mounted steam shovel from the East Coast that San Franciscans dubbed the *Steam Paddy*. Laying temporary iron tracks into the towering eighty-foot sand dunes that blocked Market Street and Happy Valley (modern South of Market), Hewes’s Steam Paddy scooped up twenty-five hundred tons of yellow sand every day, dumping it into strings of wooden tip-carts that rolled down the incline and tipped their loads into the salt-water lagoons between the wharves and storeships. Simultaneously, quarrymen blasted thousands of tons of fractured sandstone off the eastern cliff face of Telegraph Hill and dumped the boulders around the hulls of the *Niantic*, the *Apollo*, and the *General Harrison*, sealing their copper-sheathed bottoms inside a rising matrix of rock, sand, and blue-gray clay.

Then struck the catastrophe that erased the visible fleet from the skyline and locked its lower hulls beneath the streets: the Great Conflagration of May 3–4, 1851, the fifth and most destructive of the six fires that incinerated Gold Rush San Francisco. Breaking out at eleven o’clock on the night of May 3 in a paint shop on Portsmouth Square, the flames were caught by a screaming westerly gale. Because the pine plank streets and elevated boardwalks rested on pilings above open air spaces, the wind-whipped fire raced underneath the streets like a blowtorch through a flue. Within ten hours, eighteen square blocks encompassing fifteen hundred buildings vanished in a cyclone of embers. Cut off from escape as the wooden wharves burned around them, the landlocked storeships *Niantic*, *Apollo*, and *General Harrison* burned furiously down to the wet mudline, boiling the water around their keels.

When dawn broke on May 5, 1851, over a smoking plain of twisted iron and blackened redwood pilings, builders did not waste a week attempting to dredge out the charred oak bottoms of the burned fleet. Civil engineers realized that a waterlogged white-oak ship’s hull pinned into anaerobic bay mud formed a sturdy raft foundation on which to erect masonry walls. At Clay and Sansome streets, the proprietors of the *Niantic* leveled her charred ribs beneath the sidewalk and raised the three-story brick *Niantic Hotel* directly atop the 1835 tea ship’s keel. When workmen excavated deeper for a new foundation on the site in 1872, their picks struck the intact lower hold of the *Niantic* and uncovered thirty-five cases of 1849 Jacquesson & Fils Champagne preserved at cellar temperature in the oxygen-free clay—which the delighted construction crew uncorked and drank on the spot.

More than a century later, as modern steel skyscrapers drove deep foundation caissons through the Financial District, the buried ghost fleet returned to light in a series of dramatic archaeological discoveries. In May 1978, when developers demolished the 1907 Niantic Block at Clay and Sansome streets, maritime archaeologists led by Dr. Allen Pastron and Dr. James P. Delgado stepped into the muddy shoring pit twenty feet below street level and uncovered the copper-sheathed oak hull of the *Niantic*, recovering champagne bottles, stoneware inkwells, and brass ship’s spikes before lifting a slice of her bow for the San Francisco Maritime National Historical Park. In December 1994, twenty-five feet beneath Justin Herman Plaza at the foot of Market Street, a tunnel-boring machine excavating the Muni Metro Turnback chewed straight through the thirty-inch oak timbers of the *Rome*, an 1850 whaler sunk as landfill; and in August 2001, at Clay and Battery streets, Pastron’s team unearthed the charred hull of the *General Harrison*, still packed with spiced wine, leather miners’ boots, and pine planks blackened on May 4, 1851.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Water-Lot Speculation: How Ships Claimed Submerged Real Estate

Why did 1849 speculators intentionally sink or beach seaworthy ships at exact street intersections that were still under ten feet of bay water? Under the Alcalde water-lot grants of 1847–1850, San Francisco surveyed and sold submerged grid parcels across Yerba Buena Cove before the land even existed. Under local maritime custom, whoever moored a ship and drove pilings around its hull on a water lot established physical 'possession' of that future city block. Once sand and telegraph-hill quarry rock filled in the surrounding cove, the sunken ship hull legally transformed into prime downtown real estate. 2. Anaerobic Bay Mud: Why 1849 Artifacts Survive Intact Underground

San Francisco's 'Young Bay Mud'—a dense, blue-gray estuarine clay deposited after the last Ice Age—contains virtually zero dissolved oxygen below the surface. When the May 1851 fire burned ships like the Niantic, the Apollo, and the General Harrison down to the mudline and landfill buried them immediately afterward, wood-eating shipworms (Teredo navalis) and aerobic bacteria were completely sealed out. As a result, leather boots, paper labels on wine bottles, oak hull timbers, and even stores of 1849 dried beans and champagne survived for 150 years beneath downtown skyscrapers.
