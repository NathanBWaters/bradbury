Bradbury 1000 • Night 1 • San Francisco Deep-Dive Story

The Buried Ghost Fleet Beneath Yerba Buena Cove and Jackson Square (1849–1851)

San Francisco — How 500 abandoned Gold Rush sailing ships became the subterranean salons, warehouses, and bedrock of downtown San Francisco (California Gold Rush (1848–1855)) • 10 min read

“Six hundred ships lie anchored in the stream, their sails furled, their yards cockbilled, and not a living soul aboard save the harbor rats.” — Bayard Taylor, Eldorado (1850)

Every weekday morning, commuters stepping off the Muni Metro at Embarcadero Station or walking past the Transamerica Pyramid at Montgomery and Clay streets tread across twenty feet of compacted sand, rubble, and bay mud—directly over the masts, oak ribs, and copper-sheathed hulls of more than forty buried nineteenth-century sailing vessels. Today’s Financial District shoreline sits nearly half a mile east of where the waters of San Francisco Bay lapped in 1848. Back then, a shallow crescent of tidal water called *Yerba Buena Cove* curved inland all the way to Montgomery Street, between Telegraph Hill to the north and Rincon Point to the south.

When James W. Marshall spotted flecks of gold in the tailrace of Sutter’s Mill in January 1848 and President James K. Polk confirmed the strike to Congress that December, the sleepy settlement of Yerba Buena—renamed San Francisco in 1847 with barely 800 residents—became the maritime vortex of the world. Between April 1849 and December 1850, more than 750 wooden brigantines, barks, whalers, and clipper ships sailed through the Golden Gate from Boston, Valparaíso, Liverpool, Canton, and Sydney, disgorging over 90,000 'Argonauts' bound for the Sierra Nevada foothills.

The moment a ship dropped anchor in the mudflats of Yerba Buena Cove, a peculiar mutiny occurred: not only did every passenger scramble into rowboats for the Sacramento river steamers, but the sailors, cooks, first mates, and often the captains themselves jumped ship and bolted for the goldfields. With no crews left to sail them home and no return cargo to carry, hundreds of three-masted ships swung idly at anchor until their rigging rotted in the salt fog, forming a floating forest of timber so dense that a person could almost walk from deck to deck across the cove.

In a treeless boomtown of canvas tents where a single rough pine board cost a dollar and a rented bunk cost thirty dollars a month in gold dust, enterprising San Franciscans looked out at the forest of abandoned hulls and realized the ships were worth far more sitting in the mud than sailing on the ocean. Speculators bought condemned ships at auction for a fraction of their value, hired teams of horses and steam tugs to haul them onto the tidal flats at high tide, drove redwood pilings around their hulls so they could never float away, and chopped square doorways directly into their oak sides.

Take the 341-ton packet ship *Niantic*, built in Waldoboro, Maine, for the China tea trade, which sailed into San Francisco from Panama with 245 gold-seekers on July 5, 1849. After her crew deserted to a man, her captain sold her on the spot. Dragged on a spring high tide right up to the corner of Clay and Sansome streets, the *Niantic* had her masts sawed off, a shingled roof with a veranda built over her main deck, and her hold converted into the *Niantic Warehouse* and dry-goods emporium, with offices painted on her hull reading 'Storage for 20,000 Barrels.'

Nearby at the foot of Sacramento Street, the ship *Apollo* became a floating saloon and lodging house; the *Euphemia*, a 150-ton brig lying at Battery and Washington streets, was purchased by the town council in October 1849 for $3,500 to serve as San Francisco’s first municipal prison chain-gang hulk; and the *General Harrison* sat landlocked at Clay and Battery streets as a bustling wholesale storehouse. Wooden plank wharves—Long Wharf (now Commercial Street), Central Wharf, and Market Street Wharf—pushed two thousand feet out into the cove on redwood pilings, linking the beached ships like beads on a grid of boardwalks.

Then came the Great Fire of May 3–4, 1851—the fifth of six catastrophic infernos that incinerated Gold Rush San Francisco between 1849 and 1851. Driven by gale-force western winds, flames raced along the pitch-pine boardwalks across eighteen city blocks, consuming the wooden superstructures of the *Niantic*, the *General Harrison*, and the *Apollo* down to the waterline. Rather than dredging out the charred hulls resting in the bay mud, builders simply dumped sand dunes leveled by steam shovels ('Steam Paddy') from Market Street directly over the burned ships and erected brick buildings on top of their waterlogged keels.

When workers excavated the foundation for the new Niantic Hotel in 1872, they struck the hull of the 1849 *Niantic* and recovered thirty-five cases of intact 1849 Jacquesson Champagne buried in the preservatively cold, anaerobic bay mud—which the construction crew promptly uncorked and drank on the spot. More than a century later, in 1978 underneath the corner of Clay and Sansome, and again in 2001 when crews dug the foundations of a modern high-rise over the *General Harrison*, maritime archaeologists led by Allen Pastron and James Delgado uncovered copper hull sheathing, burned pine planks, crates of spiced wine, brass ship spikes, and personal effects preserved like an underwater Pompeii beneath the financial heart of the city.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Water-Lot Speculation: How Ships Claimed Submerged Real Estate

Why did 1849 speculators intentionally sink or beach seaworthy ships at exact street intersections that were still under ten feet of bay water? Under the Alcalde water-lot grants of 1847–1850, San Francisco surveyed and sold submerged grid parcels across Yerba Buena Cove before the land even existed. Under local maritime custom, whoever moored a ship and drove pilings around its hull on a water lot established physical 'possession' of that future city block. Once sand and telegraph-hill quarry rock filled in the surrounding cove, the sunken ship hull legally transformed into prime downtown real estate. 2. Anaerobic Bay Mud: Why 1849 Artifacts Survive Intact Underground

San Francisco's 'Young Bay Mud'—a dense, blue-gray estuarine clay deposited after the last Ice Age—contains virtually zero dissolved oxygen below the surface. When the May 1851 fire burned ships like the Niantic, the Apollo, and the General Harrison down to the mudline and landfill buried them immediately afterward, wood-eating shipworms (Teredo navalis) and aerobic bacteria were completely sealed out. As a result, leather boots, paper labels on wine bottles, oak hull timbers, and even stores of 1849 dried beans and champagne survived for 150 years beneath downtown skyscrapers.
