Of Studies & The Architecture of Reading

Francis Bacon (1625) • 8 min read

Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested: the foundational Renaissance treatise on how reading shapes character and judgment.

Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability. Their chief use for delight, is in privateness and retiring; for ornament, is in discourse; and for ability, is in the judgment and disposition of business. For expert men can execute, and perhaps judge of particulars, one by one; but the general counsels, and the plots and marshalling of affairs, come best from those that are learned. To spend too much time in studies is sloth; to use them too much for ornament, is affectation; to make judgment wholly by their rules, is the humor of a scholar. They perfect nature, and are perfected by experience: for natural abilities are like natural plants, that need proyning by study; and studies themselves do give forth directions too much at large, except they be bounded in by experience.

Crafty men contemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them; for they teach not their own use; but that is a wisdom without them, and above them, won by observation. Read not to contradict and confute; nor to believe and take for granted; nor to find talk and discourse; but to weigh and consider. Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested; that is, some books are to be read only in parts; others to be read, but not curiously; and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention. Some books also may be read by deputy, and extracts made of them by others; but that would be only in the less important arguments, and the meaner sort of books, else distilled books are like common distilled waters, flashy things.

Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man. And therefore, if a man write little, he had need have a great memory; if he confer little, he had need have a present wit: and if he read little, he had need have much cunning, to seem to know that he doth not. Histories make men wise; poets witty; the mathematics subtle; natural philosophy deep; moral grave; logic and rhetoric able to contend. Abeunt studia in mores: studies pass into and influence manners. Nay, there is no stond or impediment in the wit, but may be wrought out by fit studies; like as diseases of the body may have appropriate exercises.

Bowling is good for the stone and reins; shooting for the lungs and breast; gentle walking for the stomach; riding for the head; and the like. So if a man’s wit be wandering, let him study the mathematics; for in demonstrations, if his wit be called away never so little, he must begin again. If his wit be not apt to distinguish or find differences, let him study the schoolmen; for they are cymini sectores, splitters of hairs. If he be not apt to beat over matters, and to call up one thing to prove and illustrate another, let him study the lawyers’ cases. So every defect of the mind may have a special receipt.

Yet let no man think that the architecture of a mind is built by mere accumulation of pages, as a quarry is heaped with unhewn stones. In our Advancement of Learning we have noted three distempers of learning that corrupt the student who reads without a compass. The first is fantastical learning, wherein men hunt more after words than matter—falling in love with choiceness of phrase, round and clean composition of sentence, and the sweet falling of clauses, while the weight of reality escapes them. The second is contentious learning, wherein men read only to arm themselves with cavils, disputes, and thorny subtleties to overthrow a rival in argument. The third is delicate learning, which treats books as mere tapestries for the parlor of vanity. True reading seeks none of these: it seeks the weight, the kernel, and the generative seed of things.

For we must beware lest our minds become either like the spider or like the ant. The men of mere scholastic dispute are like spiders, who spin out of their own bowels intricate cobwebs of speculation—admirable for the fineness of the thread and workmanship, but of no substance or profit. The mere collectors of facts and quotations, on the other hand, are like the ants, which only lay up and use their store just as they gather it, piling grain upon grain without transforming it. But the true reader and philosopher must imitate the bee, which follows a middle course: it gathers its material from the flowers of the garden and of the field, yet transforms and digests it by a power of its own into honey.

When therefore you sit down by night to your book, ask not merely what the author hath said, nor copy out his sentences as dead ornaments for your notebook, but observe how one nature illuminates another. The human understanding of its own nature readily supposes a greater degree of order and equality in things than it really finds; and when a man has once adopted an opinion, he draws all other things to support and agree with it. Against these Idols of the Tribe and Idols of the Cave—where every individual lives within a cavern of his own customs, education, and favorite books that refract and discolor the light of nature—nothing is so sovereign a remedy as the deliberate collision of diverse studies.

Let the reader who is accustomed to the strict demonstrations of natural philosophy steep his mind for an hour in the parables of the poets; let him who lives wholly in the imaginations of poesy or the passions of the stage temper his spirit with the severe and unsparing chronicles of history. For poesy was ever thought to have some participation of divineness, because it doth raise and erect the mind, by submitting the shows of things to the desires of the mind; whereas reason and history doth buckle and bow the mind unto the nature of things. When both faculties work together in the same breast, the mind is neither puffed up by empty dreams nor bowed down by servile custom.

In discourse and meditation upon what we have read, it is good also to vary and intermingle speech of the present occasion with arguments of perpetuity; and to mingle jest with earnest. Certain men wrap themselves in a solemn silence or a monotonous gravity, desiring to seem deeper than they are; and others run wholly into the sharpness of wit, shooting arrows at every mark. But he that hath digested his reading into his blood and judgment holds the bridle of his own thoughts: he knows when to question, when to pause, and when to strike direct to the marrow of the business.

Finally, remember that the monuments of wit and learning are more durable than the monuments of power or of the hands. For have not the verses of Homer continued twenty-five hundred years, or more, without the loss of a syllable or letter; during which time infinite palaces, temples, castles, cities, have been decayed and demolished? It is not possible to have the true pictures or statues of Cyrus, Alexander, Caesar, no nor of the kings or great personages of much later years; for the originals cannot last, and the copies cannot but leese of the life and truth. But the images of men’s wits and knowledges remain in books, exempted from the wrong of time and capable of perpetual renovation. Neither are they fitly to be called images, because they generate still, and cast their seeds in the minds of others, provoking and causing infinite actions and opinions in succeeding ages.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

Sir Francis Bacon (1561–1626)—Lord Chancellor of England, philosopher of the scientific revolution, and father of the English essay—first published a slender volume of ten Essais in 1597, inspired by Michel de Montaigne's French Essais (1580). Across three decades of political rise and catastrophic fall, Bacon expanded the collection to fifty-eight essays in his final 1625 edition, The Essayes or Counsels, Civill and Morall, of which 'Of Studies' is the crown jewel, here paired with his companion reflections from The Advancement of Learning (1605) and Novum Organum (1620). Writing as the printing press flooded Europe with books for the first time, Bacon recognized that humanity needed a disciplined method for transforming raw information into wisdom.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Two master metaphors govern Bacon's philosophy of reading: digestion ('Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested') and the Bee versus the Spider and the Ant. To merely browse is to taste; to read uncritically is to swallow whole; to read deeply is to chew and digest until the text becomes part of your own cellular structure ('Abeunt studia in mores'—studies pass into character). Equally transformative is Bacon's bee metaphor: unlike the spider (who spins self-referential cobwebs out of its own ego) or the ant (who hoards quotations without changing them), the bee gathers nectar from widely different flowers—poetry, history, science—and alchemizes them through its own digestive enzymes into honey.

3. Craft, Form & Rhetorical Technique

Bacon is the undisputed master of Renaissance senecan prose—a style built on razor-sharp tricolons (groups of three), balanced antitheses, and aphorisms that strike like coins stamped in gold. Look at the architectural symmetry of his opening sentence ('for delight, for ornament, and for ability') and his most famous triad: 'Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man.' Each clause contains no wasted fat; the verb 'maketh' is stated once and implied twice (zeugma), forcing the reader's mind to supply the connection. He then shifts seamlessly from abstract maxim to concrete Renaissance analogy—comparing mental exercises to physical sports like bowling, archery, and horseback riding.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Percy Bysshe Shelley admired Bacon so deeply that he declared in A Defence of Poetry, 'Lord Bacon was a poet,' praising the rhythmic majesty and figurative brilliance of his prose. For four centuries, 'Of Studies' has served as the inaugural manifesto of liberal education, commonplace-book keeping, and active reading—directly influencing John Locke, Samuel Johnson, Thomas Jefferson, and Mortimer Adler's How to Read a Book. Every modern discussion of information overload versus deep reading still echoes Bacon's distinction between 'distilled waters' (flashy summaries) and books 'chewed and digested.' Every night you spend weighing an essay's central metaphor against a short story and a poem trains your own intellect to see beneath the surface of convention and discover original connections across centuries of human thought.

5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

Why does Night 1 of the Bradbury 1,000-Night Odyssey open with Bacon's 'Of Studies' alongside Chekhov's 'The Bet' and Shelley's 'Ozymandias'? Because Bacon gives you the operating manual for the entire 1,000 nights! Notice how Bacon's closing paragraph—proclaiming that the 'monuments of wit and learning are more durable than the monuments of power' while palaces and statues crumble—speaks directly across two centuries to Shelley's shattered Ozymandias in the desert and to Chekhov's lawyer reading in his cell. Tonight, resolve to read not as an ant hoarding trivia nor a spider spinning dogma, but as Bacon's bee: chewing and digesting one story, one poem, and one essay into the honey of new metaphors.
