Nightly Essay • Aphoristic Moral & Intellectual Essay

Of Studies

Francis Bacon (1625) • 8 min read

Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested: the foundational Renaissance treatise on how reading shapes character and judgment.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Francis Bacon's "Of Studies" is one of the shortest famous essays in English, and one of the most quoted. Bacon was a lawyer, courtier and philosopher who rose to be Lord Chancellor of England under King James the First, then fell in disgrace in 1621 when he admitted taking gifts from people whose cases he judged. He first published a small book of essays in 1597, borrowing the idea from the French writer Montaigne, and kept expanding it for nearly thirty years. "Of Studies" is the most polished piece in that collection. It was written at a moment when printing had made books cheap and plentiful, and people needed advice on how to read them. Bacon's answer is brisk and practical. Reading is not decoration or an escape, he says. It is a tool for shaping judgment and character, and different books deserve different kinds of attention. Listen for his famous line about tasting, swallowing, and chewing books, and for how packed each sentence is.

Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability. Their chief use for delight, is in privateness and retiring; for ornament, is in discourse; and for ability, is in the judgment and disposition of business. For expert men can execute, and perhaps judge of particulars, one by one; but the general counsels, and the plots and marshalling of affairs, come best from those that are learned. To spend too much time in studies is sloth; to use them too much for ornament, is affectation; to make judgment wholly by their rules, is the humor of a scholar. They perfect nature, and are perfected by experience: for natural abilities are like natural plants, that need proyning by study; and studies themselves do give forth directions too much at large, except they be bounded in by experience.

Crafty men contemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them; for they teach not their own use; but that is a wisdom without them, and above them, won by observation. Read not to contradict and confute; nor to believe and take for granted; nor to find talk and discourse; but to weigh and consider. Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested; that is, some books are to be read only in parts; others to be read, but not curiously; and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention. Some books also may be read by deputy, and extracts made of them by others; but that would be only in the less important arguments, and the meaner sort of books, else distilled books are like common distilled waters, flashy things.

Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man. And therefore, if a man write little, he had need have a great memory; if he confer little, he had need have a present wit: and if he read little, he had need have much cunning, to seem to know that he doth not. Histories make men wise; poets witty; the mathematics subtle; natural philosophy deep; moral grave; logic and rhetoric able to contend. Abeunt studia in mores: studies pass into and influence manners. Nay, there is no stond or impediment in the wit, but may be wrought out by fit studies; like as diseases of the body may have appropriate exercises.

Bowling is good for the stone and reins; shooting for the lungs and breast; gentle walking for the stomach; riding for the head; and the like. So if a man’s wit be wandering, let him study the mathematics; for in demonstrations, if his wit be called away never so little, he must begin again. If his wit be not apt to distinguish or find differences, let him study the schoolmen; for they are cymini sectores, splitters of hairs. If he be not apt to beat over matters, and to call up one thing to prove and illustrate another, let him study the lawyers’ cases. So every defect of the mind may have a special receipt.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, "Of Studies" is a short list of advice about reading. Underneath, it is an argument about what knowledge is for. Bacon opens with three uses of study: delight, ornament, and ability. Delight is private, what you get alone in a quiet room. Ornament is public, the polish study gives your conversation. Ability is practical, the sound judgment you bring to real business. Then he ranks them without quite saying so. The whole essay leans toward ability, toward knowledge that changes how you act in the world.

He immediately warns against three ways of getting this wrong. "To spend too much time in studies is sloth; to use them too much for ornament, is affectation; to make judgment wholly by their rules, is the humor of a scholar." In other words, do not hide in books, do not show off with them, and do not let them replace your own eyes. Then comes one of his best images. Natural abilities are like plants that need pruning, and books are the pruning knife. But books alone give directions "too much at large," meaning too general, until experience bounds them in. Reading and living have to correct each other.

The next paragraph is the famous one. "Crafty men contemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them." Notice that he does not sneer only at the ignorant. The cunning dismiss books because they trust their own tricks, the simple worship books because they cannot judge them, and only the wise treat them as instruments. He adds that studies "teach not their own use." Nobody can learn from a book how to apply books. That wisdom comes "by observation," from paying attention to life.

Then the rules for reading itself. "Read not to contradict and confute; nor to believe and take for granted; nor to find talk and discourse; but to weigh and consider." Four bad motives, one good one: weighing. The tasting, swallowing and chewing line follows, which is really a lesson in budgeting attention. Most books deserve a sample. Some can be gulped. A very few deserve slow, complete digestion. He even allows reading "by deputy," having someone else summarize minor books, though he sniffs that such distilled books are like cheap distilled water, "flashy things."

The last third is about transformation. "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man." Each activity builds something different: stock, speed, precision. Then comes a catalogue: history makes men wise, poetry witty, mathematics subtle, and so on, ending in the Latin tag "Abeunt studia in mores," studies pass into character. His closing comparison, that every mental defect has its special remedy in study the way bowling is good for the kidneys and shooting for the lungs, treats the mind as a body to be trained. The reader a first-time listener might miss is that this is a self-improvement manual from an age that was only just inventing the idea.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, "Of Studies" is close to flawless, and that is also the source of its limits. Bacon writes in what critics call the aphoristic or "curt" style: short, balanced, self-contained sentences that each feel like a coin. "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man" works because of its rhythm, three clauses with the verb dropped in the second and third, so the reader supplies the pattern. The three-part structure, the antitheses, the compression, all make the essay unusually memorable. It is hard to think of a piece of prose of its length that has donated so many lines to the language.

The tasting-and-chewing metaphor is the best single stroke. It does in a handful of words what a modern book on reading strategy takes chapters to say, and it is physical, so it sticks. The pruning image for natural ability is nearly as good. Even the slightly strange medical comparison at the end has a logic: if the mind has weaknesses, you can prescribe reading the way a doctor prescribes exercise. Bacon's examples there, such as the wandering mind being cured by mathematics, are clever and a little funny.

But the same compression has costs. Because every sentence is a verdict, the essay has almost no room for doubt, complication or personal voice. Compare Montaigne, who thinks out loud, contradicts himself and tells you about his kidney stones. Bacon never lets you see him struggle. He sounds like a judge reading out a ruling, and in a sense he is. The cataloguing in the final section, with each subject matched to a single virtue, feels neat to the point of glibness. Is it really true that poets make men "witty" and logicians able to "contend," and that is all? It reads more like a sorting exercise than an observation.

There is also a question of dated assumptions. The essay speaks of "men" throughout and assumes a reader preparing for public affairs, law and counsel, which was the audience of a Jacobean gentleman. Its snobbery shows in the line about "the meaner sort of books." And its confidence that books can be ranked by how much attention they deserve sits uneasily with how readers actually behave. We often discover what deserves chewing only after we have swallowed it.

Finally, a modern reader may find the utilitarian streak a bit narrow. Delight gets one sentence of respect and is then overshadowed by use. A work that treats reading mostly as career and character development leaves out the sheer joy and strangeness that other essayists celebrate. Still, these are the faults of a very sharp tool. It does one job superbly, and the job is persuading you to read with a purpose.

III. The Impact It Had

The most visible impact of "Of Studies" is on the language itself. Phrases from it have lived far beyond the essay: reading makes a full man, some books are to be tasted and some chewed and digested, studies pass into manners. They appear in school mottoes, graduation speeches, library walls and the front pages of countless study-skills guides. Few essays have been quoted so often by people who have never read the rest of the book they came from.

For Bacon's own career, the essay belongs to a double life. He is remembered above all as a founder of the modern scientific method, through works like the Novum Organum of 1620, which argued that knowledge should come from careful observation and experiment, not from repeating ancient authorities. The essays were his lighter, more personal work, but they carry the same attitude. Notice that "Of Studies" insists wisdom is "won by observation" and that book learning needs to be "bounded in by experience." That is the empirical program in miniature. Readers who found the Novum Organum heavy often met his thinking first through the essays.

In literary history, Bacon is often credited with establishing the essay in English. Montaigne's French Essais came first and Bacon openly borrowed the word, but he turned the form in a different direction: not a wandering self-portrait but a distilled piece of counsel. The two models, the personal and the aphoristic, shaped everything that followed. Later English essayists such as Addison and Steele, Samuel Johnson, Charles Lamb, and Hazlitt each leaned toward one pole or the other, and Bacon was the standard-bearer for compression and authority.

The essay also became a permanent fixture in education. For centuries it was a staple of schoolbooks and examinations in Britain and its former colonies, where it was learned, parsed and sometimes memorized. Its Latin tag and its sorting of books helped shape how many generations were taught to think of reading as a discipline. Even today, when someone advises skimming some books and studying others closely, they are repeating Bacon, usually without knowing it.

Its modern afterlife includes everything from self-help to media studies. Writers on speed reading, note-taking and "reading for understanding" regularly start from his three categories. Advocates of the "great books" tradition use him as an ancestor, while those who defend skimming and summaries point to his permission to read "by deputy." The essay has become a kind of common property, which is perhaps the highest compliment an essay can earn. It is also worth noting that it has been repeatedly adapted for classroom use, set for translation exercises and used to teach rhetoric, since its parallel clauses are a gift to anyone teaching how sentences are built.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved "Of Studies" first for its density. In about five hundred words it manages to give a theory of knowledge, a lesson in reading, a map of the disciplines, and a promise about self-transformation. People who value economy see in it a model: nothing wasted, nothing apologized for. It is the opposite of padding, and that is a rare pleasure in any age.

They have loved it, too, for the gift of permission. Most people feel guilty about books they have not finished. Bacon says plainly that some books are only meant to be tasted, and that is not a failing but a skill. Readers who were taught that every book must be read cover to cover often find this liberating. At the same time, he sets a high bar for the "few" that deserve chewing, which flatters the serious reader. The essay is generous and demanding at once.

Writers have admired how it combines practical wisdom with a feel for the physical. The idea that books are food, that the mind is a body with weaknesses and exercises, makes abstract advice concrete. Teachers of writing often point to the sentence "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man" as a model of parallel structure and of an idea that can be tested in your own life. Anyone who has tried to explain something and discovered that they did not really understand it knows that writing makes you exact.

There is also a deeper appeal. The essay treats study as a moral activity, something that "passes into manners." That gives modest daily habits a large meaning. To read a little each evening is not just a pastime but a way of building the person you will be. Readers in every century who feel their lives are scattered and hurried have found comfort and direction in the idea that attention, steadily applied, adds up to character.

Finally, there is the sheer sound of it. Many readers say they love the music of Bacon's sentences: the balance, the stress on the last word, the confident rhythm of "tasted... swallowed... chewed and digested." Read aloud, which is how you may be hearing it now, the essay has a cadence that sticks in the ear. It is the kind of prose that wants to be spoken and remembered. That quality helps explain why it has been learned by heart by so many schoolchildren, and why adults who haven't thought of it for decades can still recite its best lines. People rarely forget a piece of writing that taught them how to eat a book.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Objections to "Of Studies" tend to cluster around three things: its coldness, its narrowness, and the gap between the author and his advice.

The first is tone. Many readers, even admirers, find Bacon chilly. Where Montaigne invites you into his tower and tells you his fears, Bacon issues rulings. Later critics have noted that his essays often seem written from outside the human heart, concerned with advantage, management and appearances rather than with feeling. In "Of Studies," even the pleasures of reading are quickly turned into uses. It has been a common complaint that the essays offer wisdom without warmth.

The second is narrowness. The essay's picture of study is practical and classically masculine: it imagines a man preparing for law, government and counsel. It has little to say about reading for consolation, for wonder, for grief or for fun. Modern readers, especially those who came to reading from outside the circles of power, sometimes find it limited. Virginia Woolf, in her own essay on how to read, took a much more open and playful view, and the contrast shows how much Bacon leaves out. His ranking of books, with a "meaner sort" that may be read by deputy, has also struck some as snobbish.

Third, there is the old charge about the man himself. Bacon was convicted of corruption in 1621, after admitting he had accepted gifts from litigants, though he maintained that they had not influenced his judgments. He lost his office and was fined and briefly imprisoned. Critics have long delighted in the irony that the author of so much advice on wisdom and judgment fell so far through a lapse of both. Alexander Pope's famous line calling him "the wisest, brightest, meanest of mankind" captures this view, though historians today tend to see the picture as more complicated, since gift-taking was widespread in the legal system of his day.

There are also intellectual quarrels. Some scholars point out that Bacon's neat sorting of subjects into effects, such as mathematics making men "subtle," is more a rhetorical flourish than a claim anyone could test, which is ironic for a champion of experiment. And a few critics have argued that his model of reading as pruning or digestion makes books too passive, as if they were raw material and the reader the only active force. Readers who think of reading as an encounter or conversation may find that the metaphors flatten what actually happens when a mind meets a text. Still, it is notable that most of these complaints are about what the essay leaves out, rather than about whether what it says is true.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"Of Studies" sits at the meeting point of three traditions. The first is classical moral philosophy. Bacon's talk of character formed by habit and study echoes Roman writers like Seneca and Cicero, and the Latin phrase "Abeunt studia in mores" is itself borrowed from the Roman poet Ovid. The second is the Renaissance humanist belief that education makes a person fit for public life. And the third is the new, restless, practical spirit that would become modern science. Bacon holds all three together, which is why the essay feels both ancient and strangely modern.

Within the history of the essay, it descends from Montaigne but argues with him. Montaigne essayed, meaning he tried and tested himself, and his writing meanders. Bacon takes the new form and sharpens it into a weapon. Together they define the two poles of the genre. If you follow the line forward you find Samuel Johnson's moral compression, Emerson's thunderclap sentences, and the pithy self-help essays of the modern era on one side, and Lamb, Hazlitt, Woolf and the confessional essayists on the other.

Comparing it with other essays on reading is illuminating. Montaigne's "Of Idleness" is about what a mind does when it is not occupied and why he began to write. Virginia Woolf's "How Should One Read a Book?" treats reading as a freedom and an act of imagination, and explicitly resists the authority that Bacon's confident rules might seem to carry. William James on habit supplies the psychology that explains why Bacon's claim that studies pass into character might be true. Reading Bacon next to these is like hearing the same question answered in four different voices.

His descendants outside the essay include the entire modern genre of advice writing, from Benjamin Franklin's maxims to contemporary productivity books, and the empirical tradition in philosophy and science that leads through the Royal Society to the present. Franklin in particular, with his tidy rules for self-improvement, writes in a recognizably Baconian register.

Why does it still matter? Partly because the problem he addressed is back, in a new form. Bacon wrote when printing had made books abundant, and readers felt overwhelmed. Today the flood is digital and far larger. His advice to choose, to read with a purpose, to weigh instead of swallow or merely argue, has become more useful, not less. And partly because it is simply a pleasure: a five-minute piece that rewards slow reading, in which almost every sentence can be taken out and held up to the light. It also reminds us that a good argument does not need to be long. If you read only one essay a night, this is one that gives you more each time you return to it.
