Nightly Short Story • Modernist Fiction

Cat in the Rain

Ernest Hemingway (1925) • 6 min read

“I’m going down and get that kitty,” the American wife said.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Cat in the Rain is a short, quiet story by Ernest Hemingway, first published in 1925 in his collection In Our Time. It is one of the clearest early examples of his method: very simple sentences, almost nothing that looks like an event, and a great deal of feeling pressed beneath the surface. An American couple is staying in an Italian seaside hotel on a rainy day. The wife sees a cat outside, wants to rescue it, and returns to a husband who is more interested in his book. Hemingway wrote it in his mid twenties, as a young American living in Europe after the First World War, married and soon to be a father, and trying to find a form for the unspoken disappointments of ordinary intimacy. Many readers take it as a story about a marriage in which one person's needs go unheard. Listen to how often the wife says what she wants, and to who is actually listening.

There were only two Americans stopping at the hotel. They did not know any of the people they passed on the stairs on their way to and from their room. Their room was on the second floor facing the sea. It also faced the public garden and the war monument. There were big palms and green benches in the public garden. In the good weather there was always an artist with his easel. Artists liked the way the palms grew and the bright colors of the hotels facing the gardens and the sea. Italians came from a long way off to look up at the war monument. It was made of bronze and glistened in the rain. It was raining. The rain dripped from the palm trees. Water stood in pools on the gravel paths. The sea broke in a long line in the rain and slipped back down the beach to come up and break again in a long line in the rain. The motor cars were gone from the square by the war monument. Across the square in the doorway of the café a waiter stood looking out at the empty square.

The American wife stood at the window looking out. Outside right under their window a cat was crouched under one of the dripping green tables. The cat was trying to make herself so compact that she would not be dripped on.

“I’m going down and get that kitty,” the American wife said.

“I’ll do it,” her husband offered from the bed.

“No, I’ll get it. The poor kitty out trying to keep dry under a table.”

The husband went on reading, lying propped up with the two pillows at the foot of the bed.

“Don’t get wet,” he said.

The wife went downstairs and the hotel owner stood up and bowed to her as she passed the office. His desk was at the far end of the office. He was an old man and very tall.

“Il piove,” the wife said. She liked the hotelkeeper.

“Si, si, Signora, brutto tempo. It is very bad weather.”

He stood behind his desk in the far end of the dim room. The wife liked him. She liked the deadly serious way he received any complaints. She liked his dignity. She liked the way he wanted to serve her. She liked the way he felt about being a hotelkeeper. She liked his old, heavy face and big hands.

Liking him she opened the door and looked out. It was raining harder. A man in a rubber cape was crossing the empty square to the café. The cat would be around to the right. Perhaps she could go along under the eaves. As she stood in the doorway an umbrella opened behind her. It was the maid who looked after their room.

“You must not get wet,” she smiled, speaking Italian. Of course, the hotelkeeper had sent her.

With the maid holding the umbrella over her, she walked along the gravel path until she was under their window. The table was there, washed bright green in the rain, but the cat was gone. She was suddenly disappointed. The maid looked up at her.

“Ha perduto qualque cosa, Signora?”

“There was a cat,” said the American girl.

“A cat?”

“Si, il gatto.”

“A cat?” the maid laughed. “A cat in the rain?”

“Yes,” she said, “under the table.” Then, “Oh, I wanted it so much. I wanted a kitty.”

When she talked English the maid’s face tightened.

“Come, Signora,” she said. “We must get back inside. You will be wet.”

“I suppose so,” said the American girl.

They went back along the gravel path and passed in the door. The maid stayed outside to close the umbrella. As the American girl passed the office, the padrone bowed from his desk. Something felt very small and tight inside the girl. The padrone made her feel very small and at the same time really important. She had a momentary feeling of being of supreme importance. She went on up the stairs. She opened the door of the room. George was on the bed, reading.

“Did you get the cat?” he asked, putting the book down.

“It was gone.”

“Wonder where it went to,” he said, resting his eyes from reading.

She sat down on the bed.

“I wanted it so much,” she said. “I don’t know why I wanted it so much. I wanted that poor kitty. It isn’t any fun to be a poor kitty out in the rain.”

George was reading again.

She went over and sat in front of the mirror of the dressing table looking at herself with the hand glass. She studied her profile, first one side and then the other. Then she studied the back of her head and her neck.

“Don’t you think it would be a good idea if I let my hair grow out?” she asked, looking at her profile again.

George looked up and saw the back of her neck, clipped close like a boy’s.

“I like it the way it is.”

“I get so tired of it,” she said. “I get so tired of looking like a boy.”

George shifted his position in the bed. He hadn’t looked away from her since she started to speak.

“You look pretty darn nice,” he said.

She laid the mirror down on the dresser and went over to the window and looked out. It was getting dark.

“I want to pull my hair back tight and smooth and make a big knot at the back that I can feel,” she said. “I want to have a kitty to sit on my lap and purr when I stroke her.”

“Yeah?” George said from the bed.

“And I want to eat at a table with my own silver and I want candles. And I want it to be spring and I want to brush my hair out in front of a mirror and I want a kitty and I want some new clothes.”

“Oh, shut up and get something to read,” George said. He was reading again.

His wife was looking out of the window. It was quite dark now and still raining in the palm trees.

“Anyway, I want a cat,” she said, “I want a cat. I want a cat now. If I can’t have long hair or any fun, I can have a cat.”

George was not listening. He was reading his book. His wife looked out of the window where the light had come on in the square.

Someone knocked at the door.

“Avanti,” George said. He looked up from his book.

In the doorway stood the maid. She held a big tortoiseshell cat pressed tight against her and swung down against her body.

“Excuse me,” she said, “the padrone asked me to bring this for the Signora.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The plot can be told in a breath. Two Americans are staying at a hotel in Italy. It is raining. From their window, the wife sees a cat crouched under a dripping green table in the garden, trying to make itself small enough not to get wet. She announces she will go down and get it. Her husband George, lying on the bed reading, says only "Don't get wet." She goes out, accompanied by an umbrella-bearing maid sent by the hotelkeeper, but the cat is gone. She goes back upstairs. And then, in front of a mirror, she begins to say what she really wants: long hair, a kitten, candles, her own silver, spring, new clothes. George tells her to shut up and get something to read. At the very end, there is a knock, and the maid arrives carrying a big tortoiseshell cat, sent by the padrone for the Signora.

The cat is the key, but it is not just a cat. It is a stand-in for everything the wife feels is missing. Think of what the animal is doing when we first see it: trying to make itself so compact that it will not be dripped on. That is a portrait of a creature making itself small to survive bad weather, and the wife, who is stuck in a hotel room with a husband who does not look up, is doing much the same. She says it plainly: "It isn't any fun to be a poor kitty out in the rain." She is talking about herself.

The second great image is her hair. She has it cropped short, like a boy's. She says she gets tired of looking like a boy, and wants to pull it back tight and smooth into a big knot she can feel. In the 1920s, bobbed hair was fashionable and a sign of modern freedom, but here it has become a sort of disguise or a loss. She wants to be a woman with a home, with a domestic, settled life, and the story suggests that she feels that life slipping away in the rootless world of hotels and travel. The husband's reply, "I like it the way it is," is not cruel, but it is entirely beside the point.

Then there is the padrone. The wife is touched by him. She likes his dignity, his seriousness, the way he takes her complaints. She likes the way he wants to serve her. When she passes his desk, she feels both small and important. He is the only person in the story who pays attention to her, and it is a stately, formal kind of attention. In the ending, his gift of the cat is a gesture of care, but it is ironic, because the thing she needed was not a cat. The story leaves us unsure whether she will be consoled, or whether the cat is a substitute that makes the loneliness plainer. The point is the gap between a want and the person who ought to answer it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The first thing to admire in Cat in the Rain is how much it does with how little. It opens with a flat statement: "There were only two Americans stopping at the hotel." From there, Hemingway describes the setting in short sentences that seem to be doing nothing: the palms, the war monument glistening in the rain, the empty square, the café. But the setting is precisely chosen. The war monument, in a seaside town in the shadow of recent war, hints at a world that has lost something. The empty square and the rain create isolation. The detail that the Americans do not know anyone on the stairs tells us they are cut off. The outer world mirrors the inner one.

The most skillful part is the handling of point of view. The narrator does not tell us what the wife feels; instead the story calls her, at different moments, the American wife, the American girl, and the wife. That shifting label is subtle. It shows how she is seen, as a function in a marriage, as a young person, as a foreign visitor, and it makes her feel unnamed, almost anonymous. The story is also careful with repetition. The word "like" echoes through the passage on the hotelkeeper, and "I want" echoes at the climax. The sheer repetition produces a kind of pressure that builds without a single raised voice.

Now the weaknesses. The story is, frankly, slight. It can feel like a sketch. Because Hemingway is so reticent, we learn almost nothing about George beyond his reading and his minimal replies. Is he cruel, tired, absorbed, or simply oblivious? The story does not say, and some readers find him a too-convenient cardboard figure, a stock inattentive husband. The wife, meanwhile, is a collection of wants that are easy to caricature: a kitten, long hair, silver, candles. A hostile reader might see her as petulant, and the story arguably does not give her enough interior depth to resist that reading.

The ending is also debatable. The maid's arrival with the cat is a clever turn, and many critics find it ambiguous in a productive way. But it can also feel a little contrived, an irony delivered a bit too neatly, and one wonders if the story gains or loses from its tidy final image. And the symbolism, with the cat as longing, the hair as lost femininity, and the rain as gloom, is clear enough that some readers feel the story is making its points too deliberately for something that pretends to be so offhand.

Still, the achievement is real. There is something remarkable about a story in which a woman looks out a window and says she wants a cat, and the reader's heart sinks. That is the accomplishment: a vast ache created from the barest materials.

III. The Impact It Had

Cat in the Rain came out in the United States in October 1925, in In Our Time, published by Boni and Liveright. That book, combining stories with very short prose sketches, was Hemingway's first major work in America and the one that announced him. It was widely noticed by critics as a new voice, and it helped bring him to the attention of his future publisher, Scribner's, and the editor Maxwell Perkins. Cat in the Rain is one of the quietest stories in that book, sitting among tales of war, hunting, and the Michigan woods, and it shows the same style turned on domestic life.

The story is often read in connection with Hemingway's own life. He and his first wife, Hadley, spent time in Rapallo, on the Italian coast, in early 1923, and later biographers have linked the story to that period and to tensions in their marriage, including questions about children and about the cut of Hadley's hair. The links are plausible and have been argued over, but the story does not require them, and critics caution against reducing it to biography. What is clear is that Hemingway was already thinking about marriage as a place where two people can be physically close and emotionally far apart.

The larger influence of the story lies in how it demonstrated that a short story could be built on a nearly invisible event. It became a standard teaching text, widely anthologized and used to introduce students to subtext, point of view, symbolism, and Hemingway's theory of omission. It is also a favorite example in discussions of gender and domestic life in modernist fiction, since it can be read as a portrait of a woman's frustrated desire for agency, recognition, and a home.

Later writers who wrote about quiet failures of intimacy, from Raymond Carver and his American domestic realists to Alice Munro and Lorrie Moore, work within a space that Hemingway helped open. The story has been adapted on a smaller scale than some of his work, but it is a frequent choice for short films, stage readings, and classroom performances.

In his own career, the story marks a stage in which Hemingway's reputation as a writer of men's adventures was balanced by his skill as an observer of intimate emotional life. That aspect is sometimes underplayed in the public image of the hunter and soldier, and this story is one of the best correctives.

IV. What Readers Loved

The usual first response to Cat in the Rain is an odd kind of ache. Readers say they finish it and feel they have witnessed something sad but are not sure what. That ambiguity is what many love about it. It sets a small human scene in front of you and trusts you to feel its edges.

Many readers love the wife, even though she is thinly sketched. She is observant and tender, and her little bursts of longing, the way she looks at herself in the mirror and studies her profile and the back of her neck, are completely recognizable. The line "I want a cat. I want a cat now," has a childlike honesty that goes straight to the heart. It is a moment of rawness that cuts through the story's careful reserve. Readers who have felt unheard in a relationship, or who have wanted something small and been told to be quiet, find the scene piercing.

They also love the ending for its quiet irony. The cat arrives, delivered by a servant at the request of a man who has barely spoken to the wife, while the husband who should have noticed is deaf to her. People respond to the contrast. A stranger, an old hotelkeeper with a heavy face, has understood what the husband could not. The story's gentleness toward the padrone is part of its warmth.

Fellow writers admire the story's technical control and its calm. The descriptions are clean, the sentences are short, and the story ends without commentary. Teachers love it because every detail can be discussed: why is the cat under a green table, why does the maid's face tighten when the wife speaks English, why is the monument glistening, why is the wife called by several names. Readers often say they notice new things on each rereading.

There is also something to be said for how contemporary it feels. The story does not belong to a distant period; its situation, of a person who wants more attention than she is getting, in a relationship where each is in a different mood and a different room, is timeless. And the small, specific longings, a kitten, a candle, spring, are the kind that make the reader see the character as an individual rather than a type. The story's power lies in the fact that no one in it is a villain, and yet someone is clearly hurting.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Cat in the Rain has not been a controversial story in the sense of provoking scandal, but it has provoked a lasting debate about how to read it, and it has drawn some pointed criticisms.

The central question is about the wife, and critics have long disagreed. Some read her as a sympathetic figure, a woman who feels trapped and unheard, and see George as a symbol of male indifference. Others find her petulant, a woman who confuses a series of whims with deep need, and see the story as an ironic portrait of dissatisfaction that cannot be satisfied. Still others emphasize the possibility that the story is about a deeper unspoken matter, such as the question of whether the couple will have a child, or a dissatisfaction rooted in the couple's rootless life. The fact that all these readings are available is a mark of the story's openness, but it is also, for some critics, a mark of its vagueness. They argue that the story withholds so much that it invites any interpretation, and that it is not clear it has a definite achievement of its own.

A second reservation concerns gender. Feminist critics have noted that the story gives its female character no resources except desire, and that its treatment of her wanting, with its repeated list of things she feels deprived of, can be read as faintly condescending, as if a woman's longing were charming but trivial. Others have defended the story as a sympathetic account of a woman's invisibility within a marriage, and still others see the author as quietly criticizing the husband. The disagreement continues.

There are also criticisms of its slightness. Some readers feel that in this story the Hemingway technique tips into something almost empty, that nothing is at stake beyond a mood, and that the symbolism is the only thing holding it together. The cat and the hair, they say, are too tidy as signs. Compared with the force of his war and violent stories, this one can seem a minor exercise.

Finally, the ending has been questioned. Some readers find the arrival of the cat a sentimental softening, an unexpected kindness that sits oddly with the story's coolness. Others say it is the opposite, a devastating irony. The dispute shows that readers do not agree about the story's final tone.

It is worth saying that much of this criticism is a matter of taste and interpretation, not of dismissal. The story is broadly respected, and the debates have, if anything, kept it alive.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Cat in the Rain belongs to the modernist short story of the early twentieth century, the form shaped by Chekhov, Joyce, and Katherine Mansfield, in which a small incident or a moment of mood replaces the plot with its twists. Mansfield's stories of women's unspoken longings, such as Bliss and The Garden Party, are close relatives, as is Joyce's Eveline, in which a young woman stands at a crossroads and cannot act. What Hemingway adds is the extreme bareness of his surface and the refusal even to hint at inner life.

It also has a place in the long literary tradition of the marriage story, from Chekhov's tales of tired marriages to Ibsen's A Doll's House, in which a wife's needs are overlooked until she insists on being heard. Ibsen's heroine leaves, while Hemingway's wife only asks for a cat. The comparison shows the difference between the older, openly dramatic treatment and the new understatement. In the world of the story, the explosion has been replaced by a quiet request, and the request is not met in kind.

Within Hemingway's own work, the story shows a side of him often overshadowed by the war and hunting material: the observer of couples who sit in the same room and cannot say what matters. A woman asks, in her own way, to be heard, and a man with the best intentions replies in a way that misses her.

Looking forward, its descendants include the domestic minimalism of Raymond Carver, the quiet, observant fiction of Alice Munro, William Trevor, and others, and many contemporary stories that locate a whole emotional drama in a gesture or an object. The fundamental technique, in which an ordinary desire stands for a deep, unnamed loss, is now so common that it can be hard to remember someone had to invent it.

Why read it now? Because it is short, because it rewards rereading, and because it captures a feeling that is hard to name and easy to recognize: wanting something small and being unable to explain that it is not small. In a few paragraphs, it shows how a person can be lonely in a room with someone she loves, and how a stranger's small kindness can both comfort and wound. The next time it rains and someone near you is looking out the window, you may find yourself wondering what they want.
