Nightly Poem • War & Witness Poetry

Dulce Et Decorum Est

Wilfred Owen (1920) • 4 min read

Through a green sea of chlorine gas on the Western Front, a young infantry officer shatters two thousand years of martial propaganda.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Dulce et Decorum Est is probably the most famous poem ever written about war. Its author, Wilfred Owen, was a young English officer in the First World War. He wrote it in late 1917 while recovering from shell shock at a military hospital near Edinburgh, where he met the older poet Siegfried Sassoon, who encouraged his writing. Owen went back to the front in 1918 and was killed in action on the fourth of November, just a week before the war ended. The poem was not published until 1920, two years after his death. The title is a line from the Roman poet Horace meaning that it is sweet and fitting to die for your country, a saying that British schoolboys of the time absorbed as a noble truth. Owen wrote the poem to take that saying apart. It describes a gas attack, one man's death, and the nightmares that follow, and it ends by calling the old saying a lie. It changed how English speakers think about war.

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,

Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,

Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,

And towards our distant rest began to trudge.

Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,

But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame, all blind;

Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots

Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.

Gas! Gas! Quick, boys!—An ecstasy of fumbling

Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,

But someone still was yelling out and stumbling

And flound’ring like a man in fire or lime.—

Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,

As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

In all my dreams before my helpless sight

He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.

If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace

Behind the wagon that we flung him in,

And watch the white eyes writhing in his face,

His hanging face, like a devil’s sick of sin,

If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood

Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs

Obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud

Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues—

My friend, you would not tell with such high zest

To children ardent for some desperate glory,

The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est

Pro patria mori.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a story in four movements, and it helps to take them one at a time.

The first stanza shows a column of exhausted soldiers retreating from the front line. Owen strips away every heroic picture. The men are bent double like old beggars under sacks, knock-kneed, coughing like hags, cursing as they wade through sludge. They march asleep. Many have lost their boots and limp on, blood-shod. They are drunk with fatigue and deaf to the hoots of gas shells dropping softly behind them. Notice how young men have been made old and sick by the first few lines. The word softly is chilling, because the thing that can kill them makes almost no noise.

The second stanza explodes into action. Gas! Gas! Quick, boys! The men fumble desperately for their clumsy helmets, which were the early gas masks, and get them on just in time. But one man does not, and he is yelling and stumbling, floundering like a man in fire or lime. Quicklime, a caustic chemical, burns skin terribly, and the comparison tells you how he is suffering. Owen watches through the misty panes of his own mask, in a thick green light, as if under a green sea, and sees the man drowning. That image is brilliant, because gas victims did die as though they were drowning, their lungs filling with fluid, and the green glass turns the whole battlefield into an undersea nightmare.

A short stanza follows, only two lines. In all my dreams, Owen says, the man still plunges at him, guttering, choking, drowning. This is the poem's turning point. The scene is not over. It repeats nightly in the speaker's sleep. The word guttering, like a candle going out, is awful and exact.

The last stanza turns to us. If you could walk behind the wagon where they flung him, hear the blood gargling from his ruined lungs at every jolt, and see his face like a devil's sick of sin, then you would not repeat the old Lie to children eager for some desperate glory. The old Lie is the Latin line quoted at the end: Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori. It is sweet and right to die for your country.

So what is the poem getting at? Its meaning is simple and angry. Patriotic slogans that make death sound glorious are false, and people who repeat them without having seen what Owen saw are, however unknowingly, passing on a lie that kills the young. The poem is built to make you witness what the slogan hides.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged as craft, Dulce et Decorum Est is a very powerful poem, and its power comes from a particular, controlled method. It is also not perfect, and a fair assessment has to say so.

The strengths first. The most impressive thing is how the poem moves from the general to the specific and then to the personal. We begin with a column of men, then a single man, then a single face. By the end the horror has been narrowed to one pair of white eyes writhing in a hanging face, and a single stream of blood gargling from a damaged lung. That narrowing is deliberate and effective. Owen understood that people can read about thousands of deaths without being moved, and that one death, described closely, can do much more.

The verbal texture is superb. Phrases such as blood-shod, drunk with fatigue, and froth-corrupted lungs are fresh and exact. The comparison of the gas victim's face to a devil sick of sin is startling, partly because it makes evil look pathetic. The simile obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues is deliberately disgusting. It is the poem's boldest stroke and its riskiest, and it works because it refuses to look away. The sound is harsh when it needs to be, full of hard consonants, and it softens on the word softly where it chills.

The form is also cleverly used. The rhymes are regular, in alternating pattern, which gives the poem a marching rhythm even as the content rebels against marching. The conventional form is a kind of irony. It makes the poem sound like the formal verse that patriots wrote, then fills it with the opposite message.

Now the weaknesses. The last stanza lectures. After the extraordinary evidence of the earlier lines, Owen addresses a my friend, a stand-in for the patriotic reader, and delivers a moral. Some readers feel that the poem would be even stronger if it simply ended on the image of the dying man. The capitalized Lie is also a little blunt, and the phrase high zest and children ardent for some desperate glory can sound slightly pointed and bitter, as if the target were being set up rather than argued with.

The shape of the poem is slightly lopsided, too. The short two-line stanza, a break in the pattern, can feel awkward on the page, though it also serves as a pause for horror. And the similes in the final passage pile up so densely that they risk overstating the case. A critic might say that Owen is working so hard to shock that he sometimes pushes past what is needed.

Still, these are the flaws of a poem that cares enormously about its subject. The method is almost always right.

III. The Impact It Had

The impact of Dulce et Decorum Est is difficult to overstate, because it changed the way an entire language talks about war.

Owen himself was almost unknown when he died. Only a handful of his poems had appeared during his lifetime. After his death in November 1918, Sassoon and others worked to bring his poems into print, and a collection came out in 1920, with a preface drawn from Owen's own notes. That preface contains his famous statement that his subject is War, and the pity of War, and that the poetry is in the pity. Later, in 1931, the poet Edmund Blunden produced a fuller edition that helped establish Owen's reputation with a wider audience.

From there the poem's influence spread slowly and then enormously. By the middle of the twentieth century it had become the central example of what people meant by war poetry. In Britain it is taught to teenagers as a standard part of the school curriculum, and for generations of students it has been their first encounter with the reality behind the glorious phrases of recruitment posters. It shaped what many people think the First World War was like: mud, gas, waste and betrayal by old men who sent the young to die.

Its effect on other writers has been profound. Poets of later wars, from the Second World War to Vietnam and Iraq, have written under its shadow, and the idea that a soldier-poet should testify truthfully rather than celebrate has become close to a rule. Owen's use of half-rhyme in his more experimental poems also influenced later poets. In music, Benjamin Britten made Owen central to his War Requiem, first performed in 1962 for the consecration of the rebuilt Coventry Cathedral, setting Owen's poems alongside the Latin Mass for the dead.

The poem has also entered politics and public life. Its title and its final lines are quoted in debates over war and remembrance, in protest speeches and in newspaper pieces. The phrase the old Lie has become shorthand for propaganda about glorious death. Whenever a government or newspaper dresses up a conflict in noble language, someone is likely to cite Owen.

It has also been the subject of music, film and art, and it is read at Remembrance services, sometimes alongside poems that take a more consoling view. That alone shows how it has become part of the culture's way of mourning.

IV. What Readers Loved

People have loved Dulce et Decorum Est for different reasons at different times, but one reason runs through them all: it feels true.

Many early admirers were fellow soldiers and veterans. Siegfried Sassoon, who knew Owen at Craiglockhart and helped shape his work, was one of the first readers to recognize how strong his poetry was, and for many who had served, the poem put into words what they had seen and been told not to describe. Veterans of later wars have said much the same. The poem's refusal to make the dying man heroic or noble, and its insistence that what happened was ugly, struck survivors as an honest account.

Ordinary readers love its force. The gas attack is written so vividly that people often say they can see the green light and hear the choking. Teachers report that students who find poetry boring are often gripped by this one, because it is a story with a clear emotional shape and a direct accusation. It does not require special knowledge, and it does not hide behind difficult language. A reader can feel its anger on a first reading.

Many also admire the poem's moral courage. Owen was not a pacifist at a distance. He was a serving officer who returned to fight, and who won the Military Cross for bravery in 1918. He wrote of what he knew from inside the experience, and he wrote out of care for the men under his command, which gives his protest a credibility that a safe observer's would lack. The idea that he died one week before the Armistice lends the poem a terrible poignancy that readers have never forgotten, even if the poem does not depend on it.

Writers and critics have also praised his craftsmanship. The poem is carefully built, rich in sound and vivid in simile, and shows how a regular, almost old-fashioned form can carry radical content. Admirers point out that the tension between the neat rhymes and the horror underneath is part of its quality.

Finally, many love the way the poem addresses the reader. By the end it is almost impossible to avoid the feeling that the my friend is you. That sense of being personally challenged to look at the truth, rather than pass along an easy phrase, is among the reasons it has stayed in so many memories.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a poem this admired has attracted real criticism, and some of it is worth taking seriously.

The first objection concerns the poem's address. According to Owen's drafts, an early version was dedicated to Jessie Pope, a popular writer who produced cheerful, patriotic verse urging young men to enlist. The final version drops her name and speaks to my friend, but the target is still a certain type of home-front cheerleader. Some historians and critics have argued that this is slightly unfair to Pope, who was one of many writers working in an accepted style of the time and who was writing before the full horrors of the war were widely known. Others reply that the poem is aimed at a general attitude, not at one person, which is why it has outlived her name.

A second complaint is that the poem is too didactic. Critics who prefer indirection in poetry feel that the final stanza hammers its moral too hard, and that Owen is telling us what to think instead of trusting the imagery. This was a minor worry in Owen's own day, but later critics have raised it. The exaggerated language of the closing similes can also look to some readers like overwriting.

A third and more famous objection came from W. B. Yeats. When Yeats edited the Oxford Book of Modern Verse in 1936, he left Owen out, and in a letter he dismissed war poetry in sharply disapproving terms, saying that passive suffering was not a proper subject for poetry. Many later critics have thought this verdict wrongheaded, but the episode shows that not every major poet of the time agreed that Owen's approach was the right one.

A fourth line of criticism comes from historians of the First World War. Some, such as Dan Todman, have argued that the poetry of Owen and his contemporaries has come to dominate popular memory so completely that it distorts our picture of the war, making it seem like a pointless slaughter that every soldier recognized as such at the time. Many soldiers believed in the cause, and many felt differently from Owen. These historians are not attacking the poem itself, but the way it has been used to stand for a whole war.

There are also scholars who note that Owen's anger is selective: the poem says little about the enemy, about political causes or about why the war was fought. As a protest it is moral and emotional rather than analytical, and some critics think that limits it.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Dulce et Decorum Est belongs to the poetry of the First World War, often called trench poetry, and within it to the strand sometimes labeled trench realism, which insisted on describing what soldiers actually saw. It stands against an earlier tradition of patriotic war verse. At the start of the war, poets such as Rupert Brooke wrote noble sonnets about dying for England, and Owen's poem is in part a direct answer to that mood. Brooke and Owen are often set side by side to show how fast illusions collapsed between 1914 and 1918.

The poem's title shows its deeper ancestry. Horace, the Roman poet, wrote the line in his Odes, and a long line of ancient and modern writers have repeated its message. Owen's poem takes an ancient classical ideal, the glory of dying for your country, and tests it against modern industrial war. In that respect it joins a long tradition of anti-heroic writing stretching back to Homer's Iliad, which also looks hard at the cost of battle even while honoring its heroes.

Among Owen's contemporaries, Sassoon is the closest parallel. Sassoon wrote satirical, angry verses about the war, and made a public protest in 1917 against its continuation. Owen's poems are generally more compassionate and more musical. Isaac Rosenberg, another soldier-poet killed in 1918, wrote in a more compressed and difficult style. Together the three give a sense of how varied the response of those poets was.

Looking ahead, the poem is a main ancestor of twentieth-century war writing in many languages. It lies behind poets like Keith Douglas and Randall Jarrell in the Second World War, and behind Vietnam-era and later poets who also wrote testimony from the field. In prose, it parallels works such as Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front, which tells the same story of disillusion in the novel form, and the later generation of war memoirs.

Why does it still matter? Because the pull of heroic language has not gone away. Every generation hears stories that make killing and dying sound noble. Owen's poem is a permanent reminder that such stories should be tested against the actual body. It is one of the few poems that can still change how you hear a slogan. At twenty-eight lines it takes only a few minutes to read, and it is not easily forgotten.
