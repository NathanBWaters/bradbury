The Fear of the Past

G. K. Chesterton (1910) • 14 min read

We have made our eyes all focused on things that have not yet happened; and we have forgotten the forgotten revolutions that made us free.

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Fear of the Past is a chapter from What's Wrong with the World, a book G. K. Chesterton published in 1910. It was a time of excitement about the future. H. G. Wells was writing scientific romances about what was coming, reformers promised progress, and European powers were racing to build bigger warships. Chesterton, a journalist and a fierce, cheerful critic of fashion, argued that all this future worship was really a kind of cowardice. People flee toward tomorrow, he says, because they are afraid of the past, with its towering heroes, unfinished ideals, and failed attempts that make us feel small. He points out that nearly every great revolution looked backward for its inspiration. His rallying cry is that we have the freedom to restore as well as to revolt, and that you really can put the clock back. As you listen, notice how he turns a slogan into a joke, and a joke into an argument.

The last few decades have been marked by a special cultivation of the romance of the future. We seem to have made up our minds to misunderstand what has happened; and we turn, with a sort of relief, to stating what will happen⁠—which is (apparently) much easier. The modern man no longer presents the memoirs of his great-grandfather; but is engaged in writing a detailed and authoritative biography of his great-grandson. Instead of trembling before the spectres of the dead, we shudder abjectly under the shadow of the babe unborn. This spirit is apparent everywhere, even to the creation of a form of futurist romance. Sir Walter Scott stands at the dawn of the nineteenth century for the novel of the past; Mr. H. G. Wells stands at the dawn of the twentieth century for the novel of the future. The old story, we know, was supposed to begin: “Late on a winter’s evening two horsemen might have been seen⁠—.” The new story has to begin: “Late on a winter’s evening two aviators will be seen⁠—.” The movement is not without its elements of charm; there is something spirited, if eccentric, in the sight of so many people fighting over again the fights that have not yet happened; of people still glowing with the memory of tomorrow morning. A man in advance of the age is a familiar phrase enough. An age in advance of the age is really rather odd.

But when full allowance has been made for this harmless element of poetry and pretty human perversity in the thing, I shall not hesitate to maintain here that this cult of the future is not only a weakness but a cowardice of the age. It is the peculiar evil of this epoch that even its pugnacity is fundamentally frightened; and the Jingo is contemptible not because he is impudent, but because he is timid. The reason why modern armaments do not inflame the imagination like the arms and emblazonments of the Crusades is a reason quite apart from optical ugliness or beauty. Some battleships are as beautiful as the sea; and many Norman nosepieces were as ugly as Norman noses. The atmospheric ugliness that surrounds our scientific war is an emanation from that evil panic which is at the heart of it. The charge of the Crusades was a charge; it was charging towards God, the wild consolation of the brave. The charge of the modern armaments is not a charge at all. It is a rout, a retreat, a flight from the devil, who will catch the hindmost. It is impossible to imagine a medieval knight talking of longer and longer French lances, with precisely the quivering employed about larger and larger German ships. The man who called the Blue Water School the “Blue Funk School” uttered a psychological truth which that school itself would scarcely essentially deny. Even the two-power standard, if it be a necessity, is in a sense a degrading necessity. Nothing has more alienated many magnanimous minds from Imperial enterprises than the fact that they are always exhibited as stealthy or sudden defenses against a world of cold rapacity and fear. The Boer War, for instance, was coloured not so much by the creed that we were doing something right, as by the creed that Boers and Germans were probably doing something wrong; driving us (as it was said) to the sea. Mr. Chamberlain, I think, said that the war was a feather in his cap and so it was: a white feather.

Now this same primary panic that I feel in our rush towards patriotic armaments I feel also in our rush towards future visions of society. The modern mind is forced towards the future by a certain sense of fatigue, not unmixed with terror, with which it regards the past. It is propelled towards the coming time; it is, in the exact words of the popular phrase, knocked into the middle of next week. And the goad which drives it on thus eagerly is not an affectation for futurity. Futurity does not exist, because it is still future. Rather it is a fear of the past; a fear not merely of the evil in the past, but of the good in the past also. The brain breaks down under the unbearable virtue of mankind. There have been so many flaming faiths that we cannot hold; so many harsh heroisms that we cannot imitate; so many great efforts of monumental building or of military glory which seem to us at once sublime and pathetic. The future is a refuge from the fierce competition of our forefathers. The older generation, not the younger, is knocking at our door. It is agreeable to escape, as Henley said, into the Street of By-and-By, where stands the Hostelry of Never. It is pleasant to play with children, especially unborn children. The future is a blank wall on which every man can write his own name as large as he likes; the past I find already covered with illegible scribbles, such as Plato, Isaiah, Shakespeare, Michelangelo, Napoleon. I can make the future as narrow as myself; the past is obliged to be as broad and turbulent as humanity. And the upshot of this modern attitude is really this: that men invent new ideals because they dare not attempt old ideals. They look forward with enthusiasm, because they are afraid to look back.

Now in history there is no Revolution that is not a Restoration. Among the many things that leave me doubtful about the modern habit of fixing eyes on the future, none is stronger than this: that all the men in history who have really done anything with the future have had their eyes fixed upon the past. I need not mention the Renaissance, the very word proves my case. The originality of Michelangelo and Shakespeare began with the digging up of old vases and manuscripts. The mildness of poets absolutely arose out of the mildness of antiquaries. So the great medieval revival was a memory of the Roman Empire. So the Reformation looked back to the Bible and Bible times. So the modern Catholic movement has looked back to patristic times. But that modern movement which many would count the most anarchic of all is in this sense the most conservative of all. Never was the past more venerated by men than it was by the French Revolutionists. They invoked the little republics of antiquity with the complete confidence of one who invokes the gods. The Sansculottes believed (as their name might imply) in a return to simplicity. They believed most piously in a remote past; some might call it a mythical past. For some strange reason man must always thus plant his fruit trees in a graveyard. Man can only find life among the dead. Man is a misshapen monster, with his feet set forward and his face turned back. He can make the future luxuriant and gigantic, so long as he is thinking about the past. When he tries to think about the future itself, his mind diminishes to a pin point with imbecility, which some call Nirvana. Tomorrow is the Gorgon; a man must only see it mirrored in the shining shield of yesterday. If he sees it directly he is turned to stone. This has been the fate of all those who have really seen fate and futurity as clear and inevitable. The Calvinists, with their perfect creed of predestination, were turned to stone. The modern sociological scientists (with their excruciating Eugenics) are turned to stone. The only difference is that the Puritans make dignified, and the Eugenists somewhat amusing, statues.

But there is one feature in the past which more than all the rest defies and depresses the moderns and drives them towards this featureless future. I mean the presence in the past of huge ideals, unfulfilled and sometimes abandoned. The sight of these splendid failures is melancholy to a restless and rather morbid generation; and they maintain a strange silence about them⁠—sometimes amounting to an unscrupulous silence. They keep them entirely out of their newspapers and almost entirely out of their history books. For example, they will often tell you (in their praises of the coming age) that we are moving on towards a United States of Europe. But they carefully omit to tell you that we are moving away from a United States of Europe; that such a thing existed literally in Roman and essentially in medieval times. They never admit that the international hatreds (which they call barbaric) are really very recent, the mere breakdown of the ideal of the Holy Roman Empire. Or again, they will tell you that there is going to be a social revolution, a great rising of the poor against the rich; but they never rub it in that France made that magnificent attempt unaided, and that we and all the world allowed it to be trampled out and forgotten. I say decisively that nothing is so marked in modern writing as the prediction of such ideals in the future combined with the ignoring of them in the past. Anyone can test this for himself. Read any thirty or forty pages of pamphlets advocating peace in Europe, and see how many of them praise the old Popes or Emperors for keeping the peace in Europe. Read any armful of essays and poems in praise of social democracy, and see how many of them praise the old Jacobins who created democracy and died for it. These colossal ruins are to the modern only enormous eyesores. He looks back along the valley of the past and sees a perspective of splendid but unfinished cities. They are unfinished, not always through enmity or accident, but often through fickleness, mental fatigue, and the lust for alien philosophies. We have not only left undone those things that we ought to have done; but we have even left undone those things that we wanted to do.

It is very currently suggested that the modern man is the heir of all the ages, that he has got the good out of these successive human experiments. I know not what to say in answer to this, except to ask the reader to look at the modern man, as I have just looked at the modern man⁠—in the looking-glass. Is it really true that you and I are two starry towers built up of all the most towering visions of the past? Have we really fulfilled all the great historic ideals one after the other, from our naked ancestor who was brave enough to kill a mammoth with a stone knife, through the Greek citizen and the Christian saint to our own grandfather or great-grandfather, who may have been sabred by the Manchester Yeomanry or shot in the ’48? Are we still strong enough to spear mammoths, but now tender enough to spare them? Does the cosmos contain any mammoth that we have either speared or spared? When we decline (in a marked manner) to fly the red flag and fire across a barricade like our grandfathers, are we really declining in deference to sociologists⁠—or to soldiers? Have we indeed outstripped the warrior and passed the ascetical saint? I fear we only outstrip the warrior in the sense that we should probably run away from him. And if we have passed the saint, I fear we have passed him without bowing.

This is, first and foremost, what I mean by the narrowness of the new ideas, the limiting effect of the future. Our modern prophetic idealism is narrow because it has undergone a persistent process of elimination. We must ask for new things because we are not allowed to ask for old things. The whole position is based on this idea that we have got all the good that can be got out of the ideas of the past. But we have not got all the good out of them, perhaps at this moment not any of the good out of them. And the need here is a need of complete freedom for restoration as well as revolution.

We often read nowadays of the valour or audacity with which some rebel attacks a hoary tyranny or an antiquated superstition. There is not really any courage at all in attacking hoary or antiquated things, any more than in offering to fight one’s grandmother. The really courageous man is he who defies tyrannies young as the morning and superstitions fresh as the first flowers. The only true freethinker is he whose intellect is as much free from the future as from the past. He cares as little for what will be as for what has been; he cares only for what ought to be. And for my present purpose I specially insist on this abstract independence. If I am to discuss what is wrong, one of the first things that are wrong is this: the deep and silent modern assumption that past things have become impossible. There is one metaphor of which the moderns are very fond; they are always saying, “You can’t put the clock back.” The simple and obvious answer is “You can.” A clock, being a piece of human construction, can be restored by the human finger to any figure or hour. In the same way society, being a piece of human construction, can be reconstructed upon any plan that has ever existed.

There is another proverb, “As you have made your bed, so you must lie on it”; which again is simply a lie. If I have made my bed uncomfortable, please God I will make it again. We could restore the Heptarchy or the stage coaches if we chose. It might take some time to do, and it might be very inadvisable to do it; but certainly it is not impossible as bringing back last Friday is impossible. This is, as I say, the first freedom that I claim: the freedom to restore. I claim a right to propose as a solution the old patriarchal system of a Highland clan, if that should seem to eliminate the largest number of evils. It certainly would eliminate some evils; for instance, the unnatural sense of obeying cold and harsh strangers, mere bureaucrats and policemen. I claim the right to propose the complete independence of the small Greek or Italian towns, a sovereign city of Brixton or Brompton, if that seems the best way out of our troubles. It would be a way out of some of our troubles; we could not have in a small state, for instance, those enormous illusions about men or measures which are nourished by the great national or international newspapers. You could not persuade a city state that Mr. Beit was an Englishman, or Mr. Dillon a desperado, any more than you could persuade a Hampshire Village that the village drunkard was a teetotaller or the village idiot a statesman. Nevertheless, I do not as a fact propose that the Browns and the Smiths should be collected under separate tartans. Nor do I even propose that Clapham should declare its independence. I merely declare my independence. I merely claim my choice of all the tools in the universe; and I shall not admit that any of them are blunted merely because they have been used.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Chesterton opens by describing a mood of his age. The last few decades, he says, have cultivated the romance of the future. Modern people have stopped writing the memoirs of their great-grandfathers and are busy writing authoritative biographies of their great-grandsons. He jokes that Walter Scott stood at the dawn of the nineteenth century for the novel of the past, and H. G. Wells stands at the dawn of the twentieth for the novel of the future. The old story began with two horsemen on a winter evening, the new one with two aviators who will be seen.

Then comes his bold charge: the cult of the future is not just a weakness but a cowardice. He ties this to the arms race. Modern armaments, he says, do not stir the imagination like the Crusades, because the Crusader charged toward God, while the modern battleship builder is in flight from the devil, in a state of panic. He quotes the nickname Blue Funk School for those who preached naval power, and the pun on a white feather for Chamberlain's Boer War. The point is that fear, not courage, drives the age even when it looks aggressive.

He then connects this to futurism. We run toward the future, he says, not because we love it but out of a certain sense of fatigue and terror with which we regard the past. It is not an affectation for futurity, since futurity does not exist; it is a fear of the past, the good as well as the evil in it. The brain breaks down under the unbearable virtue of mankind. So many flaming faiths we cannot hold, so many harsh heroisms we cannot imitate. The future is a blank wall on which anyone can write his own name as large as he likes. The past is already covered with scribbles like Plato, Isaiah, Shakespeare, Michelangelo, and Napoleon.

His second key claim is that great changes have always looked backward. There is no revolution that is not a restoration. The Renaissance, the medieval revival, the Reformation, and the French Revolution all appealed to some older ideal, whether Greek manuscripts, the Roman Empire, the Bible, or the little republics of antiquity. Man, he says, is a monster with his feet forward and his face turned back, and he plants his fruit trees in a graveyard. Tomorrow is the Gorgon, and we can only look at it mirrored in the shield of yesterday.

The last movement is a call for freedom. The moderns say you cannot put the clock back. Chesterton answers that you can, because a clock is a human construction and can be set to any hour. Society too can be rebuilt on any plan that has ever existed. He does not propose a return to Highland clans or Greek city states, but claims the right to choose among all the tools in the universe, since none is blunted merely by having been used. A reader might miss that this is not nostalgia but a plea for imaginative freedom, the right to take good ideas wherever they come from.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a compelling piece of polemic, built on one fine idea and many good jokes, though it also shows how Chesterton can be carried away by his own paradox. The strengths are clear. The central observation, that the future is a blank wall while the past is crowded with towering figures, is both witty and psychologically sharp. It names a real temptation: the future makes no demands on you, offers no rivals, and can be shaped to fit your own small imagination. The list of scribbles, Plato, Isaiah, Shakespeare, Michelangelo, Napoleon, is a lovely comic rhythm that also makes the point about intimidation.

The argument about revolutions is also effective. His observation that reformers constantly appeal to a remembered past, from the Renaissance to the Jacobins who revered Roman republics, is historically true and runs against the cliche that progress means leaving tradition behind. The image of the Gorgon, with its idea that looking at the future directly turns you to stone, and that Calvinists and eugenists were both petrified by certainty about fate, is memorable and pointed. The jab at eugenics is especially notable, since it came when that movement was fashionable among progressive intellectuals; Chesterton opposed it firmly and would later write a whole book against it.

The writing carries the reader. Sentences like the one about the aviators at the beginning, or the retort that you can indeed put the clock back, have the snap of a good stand-up routine. He pushes a single metaphor until it either gives or breaks, and mostly it gives.

But there are weaknesses. The most obvious is that the argument is one-sided. He is brilliant about the dangers of futurism and almost silent about the dangers of nostalgia. The past was also full of cruelty, poverty, and plague, and the modern mind's reluctance to restore it is not purely cowardice. A reader may feel that his claim that you can restore the Heptarchy or the stage coach is true in a trivial sense and misleading in a practical one. You can rebuild a clock, but societies are not clocks, and the conditions that made old arrangements work may be gone.

Second, his diagnosis of motive is a kind of mind reading. To say that people are drawn to the future out of fear is a claim he cannot prove, and he makes it with total confidence. The same method of psychological accusation that he criticizes in others appears here.

Third, his examples are selective. The modern world did not simply ignore Rome or the medieval order, and many futurists, including Wells, were deeply engaged with history. Still, as a provocation it works, and its best passages remain exhilarating.

III. The Impact It Had

What's Wrong with the World was one of Chesterton's most important books of the Edwardian years, and the chapter on the fear of the past contributed one of its most quotable slogans. The statement that there is no revolution that is not a restoration, and the defense of the right to restore, became part of the vocabulary of traditionalist and conservative thinkers who wanted to argue that looking backward was not the same as being reactionary.

The book as a whole helped lay the foundations of Distributism, the economic and social movement that Chesterton and Hilaire Belloc developed in the following decades. Distributism proposed that property should be widely spread among families rather than concentrated in the hands of the state or of large capitalists, and it appealed to medieval guilds and village life as inspiration. This chapter's argument, that we are free to choose any model from history, belongs to the intellectual background of that movement. Its principle that society is a human construction that can be rebuilt on any plan gave Distributists a philosophical license for their proposals.

The chapter also stands in the early history of the argument over progress. Chesterton's skepticism toward futurist prophecy anticipated later criticisms of technological utopianism and of the confidence of planners. He saw, earlier than many, that predicting the future often says more about the present than about what is to come. Writers like Aldous Huxley, in Brave New World, and George Orwell, in Nineteen Eighty-Four, took very different roads, but their suspicion of confident, planned futures has a distant kinship with his. Chesterton is not usually named as their source, but the cultural conversation he joined shaped theirs.

The line about the clock has become a common retort in political argument. When someone says you cannot turn back the clock, the answer that you can, because a clock is a human thing, still turns up in conservative and traditionalist writing. The phrase about the democracy of the dead, though it comes from a different book, belongs to the same strand of his thought: tradition gives a vote to the people who are not here.

In teaching, the chapter appears in anthologies of Chesterton and in courses on the history of British political thought, where it is used as an example of the Edwardian critique of progressivism. The book is also read by students of Catholic social thought, since Chesterton's ideas about property and family ran parallel to papal teaching of the period, though he wrote as a layman and arrived at his conclusions in his own way.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this chapter for the pleasure of watching a very fashionable idea get turned upside down. In 1910, the future was in vogue, and anyone who has felt tired of being told that tomorrow will be better will recognize the relief of hearing someone say that this might be a form of timidity. The joke that people are busy writing biographies of their great-grandsons is a classic Chestertonian inversion: brief, obvious once said, and impossible to unsee.

They also love the courage of the argument. The claim that the cult of the future is cowardice is deliberately provocative, and Chesterton delivers it with such good humor that even those who disagree enjoy the show. The comparison of the Crusader's charge, which was a charge toward God, with the modern arms race, which is a flight from the devil, is the kind of line that makes readers stop and think about the psychology of military panic. The pun about the white feather, the sneer at the Blue Funk School, and the portrait of the sociologist turned to stone by his own certainties give the chapter a lively, combative flavor.

There is also something inspiring in its central appeal. Chesterton says that the past is not a graveyard but a toolbox, and that we are free to take any tool from it. That message of liberty appeals to people who feel boxed in by the assumption that history only moves one way. Preservationists, traditionalists, craftspeople, and anyone who loves old things have found in the chapter permission to say that old ideas are not out of date merely because they are old.

Readers also love the humane sweep of the examples. The list of the past's greatness, Plato, Isaiah, Shakespeare, Michelangelo, is a reminder of how much human achievement we inherit, and the chapter's tone toward it is awe rather than resentment. The suggestion that modern people look at themselves in the mirror and ask whether they are really the heirs of all the ages is both funny and chastening.

Admirers such as C. S. Lewis, who made a related point about what he called chronological snobbery, and conservative thinkers who value tradition have returned to the passage often. It speaks to a lasting human feeling: that we are smaller than our ancestors in some respects, and that the honest response to this is neither despair nor denial but a willingness to learn from them. That is the note that keeps the chapter alive.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critics have raised several objections. The first is that the chapter is selective about history. Chesterton recalls the Roman Empire and the Holy Roman Empire as keepers of European peace, and the Jacobins as heroes of democracy, but he passes lightly over the violence, hierarchy, and suffering of those eras. The medieval order he admires, with its Popes and Emperors, was not so peaceful, and the Jacobin Terror is a complicated model. Historians point out that the past he praises is partly an imagined one, and that idealizing it can be as misleading as idealizing the future.

The second objection is about the practical meaning of putting the clock back. In a narrow logical sense, he is right that nothing forbids us from restoring an old institution. But critics argue that social arrangements depend on conditions such as population, technology, and belief that cannot simply be reversed. To say we could restore the Heptarchy or the stage coach if we chose is true but not very helpful, and he himself admits it might be inadvisable. Some readers feel that the argument works as a point about freedom of thought but not as guidance for action.

Third, progressive critics, including those he was arguing with, said that he misunderstood the future-minded. Wells and others did not hate the past; they believed that scientific and social change could solve problems the past had left unsolved, such as poverty and disease. Chesterton's claim that this is cowardice, rather than a different judgment about what is possible, is an accusation of motive rather than a reply to their arguments. In the decades after, the reforms that the futurists championed, including public health, education, and the expansion of rights, delivered real improvements that his rhetoric seems to underrate.

Fourth, his dismissal of eugenics, though admirable in hindsight, appears here as part of a larger tone of scorn toward modern social science, which some find unfair to the many researchers doing valuable work. Others note that his wider politics, with its romantic view of the family and of old social forms, could be used to defend inequalities he did not intend to defend.

Finally, a few have found his style too pleased with itself. The steady drumbeat of paradox can feel like a performance, and the fun of the wordplay sometimes substitutes for evidence. These criticisms do not erase the chapter's charm, but they explain why it is usually read as brilliant provocation rather than as a balanced account of history.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Chesterton's chapter belongs to a long tradition of conservative and traditionalist reflection that runs from Edmund Burke through Samuel Taylor Coleridge to Chesterton himself and beyond. Burke's idea that society is a partnership between the living, the dead, and those yet to be born is the deepest ancestor of the view that we owe the past a hearing. Chesterton's version is lighter and more playful, but it shares Burke's suspicion that a generation that cuts itself off from tradition impoverishes itself.

It also belongs to the Edwardian debate about progress, in which Chesterton, Belloc, and Shaw and Wells argued constantly, usually as friends. Wells represented the confident, scientific, forward-looking position, and many of Chesterton's best jokes in this chapter are aimed at him, however gently. Where Wells imagined machines and world states, Chesterton imagined villages, craft, and family. Their quarrel was one of the great public arguments of the early twentieth century, and it still frames debates about technology, planning, and tradition today.

The chapter's descendants are many. Nostalgia has become a major force in politics and culture, and the question of whether to look back or forward remains a dividing line. Later conservative thinkers, such as Michael Oakeshott, who wrote about the difference between rationalism and tradition in politics, developed related ideas in a more philosophical key. C. S. Lewis's warning against chronological snobbery, the assumption that whatever is newer is better, echoes Chesterton's point. Outside politics, movements to revive crafts, local food, and older ways of building may be seen as in part carrying out his claim that old tools are not blunted by use.

It is also instructive to compare the chapter with works that take the opposite view. Wells's A Modern Utopia and the futurist manifestos of Marinetti celebrated speed and novelty, while the Victorian Whig historians told the story of history as steady improvement. Chesterton is the mocking counterpoint to both.

Why read it now? We live in an age of constant prediction, from technology forecasts to political promises, and also in an age of intense nostalgia. Chesterton's chapter helps us notice both impulses, and ask whether each is a form of fear. Its most useful gift is a question: are you running toward something, or away from something? Whether or not you agree with his answers, the question, and the laughter along the way, are worth the few minutes it takes to listen.
