Hills Like White Elephants

Ernest Hemingway (1927) • 7 min read

“What should we drink?” the girl asked. She had taken off her hat and put it on the table.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Hills Like White Elephants was published in 1927, when Ernest Hemingway was in his late twenties and already known as the leading voice of a new, stripped-down American style. It is one of the most famous examples of what he called the iceberg idea: tell only the part above the water, and trust the bulk beneath to be felt. The story is almost entirely dialogue. An American man and a young woman wait at a Spanish railway junction for a train to Madrid, drink beer and anise liqueur, and talk about something that the story never names. The setting is the aftermath of the First World War, a time when many young people, Hemingway among them, felt cut loose from older rules about marriage, religion, and what a life was supposed to look like. As you listen, pay attention to what the two characters avoid saying, and to how differently each of them uses the word fine.

The hills across the valley of the Ebro were long and white. On this side there was no shade and no trees and the station was between two lines of rails in the sun. Close against the side of the station there was the warm shadow of the building and a curtain, made of strings of bamboo beads, hung across the open door into the bar, to keep out flies. The American and the girl with him sat at a table in the shade, outside the building. It was very hot and the express from Barcelona would come in forty minutes. It stopped at this junction for two minutes and went on to Madrid.

“What should we drink?” the girl asked. She had taken off her hat and put it on the table.

“It’s pretty hot,” the man said.

“Let’s drink beer.”

“Dos cervezas,” the man said into the curtain.

“Big ones?” a woman asked from the doorway.

“Yes. Two big ones.”

The woman brought two glasses of beer and two felt pads. She put the felt pads and the beer glasses on the table and looked at the man and the girl. The girl was looking off at the line of hills. They were white in the sun and the country was brown and dry.

“They look like white elephants,” she said.

“I’ve never seen one,” the man drank his beer.

“No, you wouldn’t have.”

“I might have,” the man said. “Just because you say I wouldn’t have doesn’t prove anything.”

The girl looked at the bead curtain. “They’ve painted something on it,” she said. “What does it say?”

“Anis del Toro. It’s a drink.”

“Could we try it?”

The man called “Listen” through the curtain. The woman came out from the bar.

“Four reales.”

“We want two Anis del Toro.”

“With water?”

“Do you want it with water?”

“I don’t know,” the girl said. “Is it good with water?”

“It’s all right.”

“You want them with water?” asked the woman.

“Yes, with water.”

“It tastes like licorice,” the girl said and put the glass down.

“That’s the way with everything.”

“Yes,” said the girl. “Everything tastes of licorice. Especially all the things you’ve waited so long for, like absinthe.”

“Oh, cut it out.”

“You started it,” the girl said. “I was being amused. I was having a fine time.”

“Well, let’s try and have a fine time.”

“All right. I was trying. I said the mountains looked like white elephants. Wasn’t that bright?”

“That was bright.”

“I wanted to try this new drink. That’s all we do, isn’t it⁠—look at things and try new drinks?”

“I guess so.”

The girl looked across at the hills.

“They’re lovely hills,” she said. “They don’t really look like white elephants. I just meant the coloring of their skin through the trees.”

“Should we have another drink?”

“All right.”

The warm wind blew the bead curtain against the table.

“The beer’s nice and cool,” the man said.

“It’s lovely,” the girl said.

“It’s really an awfully simple operation, Jig,” the man said. “It’s not really an operation at all.”

The girl looked at the ground the table legs rested on.

“I know you wouldn’t mind it, Jig. It’s really not anything. It’s just to let the air in.”

The girl did not say anything.

“I’ll go with you and I’ll stay with you all the time. They just let the air in and then it’s all perfectly natural.”

“Then what will we do afterward?”

“We’ll be fine afterward. Just like we were before.”

“What makes you think so?”

“That’s the only thing that bothers us. It’s the only thing that’s made us unhappy.”

The girl looked at the bead curtain, put her hand out and took hold of two of the strings of beads.

“And you think then we’ll be all right and be happy.”

“I know we will. You don’t have to be afraid. I’ve known lots of people that have done it.”

“So have I,” said the girl. “And afterward they were all so happy.”

“Well,” the man said, “if you don’t want to you don’t have to. I wouldn’t have you do it if you didn’t want to. But I know it’s perfectly simple.”

“And you really want to?”

“I think it’s the best thing to do. But I don’t want you to do it if you don’t really want to.”

“And if I do it you’ll be happy and things will be like they were and you’ll love me?”

“I love you now. You know I love you.”

“I know. But if I do it, then it will be nice again if I say things are like white elephants, and you’ll like it?”

“I’ll love it. I love it now but I just can’t think about it. You know how I get when I worry.”

“If I do it you won’t ever worry?”

“I won’t worry about that because it’s perfectly simple.”

“Then I’ll do it. Because I don’t care about me.”

“What do you mean?”

“I don’t care about me.”

“Well, I care about you.”

“Oh, yes. But I don’t care about me. And I’ll do it and then everything will be fine.”

“I don’t want you to do it if you feel that way.”

The girl stood up and walked to the end of the station. Across, on the other side, were fields of grain and trees along the banks of the Ebro. Far away, beyond the river, were mountains. The shadow of a cloud moved across the field of grain and she saw the river through the trees.

“And we could have all this,” she said. “And we could have everything and every day we make it more impossible.”

“What did you say?”

“I said we could have everything.”

“We can have everything.”

“No, we can’t.”

“We can have the whole world.”

“No, we can’t.”

“We can go everywhere.”

“No, we can’t. It isn’t ours any more.”

“It’s ours.”

“No, it isn’t. And once they take it away, you never get it back.”

“But they haven’t taken it away.”

“We’ll wait and see.”

“Come on back in the shade,” he said. “You mustn’t feel that way.”

“I don’t feel any way,” the girl said. “I just know things.”

“I don’t want you to do anything that you don’t want to do⁠—”

“Nor that isn’t good for me,” she said. “I know. Could we have another beer?”

“All right. But you’ve got to realize⁠—”

“I realize,” the girl said. “Can’t we maybe stop talking?”

They sat down at the table and the girl looked across at the hills on the dry side of the valley and the man looked at her and at the table.

“You’ve got to realize,” he said, “that I don’t want you to do it if you don’t want to. I’m perfectly willing to go through with it if it means anything to you.”

“Doesn’t it mean anything to you? We could get along.”

“Of course it does. But I don’t want anybody but you. I don’t want anyone else. And I know it’s perfectly simple.”

“Yes, you know it’s perfectly simple.”

“It’s all right for you to say that, but I do know it.”

“Would you do something for me now?”

“I’d do anything for you.”

“Would you please please please please please please please stop talking?”

He did not say anything but looked at the bags against the wall of the station. There were labels on them from all the hotels where they had spent nights.

“But I don’t want you to,” he said, “I don’t care anything about it.”

“I’ll scream,” the girl said.

The woman came out through the curtains with two glasses of beer and put them down on the damp felt pads. “The train comes in five minutes,” she said.

“What did she say?” asked the girl.

“That the train is coming in five minutes.”

The girl smiled brightly at the woman, to thank her.

“I’d better take the bags over to the other side of the station,” the man said. She smiled at him.

“All right. Then come back and we’ll finish the beer.”

He picked up the two heavy bags and carried them around the station to the other tracks. He looked up the tracks but could not see the train. Coming back, he walked through the barroom, where people waiting for the train were drinking. He drank an Anis at the bar and looked at the people. They were all waiting reasonably for the train. He went out through the bead curtain. She was sitting at the table and smiled at him.

“Do you feel better?” he asked.

“I feel fine,” she said. “There’s nothing wrong with me. I feel fine.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, nothing happens. A man and a woman wait forty minutes for a train in a hot Spanish station, order drinks, and talk. The hills across the valley are long and white. She says they look like white elephants. He says he has never seen one. And so the conversation goes, apparently about nothing, until you notice how tightly everything they say is wound around one unspoken thing.

The unspoken thing is an abortion. The word never appears in the story. The man keeps calling it "an awfully simple operation" and says it is "just to let the air in." The woman, whom he calls Jig, has been brought to this station to catch a train to a city where it will be done. He insists he does not want her to do it unless she wants to. But everything he says pushes in one direction. He is perfectly willing, he says, to "go through with it" if it means anything to her, and yet he describes how simple it is, how many people have done it, how happy they all were afterward. She hears the pressure beneath the reassurance, and the story is the record of her hearing it.

Look at the way the big images work. The white hills are fertile, round, like the body of a pregnant woman, and on the other side of the station, the dry side, is a barren plain. The woman looks across the valley and sees the fields of grain and the river and says, "And we could have all this." The man answers, "We can have everything." She replies, "No, we can't." They are talking about the child, but also about a whole way of living that he does not want and she is starting to want. Their bags carry labels from all the hotels where they have spent nights. That is the life he is protecting, endless travel and drinks and no tie. The white elephant itself is an old English idiom for a gift that is more burden than benefit, and the joke may be on him as much as her: perhaps the thing he treats as a burden is the one valuable thing between them.

Notice also the drinks. They order beer, then Anis del Toro, which she says tastes like licorice, and she adds that everything tastes like licorice, especially the things you have waited so long for, like absinthe. The ordering of drinks fills silences, and it shows a couple used to drowning difficult subjects in pleasure.

The last lines are the most chilling. The man carries the bags to the other side, drinks an Anis, and comes back. "Do you feel better?" he asks. "I feel fine," she says. "There's nothing wrong with me. I feel fine." The repetition is the entire tragedy in miniature. She has stopped arguing, and what she says is not a report but a closing of a door. We never learn what she decides. The story is not about the decision. It is about a relationship in which one person talks past the other until the other falls silent.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Hills Like White Elephants is, by almost any measure, one of the most carefully built short stories in English. Its first virtue is control. Everything is rendered in short, plain sentences, with almost no interior access. We are never told what either character thinks. Instead the story gives us physical things, such as the bamboo bead curtain, the bags with their hotel labels, the damp felt pads under the beer glasses, and the station standing between two lines of rails, and it trusts those to carry the weight. The setting itself is an argument: a junction, a place of transit, where nobody belongs, positioned precisely between two options.

The dialogue is the real achievement. Listen to how the pattern shifts. In the early lines the woman is playful, almost teasing, and the man is patient. As the subject is gradually approached, her sentences shorten and her sarcasm sharpens. "Yes, you know it's perfectly simple." Then she explodes into the famous plea: "Would you please please please please please please please stop talking?" The stacking of pleases is a small miracle of rhythm, and it reads as exhaustion and rage in one breath. Meanwhile the man's speech becomes more and more circular, repeating the same reassurance in slightly different words. Hemingway lets repetition do what a lesser writer would do with adjectives.

There are weaknesses worth admitting. The first is that the man is drawn as somewhat too smoothly selfish, and some readers feel that the woman, by contrast, is mostly a vessel for pain. She is perceptive and bitter, but we learn little about what she actually wants beyond what she says in a few lines. The imbalance can make the story feel like a scenario with a built-in moral rather than a fully lived encounter. Secondly, a few of the symbolic touches, the fertile hills against the barren plain, the elephant as unwanted burden, are so neatly placed that they can feel like the author's hand arranging a still life.

There is also the question of the iceberg itself. Hemingway's method works best when the submerged part is felt and not merely hidden. Here, some argue, the story is so reticent that readers must reconstruct the subject from cues, which means the experience depends heavily on a certain background knowledge. A reader with no sense of what is going on can finish the story thinking it is a quarrel about a trip. Whether that is a flaw or a feature depends on taste; many admirers feel the difficulty is part of the point, since the characters themselves are avoiding the subject.

And yet the story's concision is so extreme and its emotional result so large that its flaws seem like the shadow of its strengths. It takes perhaps ten minutes to read and an afternoon to stop thinking about.

III. The Impact It Had

The story first appeared in 1927 in the Paris magazine transition, an avant-garde journal, and was soon collected in Hemingway's book Men Without Women, which came out in October of that year. By then Hemingway was already famous for The Sun Also Rises, published the previous year, and this story reinforced his standing as the most influential new prose stylist in America. It showed what his method could do in a very small space: a whole relationship, a moral crisis, and a social era, compressed into a few pages of speech.

Its most important legacy is as the model of what Hemingway later described, in his book on bullfighting, Death in the Afternoon, as the theory of omission. A writer who knows enough about the subject, he said, may leave things out, and the reader will feel them as strongly as if they had been stated. The image of the iceberg, with only an eighth above water, comes from there, and Hills Like White Elephants has become the textbook illustration. It is routinely used in writing classes to teach subtext: how dialogue can carry meaning that the words themselves avoid.

The story's effect on later short fiction has been enormous. The spare, dialogue-driven, reticent realism associated with writers like Raymond Carver, whose characters talk around the things that matter most, owes a great deal to this model. So do countless minimalist stories and, for that matter, screenwriting. The rhythm of overlapping, evasive conversation can be traced into film and television drama.

It also has a cultural and political afterlife. The story deals with abortion without ever naming it, at a time when the subject was legally and socially taboo, and it has been taught, argued over, and read in many different ways as the public debate has changed. Some readers see it as sympathetic to a woman pressured into a choice, others as a story about a couple's divergence of values, and still others as a study in male evasion. The fact that it can support these readings is part of why it endures.

It has been adapted for the screen and stage a number of times, including a 1990 cable television version, and it has been staged as a short theater piece. Few of these adaptations keep the bare, unforced feeling of the original, which is a kind of tribute to how much depends on the text's silences. In Hemingway's career, the story sits near the height of his early style, before the more public persona of the war correspondent and big-game hunter overshadowed the quiet, exacting craftsman who wrote it.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers and writers have loved Hills Like White Elephants above all for its nerve. It dares to leave the biggest subject unsaid, and it does so with such confidence that the reader is drawn into collaboration. You feel clever when you catch on, and then you feel the weight of what you have caught. That experience of discovery, of slowly realizing that this idle chatter is a life-changing conversation, is part of what people remember. Many readers say they first read it as a puzzle and finished it as a heartbreak.

They also love how true the dialogue feels. Anyone who has been in a relationship where one person is pressing and the other is retreating will recognize the rhythm: the "I don't want you to do anything you don't want to do" followed immediately by more pressure; the "I'm perfectly willing" that is nothing of the kind. The man's gentle, reasonable tone is, in some ways, the most unsettling part, because it is how real coercion often sounds. It is rarely a shout. It is a patient voice repeating that everything will be fine.

Another thing admirers cherish is the woman's quiet intelligence. She is witty and sharp, and she hears everything. Her remark about the hills and her small bitter jokes show a mind that has outpaced the man's. At the end, her insistence that she feels fine is a performance, and readers love the dignity and the sorrow of it. Many say she is among the most memorable women in Hemingway's work, a character who stays vivid even though she is rarely given the narrative's sympathy outright.

Fellow writers admire the story as a masterclass in technique. It is frequently named in discussions of the best American short stories, and it is a favorite of teachers because every detail, down to the beaded curtain, rewards close reading. The sheer compression of it, a whole drama in a few pages and almost no action, is a source of awe. Even people who dislike Hemingway's public persona tend to concede that this story shows him at his most disciplined.

Finally, readers respond to the setting, which is almost a character. The heat, the white hills, the dry side and the green side, the clinking of beer glasses, the sense of a train that will take them somewhere: it is a landscape in which a decision hangs in the air. The atmosphere does the emotional work. A reader who has never been to Spain can feel the stillness of that platform and the pressure of the clock ticking toward a train.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the story is so open, much of the criticism has been about how to read it, and some of it is sharply divided. The central dispute concerns whose side the story is on. One group of readers, many of them feminist critics, see it as an indictment of a man who manipulates a woman into an abortion she does not want while claiming to leave the choice to her. Another group reads it more ambiguously, as a portrait of a couple in which both are at fault, or as a story about a woman who has already decided and is simply waiting for the man to catch up. A third strain argues that the story is, in the end, more concerned with the man's emotional limits than with the woman's, and that Hemingway's sympathies, as in much of his work, lie more with male experience. Reasonable readers have landed everywhere in this range, and the text does not settle it.

A related criticism is that the woman, for all her vividness, has little agency or inner life in the story. We see her only through speech and through the man's eyes, and she is reduced at times to a symbol, the fertile landscape, the vessel of pain. Some critics find that this reproduces the very objectification the story seems to deplore. Others reply that this is the point, that the story shows how a woman can be silenced by a conversation, and that the form enacts the problem.

There is also the broader criticism of Hemingway's style that this story sometimes attracts. The famous reticence can look like a pose. Critics from his own time onward have charged that his clipped, repetitive prose, imitated by countless lesser writers, can degrade into mannerism, with a tough-guy simplicity that conceals thinness. Hills Like White Elephants largely escapes that charge, but it is the kind of story that spawned a thousand imitators who did not escape it.

Finally, some readers object to the symbolism as too schematic: the two sides of the valley, the white elephant, the barren versus fertile imagery. They feel that a story pledged to understatement should not be quite so careful about its metaphors, and that the arrangement shows the writer's hand more than he intended. Others defend the images as natural, since people notice such things when they are anxious.

What stands out is that few critics dispute the story's quality. The argument is almost entirely about its meaning and its politics, which is often the sign of a work that has genuinely gotten under the skin of its readers.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Hills Like White Elephants belongs to the modernist moment of the 1920s, when writers in Paris, London, and New York were trying to break with Victorian fullness and moralizing and to find a language equal to the shock of the First World War. Hemingway was part of the group of American expatriates in Paris, and he was mentored in compression by Ezra Pound and Gertrude Stein. The idea that a story should drop its explanations and present things as they are, with feeling carried by objects and gestures, is a modernist principle shared by Pound's imagist poetry and by Joyce and Eliot in different ways.

Within that movement, Hemingway's contribution was a particular kind of plainness. His ancestors include Mark Twain's vernacular in Huckleberry Finn, which he once called the source of all modern American literature, and the compression of Chekhov, whose stories also end on an open note and trust subtext. The comparison with Chekhov is useful: both writers show an unresolved moment and let the reader supply the rest, though Chekhov is warmer and more interested in the sorrow of ordinary lives, while Hemingway is colder and more concerned with stoic endurance.

Descendants are everywhere. Raymond Carver's minimalist stories, with their halting, evasive speech, are one obvious line. Another runs through writers like Grace Paley, Alice Munro, and Lorrie Moore, who work with subtext in different keys. In drama, Harold Pinter's famous pauses and menace beneath polite talk have something in common with the story's method. And in film, the idea that the most important part of a scene may be what is not said is now commonplace.

Placed beside Hemingway's other work, the story shows a side of him that is less swaggering than his reputation. It belongs with his quieter tales of failing intimacy and dread, rather than with the big-game and war material. It is also comparable to Joyce's The Dead, which ends with a revelation between husband and wife, though Joyce works in a lush, lyrical register and Hemingway in a bare one.

Why read it now? Because the subject remains charged, and because the story's real subject, how a conversation can be a struggle for power disguised as concern, is permanent. It is also one of the cleanest lessons in how fiction works: how a handful of objects and a bit of dialogue can create a whole moral situation, and how silence can be louder than speech. After reading it, you may start listening differently to the way people say that they are fine.
