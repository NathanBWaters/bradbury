When I Have Fears That I May Cease to Be

John Keats (1818) • 3 min read

Haunted by the premonition of dying young before his teeming brain can harvest its books, Keats stands alone on the shore of the wide world until love and fame sink into nothingness.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Composed in January 1818 when Keats was twenty-two—already trained as an apothecary-surgeon at Guy’s Hospital and acutely aware of the family tuberculosis that had killed his mother and was then consuming his brother Tom—this sonnet was sent in a letter to John Hamilton Reynolds as Keats’s first deliberate experiment in the Shakespearean sonnet form. Belonging to the British Romanticism tradition and categorized under Romantic Ode & Lyric, 'When I Have Fears That I May Cease to Be' (1818) unfolds across 14 verbatim lines in 1 stanza. Published posthumously in 1848 by Richard Monckton Milnes, the sonnet is tragic in its prophetic accuracy—Keats died just three years after writing it—yet triumphant in its paradox: by articulating his fear of leaving no immortal verse behind, he created one of the most immortal sonnets in English.

When I have fears that I may cease to be

Before my pen has glean’d my teeming brain,

Before high pilèd books, in charactry,

Hold like rich garners the full-ripen’d grain;

When I behold, upon the night’s starr’d face,

Huge cloudy symbols of a high romance,

And think that I may never live to trace

Their shadows, with the magic hand of chance;

And when I feel, fair creature of an hour!

That I shall never look upon thee more,

Never have relish in the faery power

Of unreflecting love;—then on the shore

Of the wide world I stand alone, and think

Till Love and Fame to nothingness do sink.
