Fort Point's Civil War Brick Casemates Beneath the Golden Gate Arch (1853–1937)

San Francisco — How U.S. Army engineers built a three-tiered Third System brick fortress on a serpentinite cliff—and why Joseph Strauss designed a 300-foot steel arch just to spare its walls (Antebellum & Civil War Defense (1853–1861) to Golden Gate Bridge Era (1933–1937)) • 8 min read

“While the old fort has no military value now, it should be preserved and restored as a fine example of the mason’s art.” — Chief Engineer Joseph B. Strauss (1930)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Tucked directly beneath the roaring southern steel arch of the Golden Gate Bridge at the northernmost tip of the San Francisco peninsula stands Fort Point—the only Third System brick-and-granite coastal casemate fortress ever constructed west of the Mississippi River. Built between 1853 and 1861 by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers after blasting away the ninety-foot Spanish promontory of Punta del Cantil Blanco down to sea level, its seven-foot-thick brick walls mounted up to 126 heavy cannon at waterline to guard Gold Rush shipping during the Civil War. Scheduled for total demolition in 1929 to make way for the Golden Gate Bridge's southern anchorage, the fort was miraculously saved when Chief Engineer Joseph Strauss redesigned the approach into a dramatic 319-foot steel arch vaulting directly over the battlements.

Long before American bricklayers arrived at the narrowest one-mile throat of the Golden Gate strait, Spanish explorer Juan Bautista de Anza planted a wooden cross atop the high white serpentinite cliff on March 28, 1776, and in 1794 Spanish Viceroy Marqués de Branciforte built the adobe horseshoe battery of Castillo de San Joaquín armed with bronze sixteen-pounder cannon. When news of James Marshall's 1848 gold discovery turned San Francisco Bay into the richest unfortified harbor on the Pacific Ocean, a joint Board of U.S. Army and Navy Engineers warned Washington in 1850 that a single enemy frigate slipping through the Golden Gate at flood tide could hold the entire city and the California gold fleet for ransom.

Beginning in autumn 1853 under Lieutenant Colonel René Edward De Russy, three hundred civilian stonemasons, quarrymen, and laborers took black powder and iron sledges to the ninety-foot cliff of Punta del Cantil Blanco, blasting the entire promontory down to a flat rock shelf just fifteen feet above the crashing surf so cannonballs fired from the lowest tier could ricochet across the water surface into wooden ship hulls. Modeled on Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor as part of the United States' 'Third System' of coastal fortifications inspired by French military architect Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban, Fort Point was engineered with three arched tiers of vaulted brick casemates and a fourth rooftop barbette tier capable of mounting 126 heavy smoothbore columbiads summing a devastating broadside.

Because early Gold Rush San Francisco lacked high-strength brickyards and dressed stone quarries, the Army fired millions of dark red clay bricks in local kilns at Fort Point, shipped precision-cut blocks of granite around Cape Horn from New England and across the Pacific from quarries in Hong Kong, and imported Scottish stonemasons to carve the fortress's breathtaking free-standing spiral staircases. Step inside the shadowy courtyard today and examine the interlocking granite treads of the circular stair towers and the groin-vaulted brick ceilings of the gun rooms: every compound curve was laid in hydraulic lime mortar to a tolerance of fractions of an inch, with iron traverse rails embedded in the stone floors so five-man gun crews could pivot seven-ton Rodman cannon across iron embrasures.

In the winter of 1860–1861, as Southern states seceded and rumors swept California that Knights of the Golden Circle secessionists planned to seize San Francisco's forts and establish a 'Pacific Republic,' the Army rushed the final cannon into place and marched Company I of the 3rd U.S. Artillery through the heavy oak-and-iron Sally Port on February 15, 1861—two months before Confederates fired on Fort Point's sister fortress, Fort Sumter. Throughout the four years of the Civil War, soldiers shivering in the damp, drafty second- and third-tier quarters of Fort Point kept thirty-six-pounder shot glowing cherry-red inside brick 'hot-shot furnaces' in the courtyard—ready to set any Confederate raider like the CSS Shenandoah ablaze if she dared enter the Golden Gate.

Ironically, the very year Fort Point was occupied—1861—marked the obsolescence of vertical brick forts: during the April 1862 Union siege of Fort Pulaski in Georgia, newly invented rifled Parrott cannon pulverized seven-foot-thick brick walls from a mile away, proving that masonry casemates could no longer withstand high-velocity conical shells. By 1868, the Army had begun moving its primary heavy guns out of the brick casemates up to earth-covered concrete batteries atop the adjacent Marin and Presidio bluffs, officially renaming the brick bastion 'Fort Winfield Scott' in 1882 before using its damp rooms for coastal artillery barracks and storage.

On the morning of April 18, 1906, the main trace of the San Andreas Fault ruptured just five miles west of Fort Point offshore in the Pacific; while modern buildings across downtown San Francisco collapsed, the seven-foot-thick interlocking brick vaults and granite spiral stairs of the 1850s fortress absorbed the seismic shock with only minor hairline cracks along its western seawall. A far deadlier threat arrived in the 1920s when preliminary blueprints for the Golden Gate Bridge called for dynamiting Fort Point down to bedrock so the southern concrete anchorage and approach viaduct could rest directly on the point.

After inspecting the hand-carved granite spiral stairs and honeycomb brick arches on site, Golden Gate Bridge Chief Engineer Joseph B. Strauss had an epiphany: 'While the old fort has no military value now,' Strauss wrote, 'it should be preserved and restored as a fine example of the mason's art'—and he ordered his structural team to redesign the southern bridge approach. Between 1933 and 1937, ironworkers sank the massive twin concrete pylons of the Golden Gate Bridge's South Anchorage directly behind the fort's southern wall and flung a soaring, four-hinged 319-foot steel arch fifty feet above Fort Point's rooftop lighthouse, enclosing the nineteenth-century brick fortress inside a dramatic frame of International Orange steel.

During World War II, Fort Point returned briefly to active front-line duty one last time: soldiers manned rapid-fire 90-millimeter anti-motor-torpedo-boat guns on its parapet while an anti-submarine steel net stretched across the bay from near the fort's seawall to Marin County. Following a tireless preservation campaign launched in the 1960s by retired Army Engineers and historians in the Fort Point Museum Association—and popularized worldwide when Alfred Hitchcock filmed Kim Novak plunging into the surging tide at the fort's seawall in his 1958 masterpiece Vertigo—President Richard Nixon signed legislation on October 16, 1970, establishing Fort Point National Historic Site.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Vauban Geometry and 'Third System' Masonry Engineering

Why does Fort Point look like a European medieval castle dropped beneath a twentieth-century suspension bridge? Following the War of 1812, when British warships burned Washington, D.C., the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers launched the 'Third System' of coastal defense—building multi-tiered brick-and-granite casemate forts at strategic harbor narrows so over a hundred heavy guns could concentrate crossfire inside a compact footprint. Fort Point was the sole Pacific Coast member of this elite architectural family alongside Fort Sumter, Fort Monroe, and Fort Pulaski.

2. How Joseph Strauss's 319-Foot Steel Arch Saved the Fortress

Standard suspension-bridge economics dictated demolishing the obsolete 1850s brick fort at the tip of the peninsula to anchor the southern approach trusses. Instead, Chief Engineer Joseph Strauss and architect Irving Morrow recognized that juxtaposing 1850s hand-laid red brick with 1930s riveted Art Deco steel would create one of the most dramatic architectural contrasts in the world—spanning the entire fort with a 319-foot steel arch that clears the 1864 rooftop cast-iron lighthouse by less than fifty feet.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three physical details inside the fort: first, the Courtyard Hot-Shot Furnaces, brick ovens built to heat solid iron cannonballs cherry-red so they would ignite wooden enemy warships; second, the Cantilevered Granite Spiral Staircases carved by Scottish masons, where each stone step supports the one above it without a central column; and third, the 1958 Vertigo seawall steps outside the west bastion where Hitchcock filmed Madeleine's leap into the bay.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk or bike west along the Crissy Field Promenade and follow Marine Drive to the end of the road beneath the Golden Gate Bridge at Fort Point National Historic Site. Pass through the oak-and-iron Sally Port into the quiet central courtyard, climb the cantilevered granite spiral stairs to the rooftop barbette tier, and stand directly beneath the massive International Orange steel belly of the bridge arch as wind and automobile tires hum overhead.
