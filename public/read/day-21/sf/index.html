Fort Point's Civil War Brick Casemates Beneath the Golden Gate Arch (1853–1937)

San Francisco — How U.S. Army engineers built a three-tiered Third System brick fortress on a serpentinite cliff—and why Joseph Strauss designed a 300-foot steel arch just to spare its walls (Antebellum & Civil War Defense (1853–1861) to Golden Gate Bridge Era (1933–1937)) • 20 min read

Key Context & Why It Matters

Tucked directly beneath the roaring southern steel arch of the Golden Gate Bridge at the northernmost tip of the San Francisco peninsula (37.8106° N, 122.4770° W) stands Fort Point—the only Third System brick-and-granite coastal casemate fortress ever constructed west of the Mississippi River. Built between 1853 and 1861 by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers after blasting away the ninety-foot Spanish promontory of Punta del Cantil Blanco down to sea level, its seven-foot-thick brick walls mounted up to 126 heavy cannon at waterline to guard Gold Rush shipping during the Civil War. Scheduled for total demolition in 1929 to make way for the Golden Gate Bridge's southern anchorage, the fort was miraculously saved when Chief Engineer Joseph Strauss redesigned the approach into a dramatic 319-foot steel arch vaulting directly over the battlements.

Why does Fort Point look like a European medieval castle dropped beneath a twentieth-century suspension bridge? Fort Point was the sole Pacific Coast member of this elite architectural family alongside Fort Sumter, Fort Monroe, and Fort Pulaski.
