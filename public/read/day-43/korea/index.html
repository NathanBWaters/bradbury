Korea Deep-Dive Story • Treaty Port History & Immigrant Gastronomy

Incheon's 1883 Open Port (Gaehangjang), Chinatown, and the Birth of Jajangmyeon

Korea — Where a single stone staircase divided Chinese and Japanese treaty concessions, and Shandong dockworkers caramelized black bean paste into Korea's national comfort dish (Late Joseon Treaty Era (1883–1905) to Present) • 9 min read

“On the left of the staircase stand Chinese stone lanterns and red brick; on the right stand Japanese gray tiles and banks—one port where three empires collided.” — Incheon Open Port Chronicle

Key Context & Why It Matters

Stand on the steep granite staircase climbing the southern slope of Mount Eungbongsan in Jung-gu, Incheon, and place your left foot in nineteenth-century Qing China and your right foot in Meiji Japan. When the fishing village of Jemulpo (Incheon) was forced to open as a treaty port in January 1883—serving as the maritime gateway just forty kilometers west of Seoul—foreign powers carved the hills into extraterritorial concessions (Jogyeji). Along the crimson streets of Incheon Chinatown, immigrant laborers and merchants from Shandong Province adapted their home dish of zhajiangmian at the 1905 Gonghwachun restaurant by roasting black soybean paste (chunjang) with caramel and onions, inventing Korean Jajangmyeon.

Walk two hundred meters uphill from Incheon Station (the western terminus of Seoul Subway Line 1, where Korea’s very first railway, the Gyeongin Line, steamed out toward Noryangjin on September 18, 1899), pass beneath the soaring red-and-gold wooden Paeru gateway of Incheon Chinatown, and climb to a remarkable granite staircase on the hillside of Seonrin-dong: the Sino-Japanese Concession Boundary Staircase (Cheong-Il Jogyeji Gyeonggye Gyedan). Look to your left (west) of the stone banister: every historic building is constructed of tall red and gray vertical brick with arched windows, wooden balconies, and crimson eaves in the style of nineteenth-century Shandong, China. Now look just three meters to your right (east) of the exact same staircase: the architecture shifts abruptly to low, horizontal timber-and-plaster machiya storefronts and neoclassical stone bank facades built in the style of Meiji-era Japan!

No single street corner in Korea makes the geopolitical earthquake of the late nineteenth century more visible to the naked eye. For five hundred years under the Joseon Dynasty’s maritime isolation policy, Jemulpo (modern Incheon) was a quiet coastal fishing village guarded by mudflats whose extreme nine-to-ten-meter tidal range—among the highest tides on planet Earth—left ships stranded on grey mud at low water. In the 1870s and 1880s, however, because Jemulpo sat at the mouth of the Han River just forty kilometers from the royal capital of Hanyang (Seoul), imperial gunboats from Japan, the United States, Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and Qing China converged on its anchorage. Following the 1876 Treaty of Ganghwa and the 1882 Imo Incident (when Qing Chinese troops led by Yuan Shikai and Japanese legation guards marched into Seoul), Jemulpo was officially opened as an international treaty port (Gaehangjang) in January 1883.

Between 1883 and 1884, the Joseon government was pressured into signing three separate municipal land leases: the Japanese Concession (Ilbon Jogye, September 1883), the Chinese Concession (Cheongguk Jogye, April 1884, covering 5,000 pyeong on the western slope), and the General Foreign Concession (Gakguk Jogye, October 1884, where American, British, German, and Russian diplomats and traders built Western brick villas and founded Korea’s first Western-style hotel, the Daebul Hotel, in 1888 and Korea’s first modern public park, Jayu Park [Manguk Gongwon], atop Mount Eungbongsan!). Overnight, this hillside became a cosmopolitan cauldron of Korean modernity. Right here in the Open Port district stood branches of the Japanese Dai-Ichi Bank (1888, now the Incheon Open Port Museum with its Renaissance stone dome), the 18th Bank, the German trading firm E. Meyer & Co. (Sechang Yanghaeng, which imported industrial machinery and needles), the Jemulpo Club (1901, where foreign diplomats played billiards), and the waterfront dock where American Methodist and Presbyterian missionaries Henry Appenzeller and Horace Grant Underwood stepped ashore on Easter Sunday, April 5, 1885, to found Korea’s first modern hospitals and universities (Pai Chai and Yonsei).

Meanwhile, across the boundary staircase in the Chinese Concession (Cheongguk Jogye, today’s Seonrin-dong and Bukseong-dong), ferries crossing the narrow Yellow Sea from Yantai and Weihai carried thousands of Chinese merchants, masons, farmers, and dockworkers (Hwagyo)—more than 90 percent of whom hailed from Shandong Province. While wealthy Chinese silk and cotton merchants built grand guildhalls such as the Uiseondang temple (1893, still fragrant with spiral incense coils today), thousands of poor Shandong coolies (kuli) sweated on the Jemulpo waterfront hauling heavy sacks of rice, salt, and coal off cargo lighters across the tidal mudflats. Between shifts, these exhausted dockworkers needed a meal that was fast, hot, calorie-dense, and cost just a few copper coins.

Along the harbor alleys, fellow Shandong immigrants set up small noodle cauldrons and wok stalls serving a rough hometown street dish called Zhajiangmian (pronounced Jajangmyeon, in Korean): thick hand-pulled wheat noodles (sutamyeon) topped with a spoonful of salty, dry-fried fermented wheat-and-soybean paste (mianjiang) and slivered raw radish. In 1905 (or 1912 under its formal name), a savvy Shandong immigrant restaurateur named Woo Hee-gwang opened a grand two-story brick courtyard restaurant in Seonrin-dong called Gonghwachun (—named in 1912 to celebrate the 'Spring of the Republic' after the Xinhai Revolution established the Republic of China, and preserved today as the Jajangmyeon Museum, Registered Cultural Heritage No. 246!).

Initially, traditional Shandong zhajiangmian—which was intensely salty, pale brown, and lacked sauce volume—tasted too harsh and dry to Korean customers accustomed to balanced savory-sweet jang and generous broths. Over the decades between the 1920s and the 1950s, the Chinese-Korean (Hwagyo) chefs of Incheon Chinatown engineered a culinary metamorphosis that turned a Shandong dockworker snack into South Korea’s beloved national comfort food. After the Korean War, Wang Song-san (founder of the Sajahu-pyo Lion Brand Chunjang) and Incheon Chinatown chefs took roasted black wheat-soybean paste (Chunjang), added caramel to give it a glossy, midnight-black sheen and mellow its sharp saltiness, and wok-fried the paste first in hot pork lard (chunjang bokkeum) to unlock a deep, nutty Maillard aroma!

Next, instead of serving a dry dollop of paste, the chefs stir-fried generous mounds of diced Korean onions (yangpa, whose natural sucrose sweetness balanced the fermented bean paste), cabbage, zucchini, and cubed pork belly into the sizzling black sauce, bound it with a splash of potato or corn starch slurry into a velvety gravy (or fried it dry to order as Ganjjajang, !), and ladled it steaming over bouncy alkaline wheat noodles stretched by hand! Throughout the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, when South Korea was rebuilding from war poverty and eating beef in a restaurant was an unreachable luxury, a bowl of Incheon-style Jajangmyeon (costing just 15 won in 1960s government price-stabilization tables!) became the supreme family celebration meal of Korean childhood: eaten after every elementary school graduation ceremony, every school sports day, and on the dusty floor of every new apartment on moving day delivered in a tin iron box (cheolgabang)!

What saved Incheon’s nineteenth-century Open Port and Chinatown from being bulldozed into high-rise apartments in the late twentieth century? Because maritime container shipping shifted south to the new Incheon Inner and South Ports in the 1970s and strict regulations restricted Hwagyo property redevelopment for decades, the 1880s–1910s red-brick warehouses, Japanese bank buildings, and Chinese guildhouses survived intact—until Incheon City restored the Open Port Creative City (Gaehangjang Cultural District), converting old 1930s coastal rice warehouses into the Incheon Art Platform. Climb the Sino-Japanese Concession Staircase in Incheon today, look out over the giant lock gates of the Inner Port toward the Yellow Sea, and then step into the original 1905 brick dining hall of Gonghwachun (now the Jajangmyeon Museum) before ordering a bowl of freshly wok-fired Ganjjajang down the street. Across 140 years, where foreign gunboats once drew borders in stone, immigrant woks and Korean onions cooked up shared joy.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Urban Morphology of Extraterritoriality: The Three Concessions of 1883–1884

Architectural historians study Incheon's Jung-gu Open Port District as a preserved textbook of late nineteenth-century treaty-port urbanism. Because the Chinese Concession (1884), Japanese Concession (1883), and General Foreign Concession (1884) were governed by separate municipal councils with their own building codes, lot widths, and street grids, a walker today can cross the granite Cheong-Il Staircase and observe an instant transition from Qing Shandong courtyard masonry to Meiji bank architecture and Western consular villas.

2. Culinary Chemistry: Caramelized 'Chunjang' and the Maillard Evolution of Jajangmyeon

Food historians trace the birth of Korean Jajangmyeon at Incheon's Gonghwachun (Registered Cultural Heritage No. 246) as a masterclass in cross-cultural adaptation. By infusing fermented Shandong wheat-soy paste with caramel into black Chunjang, pre-frying the paste in hot lipid at 180°C to trigger pyrazine Maillard aromatics, and caramelizing high-sugar Korean onions, Hwagyo chefs transformed a salty northern Chinese condiment into a glossy, savory-sweet sauce now consumed at a rate of over six million bowls a day in Korea.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three Incheon terms in mind: first, Gaehangjang (Open Port District), the 1883 treaty port precinct of Jemulpo; second, Jogyeji (Foreign Concession), the extraterritorial zones divided by the still-standing Cheong-Il boundary staircase; and third, Gonghwachun & Chunjang, the historic 1905 Chinatown restaurant building (now the Jajangmyeon Museum) and the roasted black bean paste at the heart of the dish.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Take Seoul Subway Line 1 to its final western stop, Incheon Station, and walk straight through the Chinatown Paeru gate. Visit the Jajangmyeon Museum housed inside the original 1905 red-brick Gonghwachun building, climb the Cheong-Il Concession Boundary Staircase up to Jayu Park, and stroll east through the Japanese Bank Street (Incheon Open Port Museum and Daebul Hotel Exhibition Hall) to the Incheon Art Platform.
