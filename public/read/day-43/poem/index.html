Mending Wall

Robert Frost (1914) • 4 min read

Walking opposite sides of a New England stone boundary in spring, two neighbors repair winter’s gaps while debating whether good fences really make good neighbors.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in England in 1913 as Robert Frost remembered walking the spring boundary wall with his French-Canadian neighbor Napoleon Guay on his Derry, New Hampshire farm, 'Mending Wall' opened Frost’s breakthrough 1914 volume North of Boston (published in London by David Nutt). Belonging to the American Pastoral Modernism tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'Mending Wall' (1914) unfolds across 45 verbatim lines in 1 stanza. Cited by diplomats, Supreme Court justices, and readers worldwide—and famously read by Frost in Moscow in 1962 just after the Berlin Wall went up—'Mending Wall' is a masterclass in dramatic irony where neither neighbor holds a monopoly on truth. Through forty-five lines of unrhymed iambic pentameter (blank verse) shaped by Frost’s theory of 'the sound of sense'—beginning with an inverted trochaic mystery ('Something there is that doesn’t love a wall') and balancing the two contrasting leitmotifs twice across the poem like stones set in place.

Something there is that doesn’t love a wall,

That sends the frozen-ground-swell under it,

And spills the upper boulders in the sun;

And makes gaps even two can pass abreast.

The work of hunters is another thing:

I have come after them and made repair

Where they have left not one stone on a stone,

But they would have the rabbit out of hiding,

To please the yelping dogs. The gaps I mean,

No one has seen them made or heard them made,

But at spring mending-time we find them there.

I let my neighbour know beyond the hill;

And on a day we meet to walk the line

And set the wall between us once again.

We keep the wall between us as we go.

To each the boulders that have fallen to each.

And some are loaves and some so nearly balls

We have to use a spell to make them balance:

“Stay where you are until our backs are turned!”

We wear our fingers rough with handling them.

Oh, just another kind of out-door game,

One on a side. It comes to little more:

There where it is we do not need the wall:

He is all pine and I am apple orchard.

My apple trees will never get across

And eat the cones under his pines, I tell him.

He only says, “Good fences make good neighbours.”

Spring is the mischief in me, and I wonder

If I could put a notion in his head:

“Why do they make good neighbours? Isn’t it

Where there are cows? But here there are no cows.

Before I built a wall I’d ask to know

What I was walling in or walling out,

And to whom I was like to give offence.

Something there is that doesn’t love a wall,

That wants it down.” I could say “Elves” to him,

But it’s not elves exactly, and I’d rather

He said it for himself. I see him there

Bringing a stone grasped firmly by the top

In each hand, like an old-stone savage armed.

He moves in darkness as it seems to me,

Not of woods only and the shade of trees.

He will not go behind his father’s saying,

And he likes having thought of it so well

He says again, “Good fences make good neighbours.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in England in 1913 as Robert Frost remembered walking the spring boundary wall with his French-Canadian neighbor Napoleon Guay on his Derry, New Hampshire farm, 'Mending Wall' opened Frost’s breakthrough 1914 volume North of Boston (published in London by David Nutt). Within the broader trajectory of American Pastoral Modernism, Robert Frost composed 'Mending Wall' (1914) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Something there is that doesn’t love a wall,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Robert Frost grounded these 45 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Mending Wall' lies the perennial human tension between boundaries and openness, custom and skepticism: while the mischievous narrator points out that his apple trees will never cross over to eat his neighbor’s pine cones and wonders what 'doesn’t love a wall,' it is actually the narrator who initiates the spring ritual alongside a neighbor who clings to his father’s proverb. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"There where it is we do not need the wall:"—Robert Frost develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"He says again, “Good fences make good neighbours.”"—the central metaphor has transformed.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Robert Frost constructs 'Mending Wall' using forty-five lines of unrhymed iambic pentameter (blank verse) shaped by Frost’s theory of 'the sound of sense'—beginning with an inverted trochaic mystery ('Something there is that doesn’t love a wall') and balancing the two contrasting leitmotifs twice across the poem like stones set in place. Spanning 45 lines across 1 stanza, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Robert Frost balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Something there is that doesn’t love a wall," and "He says again, “Good fences make good neighbours.”" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Cited by diplomats, Supreme Court justices, and readers worldwide—and famously read by Frost in Moscow in 1962 just after the Berlin Wall went up—'Mending Wall' is a masterclass in dramatic irony where neither neighbor holds a monopoly on truth. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Mending Wall' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Robert Frost’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
