Nightly Poem • Pastoral & Elegy

Mending Wall

Robert Frost (1914) • 4 min read

Walking opposite sides of a New England stone boundary in spring, two neighbors repair winter’s gaps while debating whether good fences really make good neighbors.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Robert Frost published "Mending Wall" in 1914 in his book North of Boston, while he was living in England and just beginning to be famous. He was nearly forty, had failed for years to get noticed, and had spent a decade farming in New Hampshire while writing at night. The poem comes straight out of that rural life: two neighbors meet each spring to repair the stone wall between their farms. One of them, the speaker, wonders why they bother. The other repeats a saying he got from his father, that good fences make good neighbors. The poem has become one of the most quoted and most misquoted in American literature, often claimed by both sides of arguments about borders and walls. In plain conversational language, it is really about the pull between our need for boundaries and our longing for connection, and about how hard it is to question what we inherited.

Something there is that doesn’t love a wall,

That sends the frozen-ground-swell under it,

And spills the upper boulders in the sun;

And makes gaps even two can pass abreast.

The work of hunters is another thing:

I have come after them and made repair

Where they have left not one stone on a stone,

But they would have the rabbit out of hiding,

To please the yelping dogs. The gaps I mean,

No one has seen them made or heard them made,

But at spring mending-time we find them there.

I let my neighbour know beyond the hill;

And on a day we meet to walk the line

And set the wall between us once again.

We keep the wall between us as we go.

To each the boulders that have fallen to each.

And some are loaves and some so nearly balls

We have to use a spell to make them balance:

“Stay where you are until our backs are turned!”

We wear our fingers rough with handling them.

Oh, just another kind of out-door game,

One on a side. It comes to little more:

There where it is we do not need the wall:

He is all pine and I am apple orchard.

My apple trees will never get across

And eat the cones under his pines, I tell him.

He only says, “Good fences make good neighbours.”

Spring is the mischief in me, and I wonder

If I could put a notion in his head:

“Why do they make good neighbours? Isn’t it

Where there are cows? But here there are no cows.

Before I built a wall I’d ask to know

What I was walling in or walling out,

And to whom I was like to give offence.

Something there is that doesn’t love a wall,

That wants it down.” I could say “Elves” to him,

But it’s not elves exactly, and I’d rather

He said it for himself. I see him there

Bringing a stone grasped firmly by the top

In each hand, like an old-stone savage armed.

He moves in darkness as it seems to me,

Not of woods only and the shade of trees.

He will not go behind his father’s saying,

And he likes having thought of it so well

He says again, “Good fences make good neighbours.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem opens with a line that sounds like a riddle: "Something there is that doesn't love a wall." Notice that the speaker doesn't say what that something is. He only says what it does. It sends "the frozen-ground-swell" under the stones, and spills the upper boulders in the sun, making gaps wide enough for two people to walk through side by side. So the first thing we learn is that nature itself works against walls. The speaker also mentions hunters, who tear walls apart to get at rabbits, but he sets them aside. The gaps he means are different. No one has seen them made or heard them made. They simply appear by spring.

Then comes the human side. Each year, the speaker lets his neighbor know, and the two men meet to "walk the line" and repair the wall, each taking the stones that have fallen on his side. The wall-building is described with a mix of tenderness and mockery. The stones are like loaves and balls and have to be balanced with a spell: "Stay where you are until our backs are turned!" They wear their fingers rough. The speaker calls it "just another kind of out-door game, / One on a side."

The disagreement arrives next. The speaker points out that there is no need for a wall here: "He is all pine and I am apple orchard. / My apple trees will never get across / And eat the cones under his pines." The neighbor's only reply is the saying "Good fences make good neighbours." The speaker, feeling the mischief of spring, wants to put a doubt in his neighbor's head. Why do fences make good neighbors? That might make sense where there are cows, but here there are none. Before building a wall, he says, he'd want to know "What I was walling in or walling out, / And to whom I was like to give offence."

The ending is where the poem becomes layered. The speaker sees his neighbor coming with a stone grasped in each hand, "like an old-stone savage armed," and adds that the man "moves in darkness as it seems to me, / Not of woods only and the shade of trees." This is a sharp, even unfair, observation. He is saying the neighbor is stuck in a kind of mental darkness, unable to go beyond his father's saying. But look at what the speaker himself has done. He is the one who calls the meeting. He goes along with the repair. He teases, but he never actually says his argument aloud. The final repetition of the proverb, with its gentle dig that the neighbor "likes having thought of it so well," seems ironic about the neighbor, but the irony can turn back on the speaker too. A first-time reader often sees a clever speaker and a dull neighbor. A deeper reading notices that both men keep building the wall, and that the speaker's feeling of superiority is its own kind of wall.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

"Mending Wall" is easy to love and surprisingly hard to pin down, and that is much of its strength. The craft is superb. Frost writes in blank verse, which means unrhymed lines of five beats, the same form Shakespeare and Milton used. But he makes it sound like a man talking over a fence. The sentences run across the line endings, as speech does, so the meter is felt more as a pulse underneath than as a rhythm on top. This was Frost's special talent. He spoke of catching what he called the sound of sense, the way a voice's tone carries meaning before we grasp the words. You can hear it in "Oh, just another kind of out-door game, / One on a side. It comes to little more." The shrug in that line is pure speech.

The structure is also admirable. The poem opens with nature's mystery, moves to a comic scene of labor, shifts to argument, and ends with a quietly unsettling image. It uses repetition well. The opening line, "Something there is that doesn't love a wall," returns in the middle, and the neighbor's proverb appears at both the midpoint and the close. The difference is that by the end we have seen how the speaker regards it, so the same words now carry more. The poem makes a circle that does not resolve.

Where might it be weaker? One criticism is that the speaker can seem a little too pleased with himself. His whimsical talk of elves and his sly remarks are charming, but there is a note of condescension that the poem may not fully control. The phrase "old-stone savage" is a loaded insult, and some readers think the poem endorses it, though a better reading treats it as the speaker's own unreliable view. The poem is more interesting if you read it that way, but it never makes this unmistakable, and the result is that readers can end up on either side.

There is also a danger in the central image. A wall as a symbol of separation is so universal and so ready to hand that the poem is easily turned into a slogan. That is not entirely Frost's fault, but a more ambiguous image might have resisted being flattened into an argument. And a few moments, like the gap-making "something" that the speaker teases us with, lean slightly toward folksy whimsy, a quality that Frost's critics have sometimes found too cozy.

Still, for sheer control of tone, the poem is hard to beat. It manages to be funny, tender, argumentative and slightly sinister in the same breath, and it leaves the reader to decide. Few poems hold that balance so naturally, and that is why it keeps being reread.

III. The Impact It Had

"Mending Wall" is the opening poem of North of Boston, and that placement matters. The book was published in London in 1914 by David Nutt, and in America the next year by Henry Holt. It made Frost's name. After years of rejection at home, he became a recognized poet, first through English reviews and then back in the United States. The collection's dramatic dialogues and monologues about rural New England life, with "Mending Wall" setting the tone at the front, gave American poetry a new voice: plain, colloquial, dramatic and rooted in the actual speech of farmers and neighbors.

Over the following decades Frost became the most famous American poet of his time, winning four Pulitzer Prizes and reading at John F. Kennedy's inauguration in 1961. "Mending Wall" became one of his signature poems, turning up in countless anthologies and school curricula. For many students it is the first poem they study in depth, and the line about good fences is among the best-known in American poetry.

The poem's reach beyond the classroom comes largely through that proverb. The phrase has been quoted in debates about property lines, national borders, immigration and international politics. It has been invoked to justify barriers and, just as often, cited ironically to argue against them. It has come up in discussions of the Berlin Wall and in more recent arguments over border walls. Very often the people quoting it cite only the neighbor's line, which is, in a sense, the opposite of the speaker's view. That pattern, in which a poem's most quotable line is detached from its irony, is itself a measure of the poem's reach.

Among writers, Frost's use of blank verse that follows speech, and his habit of building a poem around a small dramatic encounter, influenced later poets who wanted to write accessible but serious work. Seamus Heaney, who wrote admiringly about Frost, is one poet often discussed in this connection, as are Richard Wilbur and others who worked in traditional forms while keeping a conversational ease.

The poem has also found a second life in conversations about conflict and cooperation. People who work on community disputes, urban planning and diplomacy sometimes quote it because it captures a real human dilemma, that boundaries can protect relationships and can also freeze them. It shows up in business writing and sermons, often cheerfully misread. That trail of casual quotation can be annoying to scholars, but it shows how deeply the poem has entered everyday thought, which is a rare kind of impact for a poem about repairing stones.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved "Mending Wall" first for its voice. From the first lines you feel you are in the company of a particular person, wry, observant, a little mischievous, who is quite aware of his own foolishness. That is a hard thing to do in a poem, and Frost does it so easily that people often forget how artful it is. The speaker's jokes about spells and elves make the poem enjoyable on the very first reading, even for people who think they don't like poetry.

They have also loved the sheer physical truth of it. Anyone who has handled rough stones, or dealt with the way frozen ground heaves in spring, recognizes the details. "We wear our fingers rough with handling them" is the kind of line that makes you feel the work. The poem respects labor, and it takes ordinary farm tasks seriously as a subject for art. For rural readers in particular, it is a rare case of a literary poem that gets their world right.

Another reason for its popularity is that it contains an argument but does not settle it. People love to debate the poem. Is Frost on the side of the wall or against it? Is the neighbor wise or stubborn? Is the speaker a thoughtful skeptic or a bit of a show-off? Readers have found it satisfying to argue about, and teachers find it a reliable way to start a lively discussion. A poem that invites participation, instead of handing down a lesson, tends to last.

Many have admired the way the poem treats the ordinary idea of neighborliness with complexity. Most of us have had a relationship in which we were annoyed by a habit we couldn't change, and in which the other person's certainty was both irritating and a little admirable. The poem captures that affectionate friction. The neighbor is not a villain. There is even a trace of respect in the image of him carrying stones with such seriousness.

And finally, readers love the line "Something there is that doesn't love a wall." It is a mysterious and musical sentence, with its odd inverted word order that sounds both old and new. Poets and general readers alike often cite it as one of the great opening lines in American verse. It sets up a sense of larger forces at work, beyond human intention, and it gives the whole poem a hint of the uncanny that lifts it above a simple country anecdote.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The criticisms of "Mending Wall" are mostly about how it has been read rather than how it was written, though a few concern the poem itself.

The most common complaint is about the public misunderstanding of the poem. Many people take it as a straightforward endorsement of fences, because the line "good fences make good neighbors" is so quotable. Critics and teachers have spent a great deal of energy pointing out that the speaker is skeptical of the saying, and that the poem is at least as much a questioning of walls as a celebration of them. At the same time, other critics have pushed back on the opposite oversimplification, the idea that the poem is a plain antiwall statement. Frost himself was known for being slippery about his meanings, and the poem does not simply side with the speaker. The speaker, after all, joins in the repair.

Some critics have taken a harder line on the speaker. They argue that his condescension toward the neighbor, culminating in the "old-stone savage" image, reveals a snobbery that the poem exposes rather than shares. Others feel that Frost himself shares it, and that the poem's tone toward its rural neighbor is uncomfortably superior. This disagreement is unresolved, and it is fair to say that the poem is unsettled on this point.

Frost's work in general drew skeptical remarks from critics who found his reputation as a folksy, wise New England farmer too neat. In the middle of the twentieth century, the critic Randall Jarrell argued that Frost's darker poems were his best and that the public image of the cozy sage had obscured them. Lionel Trilling, in a famous 1959 speech at Frost's eighty-fifth birthday dinner, called him a terrifying poet, which unsettled many in the room. Both critics, in different ways, were pushing back against a comfortable reading of poems like this one. If the poem seems like a pleasant country chat, they suggested, you may not be reading it closely enough.

There have also been complaints that the poem's central metaphor is too tidy and its ending too gentle to bear the weight of the political arguments it has been used in. And some modern readers, particularly those thinking about real walls that separate families and communities, find the poem's playful tone unequal to the stakes. Frost was writing about a stone wall between two farms, not a border between nations, and applying the poem to the latter can feel like stretching it past what it can bear.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"Mending Wall" belongs to a tradition that might be called American pastoral modernism. The pastoral is an old literary mode that looks at country life, often with an eye to larger truths. Frost took that ancient form and gave it a modern edge: his farmers are not idealized shepherds but real people with sore hands and stubborn opinions, and his landscapes hold unease as well as beauty. In this he stands in the line of Virgil, Wordsworth and the New England writers like Emerson and Thoreau, but he also fits the early twentieth century's push to make poetry sound like modern speech. He did this in a very different way from experimental contemporaries like Ezra Pound or T. S. Eliot. While they broke old forms apart, Frost kept traditional meter and rhyme and bent them toward ordinary talk. He once compared writing free verse to playing tennis without a net, which tells you where he stood.

Within his own book, "Mending Wall" speaks to the other poems in North of Boston, which are full of neighbors who cannot quite understand one another, such as "The Death of the Hired Man" and "Home Burial." The book as a whole is about the gaps between people, and the wall is its central image.

Looking at comparable works, the poem has natural companions in other literature about boundaries. The English tradition has many fence-and-hedge poems, and the notion of a limit that both protects and divides appears in countless later stories about borders and property. Frost's own proverb is much older than the poem, and he did not invent it, which is part of its charm: he took a piece of folk wisdom and put it under a gentle pressure.

What descends from it is a whole stream of American poetry that finds drama in a conversation. You can see something of it in Heaney's treatments of rural labor, and in many contemporary poets who use plain speech for complicated feelings.

Why read it today? Because it is a model of how to think about something you disagree with. The speaker does not storm in and demand the wall come down. He wonders, teases, and also helps. In an age that tends to argue in slogans, a poem that holds two views in tension, and that asks us to look at our own habits of mind, is remarkably useful. It also reminds us that the walls we keep are often inherited, repaired by habit, and that the real question is not whether to have boundaries but whether we have asked what each one is keeping in and out.
