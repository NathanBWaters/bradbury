How Aftershocks of the 1989 Loma Prieta Earthquake Handed Pier 39's K-Dock to 1,500 California Sea Lions

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk out to the western edge of **Pier 39** along the northern Embarcadero at Beach Street (37.8100° N, 122.4104° W) and you will hear the raucous, barking chorus of **K-Dock** three blocks before you see the water. Until autumn 1989, K-Dock was a quiet wood-and-concrete floating slip marina for pleasure yachts, while San Francisco's California sea lions (*Zalophus californianus*) hauled out almost exclusively on the wave-lashed offshore crags of **Seal Rocks** below the Cliff House. Then, shortly after the **October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake** (magnitude 6.9) emptied K-Dock for pier repairs and coincided with a massive winter run of Pacific herring inside the bay, ten sea lions climbed onto the flat floating docks—multiplying within weeks to over 500 and reaching a peak of 1,701 by 2009.

1. Pinniped Ecology: How to Tell a California Sea Lion from a Harbor Seal

Visitors standing above K-Dock often call the animals 'seals,' yet every mammal barking on those pontoons is an eared sea lion (*Zalophus californianus*, family Otariidae) rather than a true earless harbor seal (*Phoca vitulina*, family Phocidae). Unlike quiet, spotted harbor seals that wriggle on their bellies at Castro Rocks or Coyote Point, California sea lions have visible external ear flaps, communicate with loud territorial barks, rotate their hind flippers forward under their hips to 'walk' and climb onto docks, and exhibit dramatic sexual dimorphism—with mature males developing a pronounced forehead sagittal crest crowned with lighter fur. Why did the January 1990 colonization of K-Dock happen when it did, and why does the herd size fluctuate from a few dozen in June to over 1,000 in winter and spring? Decades of federal protection under the 1972 Marine Mammal Protection Act quadrupled the Eastern Pacific sea lion population, while the seasonal migration of K-Dock's predominantly male herd tracks two biological rhythms: winter/spring baitfish runs of Pacific herring (*Clupea pallasii*) and northern anchovy (*Engraulis mordax*) inside San Francisco Bay, and the June–July breeding migration south to the Channel Islands.
