How Aftershocks of the 1989 Loma Prieta Earthquake Handed Pier 39's K-Dock to 1,500 California Sea Lions

San Francisco — Why Zalophus californianus abandoned Seal Rocks for an empty yacht marina after October 1989—and why the Marine Mammal Center told harbor masters to move the boats instead of the pinnipeds (Post-Loma Prieta Ecological Shift (October 1989 – January 1990 to Present)) • 8 min read

“The boat owners wanted their slips back, and we looked at the numbers and the Marine Mammal Protection Act and said: the sea lions own K-Dock now.” — Sheila Chandor, Pier 39 Harbor Master (1990)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk out to the western edge of Pier 39 along the northern Embarcadero at Beach Street and you will hear the raucous, barking chorus of K-Dock three blocks before you see the water. Until autumn 1989, K-Dock was a quiet wood-and-concrete floating slip marina for pleasure yachts, while San Francisco's California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) hauled out almost exclusively on the wave-lashed offshore crags of Seal Rocks below the Cliff House. Then, shortly after the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (magnitude 6.9) emptied K-Dock for pier repairs and coincided with a massive winter run of Pacific herring inside the bay, ten sea lions climbed onto the flat floating docks—multiplying within weeks to over 500 and reaching a peak of 1,701 by 2009.

For more than a century prior to 1989, if a visitor to San Francisco wanted to see wild pinnipeds—California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) and gigantic Steller sea lions (Eumetopias jubatus)—there was only one famous spot in town: the wave-battered basalt outcrops of Seal Rocks, four hundred feet offshore from the Cliff House at Point Lobos on the open Pacific coast. Why had sea lions avoided hauling out inside San Francisco Bay for most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries? Throughout the 1800s, commercial hunters slaughtered thousands of sea lions for oil and hides until state protections intervened; later, industrial ship traffic, raw sewage, and the absence of low, accessible floating structures kept the animals out on the rocky ocean perimeter.

In 1972, Congress fundamentally changed the relationship between coastal cities and marine wildlife by passing the landmark Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA)—making it a federal crime to hunt, harass, capture, or disturb any wild seal, sea lion, whale, or otter in United States waters. As California sea lion populations along the Pacific coast rebounded dramatically under the protection of the 1972 MMPA—growing from roughly 50,000 animals in the early 1970s to nearly 200,000 by the late 1980s—sixty-five-million-year-old bay food webs were also recovering thanks to Clean Water Act municipal wastewater treatment plants. Meanwhile, in 1978, developer Warren Simmons had converted an old 1905 cargo wharf on the northern waterfront into the festival marketplace and 350-berth yacht marina of Pier 39, protected on its western flank by a long timber breakwater that damped down the rough Golden Gate swell.

Then, at 5:04 p.m. on October 17, 1989, the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta earthquake shook the San Francisco waterfront, prompting boat owners on Pier 39's exposed western K-Dock to move their yachts temporarily while engineers inspected pilings and repaired utility hookups. Did submarine seismic tremors out at Seal Rocks frighten the sea lions through the Golden Gate, or did the empty, quiet wooden slips of K-Dock simply offer an irresistible opportunity? Marine biologists point to a convergence of three irresistible factors in late autumn 1989. First, K-Dock's low-freeboard floating wooden fingers sat inside a calm breakwater basin completely shielded from the violent winter Pacific storm surf that pounded Seal Rocks, and inside the Golden Gate there were virtually zero great white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias) or orcas.

Second, the winter of 1989–1990 brought an extraordinarily dense spawning run of Pacific herring (Clupea pallasii) and northern anchovies right past Alcatraz and Fisherman's Wharf: for a 700-pound male California sea lion that burns massive calories diving for fish, K-Dock was like moving your bedroom right next door to an all-you-can-eat buffet. Third, because K-Dock was temporarily emptied of yachts and human foot traffic after the October 1989 earthquake, the first pioneer bachelor sea lions who hopped onto the floating wooden walkways in late 1989 met zero resistance—and because California sea lions are intensely gregarious, thigmotactic animals that love to pile on top of one another for warmth and safety, their loud barks acted as an acoustic beacon across the bay.

In the first week of January 1990, Pier 39 Harbor Master Sheila Chandor counted a dozen sea lions lounging across K-Dock; within fourteen days the count passed 150; by February 1990, more than 400 massive, barking pinnipeds—mostly juvenile and sub-adult males up to 850 pounds—completely covered every square foot of K-Dock. Under the staggering combined weight of more than 100 tons of blubber and muscle, the original wooden marina fingers of K-Dock began sinking below the waterline, snapping utility pipes, fouling the slips, and triggering furious complaints from yacht owners who demanded that Pier 39 management chase the animals away.

Harbor Master Sheila Chandor and Pier 39 executives reached out for scientific guidance to The Marine Mammal Center in the Marin Headlands—whose researchers explained two decisive truths: under the federal Marine Mammal Protection Act it was illegal to harass the sea lions off the dock, and any attempt to haze them would fail because K-Dock was the safest, richest winter haul-out habitat in Northern California. Demonstrating remarkable civic wisdom, Pier 39 and Sheila Chandor made a permanent pact with nature in the spring of 1990: they permanently relocated all thirty-six displaced yacht owners from K-Dock to other slips in the marina, removed the boat cleats and utility boxes, and rebuilt K-Dock out of sixty heavy-duty, extra-buoyant twelve-by-twenty-four-foot timber-and-concrete pontoons engineered specifically for sea lions.

Each June, most of K-Dock's adult male sea lions swim 350 miles south to the Channel Islands off Santa Barbara (San Miguel and San Nicolas islands) for the summer breeding season before returning to Pier 39 in August—with the colony hitting an all-time record of 1,701 sea lions in November 2009 and surging over 1,000 animals again in spring 2024 during a booming anchovy run. Lean over the wooden railing above K-Dock today as two whiskered bulls chest-bump for the sunniest corner of a floating pontoon while naturalist docents from the Sea Lion Center explain pinniped biology to children from around the globe, and you witness San Francisco's happiest unintended consequence of the 1989 earthquake.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Pinniped Ecology: How to Tell a California Sea Lion from a Harbor Seal

Visitors standing above K-Dock often call the animals 'seals,' yet every mammal barking on those pontoons is an eared sea lion (Zalophus californianus, family Otariidae) rather than a true earless harbor seal (Phoca vitulina, family Phocidae). Unlike quiet, spotted harbor seals that wriggle on their bellies at Castro Rocks or Coyote Point, California sea lions have visible external ear flaps, communicate with loud territorial barks, rotate their hind flippers forward under their hips to 'walk' and climb onto docks, and exhibit dramatic sexual dimorphism—with mature males developing a pronounced forehead sagittal crest crowned with lighter fur.

2. The 1972 Marine Mammal Protection Act and Pacific Herring Runs

Why did the January 1990 colonization of K-Dock happen when it did, and why does the herd size fluctuate from a few dozen in June to over 1,000 in winter and spring? Decades of federal protection under the 1972 Marine Mammal Protection Act quadrupled the Eastern Pacific sea lion population, while the seasonal migration of K-Dock's predominantly male herd tracks two biological rhythms: winter/spring baitfish runs of Pacific herring (Clupea pallasii) and northern anchovy (Engraulis mordax) inside San Francisco Bay, and the June–July breeding migration south to the Channel Islands.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three anchors in view: first, Seal Rocks below the Cliff House at Point Lobos, the historic open-ocean haul-out before 1989; second, Harbor Master Sheila Chandor and The Marine Mammal Center, who convinced Pier 39 in 1990 to surrender K-Dock permanently to the sea lions; and third, the sixty custom twelve-by-twenty-four-foot floating pontoons that now support up to 1,500 animals.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Ride the historic F-Market & Wharves streetcar to Beach and Stockton streets and walk out to the western promenade of Pier 39 above K-Dock. After watching the sea lions jostle for sunny spots on the floating pontoons against the backdrop of Alcatraz and the Golden Gate Bridge, walk upstairs to the free Sea Lion Center operated by the Bay.org Aquarium of the Bay to touch sea lion skull casts and pelt samples.
