Alyosha the Pot

Leo Tolstoy (1905) • 11 min read

“I thought you would bring me a man in Simeon’s place,” he said, scanning Alyosha; “and you’ve brought me this! What’s the good of him?”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Leo Tolstoy wrote this very short story in 1905, in his late seventies, and it was published after his death. By then he was the most famous writer in the world, but he had spent decades turning away from his own great novels, preferring plain tales about ordinary people that a peasant could understand. Russia in 1905 was shaking with strikes, a failed revolution, and anger at the old order, and Tolstoy was convinced that real change had to begin inside the human heart. Alyosha the Pot is one of his simplest and most quietly devastating pieces. It follows a humble, uncomplaining farm boy sent to work as a servant in a merchant's house. You may find it almost too plain at first, so let the plainness work on you. Pay attention to what Alyosha is asked to do, what he is never asked about, and how he reacts. The story is really asking what a good life looks like when nobody has ever treated you as worth anything.

Alyosha was the younger brother. He was called the Pot, because his mother had once sent him with a pot of milk to the deacon’s wife, and he had stumbled against something and broken it. His mother had beaten him, and the children had teased him. Since then he was nicknamed the Pot. Alyosha was a tiny, thin little fellow, with ears like wings, and a huge nose. “Alyosha has a nose that looks like a dog on a hill!” the children used to call after him. Alyosha went to the village school, but was not good at lessons; besides, there was so little time to learn. His elder brother was in town, working for a merchant, so Alyosha had to help his father from a very early age. When he was no more than six he used to go out with the girls to watch the cows and sheep in the pasture, and a little later he looked after the horses by day and by night. And at twelve years of age he had already begun to plough and to drive the cart. The skill was there though the strength was not. He was always cheerful. Whenever the children made fun of him, he would either laugh or be silent. When his father scolded him he would stand mute and listen attentively, and as soon as the scolding was over would smile and go on with his work. Alyosha was nineteen when his brother was taken as a soldier. So his father placed him with the merchant as a yard-porter. He was given his brother’s old boots, his father’s old coat and cap, and was taken to town. Alyosha was delighted with his clothes, but the merchant was not impressed by his appearance.

“I thought you would bring me a man in Simeon’s place,” he said, scanning Alyosha; “and you’ve brought me this! What’s the good of him?”

“He can do everything; look after horses and drive. He’s a good one to work. He looks rather thin, but he’s tough enough. And he’s very willing.”

“He looks it. All right; we’ll see what we can do with him.”

So Alyosha remained at the merchant’s.

The family was not a large one. It consisted of the merchant’s wife: her old mother: a married son poorly educated who was in his father’s business: another son, a learned one who had finished school and entered the University, but having been expelled, was living at home: and a daughter who still went to school.

They did not take to Alyosha at first. He was uncouth, badly dressed, and had no manner, but they soon got used to him. Alyosha worked even better than his brother had done; he was really very willing. They sent him on all sorts of errands, but he did everything quickly and readily, going from one task to another without stopping. And so here, just as at home, all the work was put upon his shoulders. The more he did, the more he was given to do. His mistress, her old mother, the son, the daughter, the clerk, and the cook⁠—all ordered him about, and sent him from one place to another.

“Alyosha, do this! Alyosha, do that! What! have you forgotten, Alyosha? Mind you don’t forget, Alyosha!” was heard from morning till night. And Alyosha ran here, looked after this and that, forgot nothing, found time for everything, and was always cheerful.

His brother’s old boots were soon worn out, and his master scolded him for going about in tatters with his toes sticking out. He ordered another pair to be bought for him in the market. Alyosha was delighted with his new boots, but was angry with his feet when they ached at the end of the day after so much running about. And then he was afraid that his father would be annoyed when he came to town for his wages, to find that his master had deducted the cost of the boots.

In the winter Alyosha used to get up before daybreak. He would chop the wood, sweep the yard, feed the cows and horses, light the stoves, clean the boots, prepare the samovars and polish them afterwards; or the clerk would get him to bring up the goods; or the cook would set him to knead the bread and clean the saucepans. Then he was sent to town on various errands, to bring the daughter home from school, or to get some olive oil for the old mother. “Why the devil have you been so long?” first one, then another, would say to him. Why should they go? Alyosha can go. “Alyosha! Alyosha!” And Alyosha ran here and there. He breakfasted in snatches while he was working, and rarely managed to get his dinner at the proper hour. The cook used to scold him for being late, but she was sorry for him all the same, and would keep something hot for his dinner and supper.

At holiday times there was more work than ever, but Alyosha liked holidays because everybody gave him a tip. Not much certainly, but it would amount up to about sixty kopecks⁠—his very own money. For Alyosha never set eyes on his wages. His father used to come and take them from the merchant, and only scold Alyosha for wearing out his boots.

When he had saved up two roubles, by the advice of the cook he bought himself a red knitted jacket, and was so happy when he put it on, that he couldn’t close his mouth for joy. Alyosha was not talkative; when he spoke at all, he spoke abruptly, with his head turned away. When told to do anything, or asked if he could do it, he would say yes without the smallest hesitation, and set to work at once.

Alyosha did not know any prayer; and had forgotten what his mother had taught him. But he prayed just the same, every morning and every evening, prayed with his hands, crossing himself.

He lived like this for about a year and a half, and towards the end of the second year a most startling thing happened to him. He discovered one day, to his great surprise, that, in addition to the relation of usefulness existing between people, there was also another, a peculiar relation of quite a different character. Instead of a man being wanted to clean boots, and go on errands and harness horses, he is not wanted to be of any service at all, but another human being wants to serve him and pet him. Suddenly Alyosha felt he was such a man.

He made this discovery through the cook Ustinia. She was young, had no parents, and worked as hard as Alyosha. He felt for the first time in his life that he⁠—not his services, but he himself⁠—was necessary to another human being. When his mother used to be sorry for him, he had taken no notice of her. It had seemed to him quite natural, as though he were feeling sorry for himself. But here was Ustinia, a perfect stranger, and sorry for him. She would save him some hot porridge, and sit watching him, her chin propped on her bare arm, with the sleeve rolled up, while he was eating it. When he looked at her she would begin to laugh, and he would laugh too.

This was such a new, strange thing to him that it frightened Alyosha. He feared that it might interfere with his work. But he was pleased, nevertheless, and when he glanced at the trousers that Ustinia had mended for him, he would shake his head and smile. He would often think of her while at work, or when running on errands. “A fine girl, Ustinia!” he sometimes exclaimed.

Ustinia used to help him whenever she could, and he helped her. She told him all about her life; how she had lost her parents; how her aunt had taken her in and found a place for her in the town; how the merchant’s son had tried to take liberties with her, and how she had rebuffed him. She liked to talk, and Alyosha liked to listen to her. He had heard that peasants who came up to work in the towns frequently got married to servant girls. On one occasion she asked him if his parents intended marrying him soon. He said that he did not know; that he did not want to marry any of the village girls.

“Have you taken a fancy to someone, then?”

“I would marry you, if you’d be willing.”

“Get along with you, Alyosha the Pot; but you’ve found your tongue, haven’t you?” she exclaimed, slapping him on the back with a towel she held in her hand. “Why shouldn’t I?”

At Shrovetide Alyosha’s father came to town for his wages. It had come to the ears of the merchant’s wife that Alyosha wanted to marry Ustinia, and she disapproved of it. “What will be the use of her with a baby?” she thought, and informed her husband.

The merchant gave the old man Alyosha’s wages.

“How is my lad getting on?” he asked. “I told you he was willing.”

“That’s all right, as far as it goes, but he’s taken some sort of nonsense into his head. He wants to marry our cook. Now I don’t approve of married servants. We won’t have them in the house.”

“Well, now, who would have thought the fool would think of such a thing?” the old man exclaimed. “But don’t you worry. I’ll soon settle that.”

He went into the kitchen, and sat down at the table waiting for his son. Alyosha was out on an errand, and came back breathless.

“I thought you had some sense in you; but what’s this you’ve taken into your head?” his father began.

“I? Nothing.”

“How, nothing? They tell me you want to get married. You shall get married when the time comes. I’ll find you a decent wife, not some town hussy.”

His father talked and talked, while Alyosha stood still and sighed. When his father had quite finished, Alyosha smiled.

“All right. I’ll drop it.”

“Now that’s what I call sense.”

When he was left alone with Ustinia he told her what his father had said. (She had listened at the door.)

“It’s no good; it can’t come off. Did you hear? He was angry⁠—won’t have it at any price.”

Ustinia cried into her apron.

Alyosha shook his head.

“What’s to be done? We must do as we’re told.”

“Well, are you going to give up that nonsense, as your father told you?” his mistress asked, as he was putting up the shutters in the evening.

“To be sure we are,” Alyosha replied with a smile, and then burst into tears.

From that day Alyosha went about his work as usual, and no longer talked to Ustinia about their getting married. One day in Lent the clerk told him to clear the snow from the roof. Alyosha climbed on to the roof and swept away all the snow; and, while he was still raking out some frozen lumps from the gutter, his foot slipped and he fell over. Unfortunately he did not fall on the snow, but on a piece of iron over the door. Ustinia came running up, together with the merchant’s daughter.

“Have you hurt yourself, Alyosha?”

“Ah! no, it’s nothing.”

But he could not raise himself when he tried to, and began to smile.

He was taken into the lodge. The doctor arrived, examined him, and asked where he felt the pain.

“I feel it all over,” he said. “But it doesn’t matter. I’m only afraid master will be annoyed. Father ought to be told.”

Alyosha lay in bed for two days, and on the third day they sent for the priest.

“Are you really going to die?” Ustinia asked.

“Of course I am. You can’t go on living forever. You must go when the time comes.” Alyosha spoke rapidly as usual. “Thank you, Ustinia. You’ve been very good to me. What a lucky thing they didn’t let us marry! Where should we have been now? It’s much better as it is.”

When the priest came, he prayed with his bands and with his heart. “As it is good here when you obey and do no harm to others, so it will be there,” was the thought within it.

He spoke very little; he only said he was thirsty, and he seemed full of wonder at something.

He lay in wonderment, then stretched himself, and died.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, Alyosha the Pot is about as simple as a story can be. A boy gets a nickname after breaking a pot of milk. He grows up working for his father, then for a merchant in town. He falls in love with the cook, Ustinia. His father and his employers forbid the marriage. He falls off a roof while clearing snow, and he dies. Nothing clever happens. Tolstoy deliberately strips away plot, suspense, and ornament, because he wants you to look straight at one human being.

The first thing to notice is how Alyosha is used. Everyone in the merchant's household orders him around, and the refrain, "Alyosha, do this! Alyosha, do that!", runs from morning till night. The more he does, the more he is given to do. He never sees his own wages, because his father collects them and scolds him for wearing out his boots. Even his joy is small and practical, like being delighted with a pair of boots and angry at his feet for aching. Tolstoy is showing you a person who has been turned into a tool, and who has never thought to object.

The emotional center comes when Alyosha discovers, in Tolstoy's phrase, that besides the relation of usefulness between people, there is another, quite different one. Ustinia saves him hot porridge and watches him eat with her chin propped on her arm. For the first time he is wanted for himself, not for his services. This frightens him, because he fears it might interfere with his work. That one detail tells you how completely he has absorbed the idea that he exists to be useful.

Then the story turns. When his father and mistress shut the romance down, Alyosha says, "All right. I'll drop it," smiles, and bursts into tears. Soon after, he falls, and on his deathbed he says something that can break your heart: "What a lucky thing they didn't let us marry!" Is he being sincere? Partly. He has been trained all his life to accept whatever happens, and the acceptance has become his only form of dignity. The reader sees what he cannot, that he is thanking people for a loss.

The ending is the subtle part. Alyosha has never learned any prayer, but he prays with his hands, crossing himself. At the end he prays "with his hands and with his heart." The thought inside it is that as it is good here when you obey and do no harm to others, so it will be there. Then he lies in wonderment, as if he has seen something astonishing, stretches, and dies. Tolstoy leaves it open what he sees. But the wonder suggests that a life of simple obedience and goodness has brought him, at the last moment, face to face with something real.

The story therefore holds two readings together. One is an accusation: this boy was ground down by a world that took everything and gave back almost nothing. The other is a kind of reverence: this boy, without knowing it, lived out the gospel humility Tolstoy valued above wealth and cleverness. Tolstoy never tells you which to choose, and the tension between them is the point.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, Alyosha the Pot is remarkable for what it refuses to do. There are no metaphors to speak of, no inner monologues, no commentary telling you how to feel. The sentences are short and factual: he chopped the wood, swept the yard, fed the cows and horses, lit the stoves. The accumulation of chores does the emotional work. By the time you reach Ustinia you understand, without being told, why one kind word could feel like a thunderclap.

The story also has superb economy. In a few pages Tolstoy gives us a whole household, its hierarchy, and its small cruelties and kindnesses. Even minor figures, like the cook who scolds Alyosha for being late but keeps something hot for his supper, are drawn with a single touch. The deathbed scene, where Alyosha is worried that the master will be annoyed, is perhaps the most piercing moment of all, because his last anxieties are still those of a servant.

Still, a fair critic can find weaknesses. The story is nearly pure pattern, and Alyosha is so consistently good that he can feel less like a person than like an ideal. He has no flash of anger, no private resentment, no joke at the merchant's expense. Real people, even meek ones, usually have some hidden reserve of irritation, and Tolstoy withholds it. A reader may feel that Alyosha's saintliness is partly an authorial demand, a figure designed to make a moral point, in the way that some of Tolstoy's late fables are.

There is also the problem of the accident. The fall from the roof arrives with little preparation, and it solves the story's central conflict by removing the character, which can feel convenient. A skeptic might say that Tolstoy kills Alyosha because he cannot imagine a happy resolution that fits his philosophy, since marriage and a modest life with Ustinia would undercut the idea that renunciation is the path to peace. The line about the lucky escape from marriage is, on this view, uncomfortably close to the author speaking through his character.

And yet these objections are not fatal, because the story knows what it is. It does not pretend to be a realistic novel about a complicated young man. It is a parable disguised as realism, the way some of Tolstoy's folk tales are, and within that mode it is nearly perfect. The very flatness that bothers you is part of the horror: Alyosha is flat because his life has been flattened.

What keeps the story from preaching is its tact. Tolstoy never lets a character deliver the moral. The father is not a villain, only a man who wants his son to follow the rules. The mistress is not a monster, only practical. The harm comes from ordinary arrangements nobody questions. That quiet, systematic injustice is harder to dismiss than a cartoon villain would be, and it is where the story earns its power.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be an exaggeration to say Alyosha the Pot changed literature on its own. It is a minor work in the shadow of War and Peace and Anna Karenina, and it appeared only late, after Tolstoy's death, so it never had the direct public effect of his novels or his essays. Its impact has been quieter, and mostly a matter of how it shows up in the story of Tolstoy's final period and in anthologies of short fiction.

Within Tolstoy's own career, it belongs to the long late phase that began after his religious crisis in the late 1870s. After producing two of the greatest novels ever written, he came to believe that elaborate art for educated people was a kind of vanity. He wanted to write for ordinary readers, in language they could use, and about moral life in its plainest form. Stories like this one, along with The Death of Ivan Ilyich, Master and Man, and the folk tales, belong to that program. Alyosha is the extreme version, where almost everything that makes his big novels dazzling has been deliberately removed.

For other writers, Tolstoy's late simplicity was a challenge and a model. Chekhov, who admired Tolstoy's moral seriousness but distrusted his preaching, wrote his own short stories about forgotten servants and peasants, often with similar compassion and with far less doctrine. Gorky and later Russian realists worked in the same territory. In the English-speaking world, writers who cared about plain style, such as Hemingway and others who cut ornament to the bone, read translations of Tolstoy's short fiction, though it is hard to trace precise lines of influence for this one story.

The story has also gained a life in teaching. Because it is short, translations are easy to find and often free, and because it can be read in ten minutes, it is a favorite for classes on the short story, Russian literature, or ethics. Teachers use it to open questions about class, obedience, and what makes a life valuable. It pairs naturally with works about servitude and sacrifice.

Its largest influence may be conceptual. It helped fix an image of Tolstoy as a writer who could, when he chose, compress a whole moral philosophy into a single unassuming person. The nickname Alyosha also carries a faint echo for readers of Russian literature. Dostoevsky gave the same name to the gentle youngest Karamazov, and the name has long suggested sweetness and humility in Russian culture, though Tolstoy's Alyosha is a far humbler figure.

Finally, the story belongs to a long tradition of stories about the invisible poor, and its quiet protest contributed to a Russian literary habit of treating servants and peasants as full moral beings. It does not seem to have been adapted widely, but that makes sense: its power depends on its restraint, and film or theater would be tempted to add the drama Tolstoy took out.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who love Alyosha the Pot tend to talk about one thing first: how much it makes them feel with so little. Many describe reading it quickly and then sitting very still afterward. The story is so plain that it seems to have no tricks, and then the last paragraph arrives, and the plainness itself turns out to have been the trick.

A great deal of the affection centers on Alyosha himself. He is cheerful, hard-working, and endlessly agreeable, and yet he never becomes a cartoon of cheerfulness. We believe the joy he takes in a red knitted jacket he bought with two roubles of his own tip money. We believe the way he laughs when the children tease him, or says yes without hesitating when asked if he can do anything. These small touches make him feel like someone you might actually know.

Readers also love the love story, small as it is. The scene where Ustinia watches him eat, and he looks up, and they both laugh, is one of the most tender in all of Tolstoy's short work. It is so understated that it feels like a private moment you have accidentally witnessed. The proposal, awkward and abrupt, with Alyosha saying, "I would marry you, if you'd be willing," and Ustinia slapping him with a towel, has the comic warmth of real life.

Another thing people prize is the story's moral seriousness without sermonizing. Tolstoy was famous for lecturing, and some of his late works lecture hard. Here he lets the facts speak. Readers across religious and political positions can find themselves moved: religious readers may see a quiet portrait of Christian humility, while others see a stinging picture of how class society wastes people.

Then there is the ending. Many readers remember the final sentences best: Alyosha spoke very little, he seemed full of wonder at something, he stretched himself, and died. It is a very Tolstoyan death, brief and unadorned, and recalls the famous deaths in his longer works, where the moment of dying is described with startling calm. Admirers often say this ending gives the story its dignity, because it neither sentimentalizes the death nor pretends it was meaningless.

Writers have often admired this kind of Tolstoy for the discipline it shows. A novelist who could write enormous scenes of war and ballrooms chose, at the end of his life, to write about a boy nobody noticed, and to do it in a few pages. That choice, more than any single sentence, is part of what readers cherish. It suggests that attention is itself a moral act, and that the person who looks closely at the overlooked is doing something close to love.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because Alyosha the Pot is a minor and fairly late work, there is not a big record of famous reviews to lean on. What we can say is that the story has met the same kinds of objections that Tolstoy's late fiction as a whole has met, and that thoughtful readers have raised them for generations.

The most common complaint is about sentimentality and didacticism. Critics, including admirers of Tolstoy, have worried that in his later years he stopped writing characters and started writing exemplars. Alyosha, on this view, is not a person but a demonstration of a thesis, the thesis being that meekness and obedience lead to inner peace. When the story ends with Alyosha thanking everyone and calling it lucky that he was not allowed to marry, some readers hear Tolstoy's own doctrine of renunciation more than a believable young man.

A related and sharper objection is political and moral. If the story is a celebration of Alyosha's patient acceptance, it can look like an endorsement of exploitation. Here is a boy whose wages are taken by his father, who is worked from before dawn to night, who is forbidden to marry, and who dies without complaint. A reader might ask whether praising such passivity is a way of excusing the people who profit from it. Tolstoy, a landowner by birth who gave up much of his privilege, was himself criticized from both left and right: radicals thought his Christian nonresistance was an evasion of real injustice, while conservatives thought his attacks on property and authority were dangerous. This story has plenty of material for either side.

Defenders answer that the story does not endorse the exploitation at all. The details are damning, and the mistress's coldly practical reasoning about the cook's future baby is exposed for what it is. Yet the ambiguity is real, and it is fair to say that the ending can be read as either a critique or a consolation.

Others have found the plot mechanics too thin. The accident is abrupt, and the father's intervention is a little schematic. Some have also suggested that a story so committed to understatement risks seeming slight, a sketch rather than a finished work. And because Tolstoy was working in his very late style, a few critics regard the stories of this period as lesser than the great novels, as if a master had chosen to work in a deliberately smaller key.

None of these complaints is frivolous. They are best understood as the price of Tolstoy's method. A story that refuses complexity gains in clarity and loses in surprise, and a story that trusts the reader to feel the injustice may be misread as approving it.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Alyosha the Pot sits at the meeting point of two big currents in Russian writing: the psychological realism that Tolstoy himself helped perfect, and the moral parable, the old tradition of the short tale told to teach. It is realism in its surface, with a real village, real wages, real boots, and a real merchant's household. But it is a parable in its shape, aimed at a single lesson about how we value people.

Behind it stands a long line of Russian fiction about the small, overlooked servant. Pushkin's Station Master and Gogol's The Overcoat both made a humble, powerless man the center of a story, and both evoked pity rather than scorn. Turgenev's Sportsman's Sketches portrayed serfs as human beings, and its effect on public feeling was considerable. Tolstoy takes that tradition and strips it of irony. Where Gogol's clerk is comic and grotesque, Alyosha is simply good, and the story trusts that goodness to carry the weight.

In Tolstoy's own work, the closest relatives are Master and Man, where a rich man learns something at the moment of death, and The Death of Ivan Ilyich, which also treats dying as a revelation. The contrast is instructive. Ivan Ilyich is a respectable official who has lived badly and wakes up too late. Alyosha has lived humbly and meekly, and his death seems to carry no terror at all. If you read the two together, you see Tolstoy's conviction that those at the bottom of the social ladder sometimes understand life better than those at the top.

Looking forward, the story's descendants are the many short stories of ordinary lives told in plain language. Chekhov's tales of servants, schoolteachers, and clerks, Joyce's Dubliners, and the work of writers like Isaac Babel and later Raymond Carver all share a belief that a small life can bear enormous meaning. Not all of them share Tolstoy's moral certainty, and many would have found his preaching oppressive, but the practice of looking hard at the overlooked owes something to him.

Today the story matters for a simple reason: it still asks a question we avoid. Whom do we treat as useful and nothing more? The modern world has its own Alyoshas, among delivery workers, cleaners, and unpaid helpers, who are praised for being reliable and never asked what they want. You do not have to share Tolstoy's religion or his belief in nonresistance to feel that question land. A story of a few pages, written by an old man in a hurry to say what he meant, can still make you look up from the page and notice who has been carrying things for you.
