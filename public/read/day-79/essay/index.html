A Winter’s Walk in Carrick and Galloway

Robert Louis Stevenson (1876) • 19 min read

Through frosted glens and beside dark Scottish sea-lochs in January, the solitary walker tastes the ancient quiet of the winter earth.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Robert Louis Stevenson wrote A Winter's Walk in Carrick and Galloway in the mid-1870s, when he was in his mid-twenties, a young Edinburgh lawyer-in-name who was really becoming a professional writer. It describes a cold January walk along the Ayrshire coast in the southwest of Scotland, Robert Burns country, the week after the New Year. What makes it interesting is that Stevenson is not writing a cheerful guidebook. He sees the beauty of snow on ruined castles, but also the poverty of an old man gathering cockles, the grim history of a castle where a man was roasted over a fire, and the heavy drinking in a market town. The piece mixes landscape painting, social observation, history and dry humour, and it shows him learning to turn a walk into literature. What you are given here is the first part of a longer essay, so it stops mid-journey. Listen for the sharp little portraits of people, and for the moment the weather changes.

At the famous bridge of Doon, Kyle, the central district of the shire of Ayr, marches with Carrick, the most southerly. On the Carrick side of the river rises a hill of somewhat gentle conformation, cleft with shallow dells, and sown here and there with farms and tufts of wood. Inland, it loses itself, joining, I suppose, the great herd of similar hills that occupies the centre of the Lowlands. Towards the sea it swells out the coastline into a protuberance, like a bay-window in a plan, and is fortified against the surf behind bold crags. This hill is known as the Brown Hill of Carrick, or, more shortly, Brown Carrick.

It had snowed overnight. The fields were all sheeted up; they were tucked in among the snow, and their shape was modelled through the pliant counterpane, like children tucked in by a fond mother. The wind had made ripples and folds upon the surface, like what the sea, in quiet weather, leaves upon the sand. There was a frosty stifle in the air. An effusion of coppery light on the summit of Brown Carrick showed where the sun was trying to look through; but along the horizon clouds of cold fog had settled down, so that there was no distinction of sky and sea. Over the white shoulders of the headlands, or in the opening of bays, there was nothing but a great vacancy and blackness; and the road as it drew near the edge of the cliff seemed to skirt the shores of creation and void space.

The snow crunched under foot, and at farms all the dogs broke out barking as they smelt a passerby upon the road. I met a fine old fellow, who might have sat as the father in “The Cottar’s Saturday Night,” and who swore most heathenishly at a cow he was driving. And a little after I scraped acquaintance with a poor body tramping out to gather cockles. His face was wrinkled by exposure; it was broken up into flakes and channels, like mud beginning to dry, and weathered in two colours, an incongruous pink and grey. He had a faint air of being surprised⁠—which, God knows, he might well be⁠—that life had gone so ill with him. The shape of his trousers was in itself a jest, so strangely were they bagged and ravelled about his knees; and his coat was all bedaubed with clay as tough he had lain in a rain-dub during the New Year’s festivity. I will own I was not sorry to think he had had a merry New Year, and been young again for an evening; but I was sorry to see the mark still there. One could not expect such an old gentleman to be much of a dandy or a great student of respectability in dress; but there might have been a wife at home, who had brushed out similar stains after fifty New Years, now become old, or a round-armed daughter, who would wish to have him neat, were it only out of self-respect and for the ploughman sweetheart when he looks round at night. Plainly, there was nothing of this in his life, and years and loneliness hung heavily on his old arms. He was seventy-six, he told me; and nobody would give a day’s work to a man that age: they would think he couldn’t do it. “And, ’deed,” he went on, with a sad little chuckle, “ ’deed, I doubt if I could.” He said goodbye to me at a footpath, and crippled wearily off to his work. It will make your heart ache if you think of his old fingers groping in the snow.

He told me I was to turn down beside the schoolhouse for Dunure. And so, when I found a lone house among the snow, and heard a babble of childish voices from within, I struck off into a steep road leading downwards to the sea. Dunure lies close under the steep hill: a haven among the rocks, a breakwater in consummate disrepair, much apparatus for drying nets, and a score or so of fishers’ houses. Hard by, a few shards of ruined castle overhang the sea, a few vaults, and one tall gable honeycombed with windows. The snow lay on the beach to the tidemark. It was daubed on to the sills of the ruin: it roosted in the crannies of the rock like white seabirds; even on outlying reefs there would be a little cock of snow, like a toy lighthouse. Everything was grey and white in a cold and dolorous sort of shepherd’s plaid. In the profound silence, broken only by the noise of oars at sea, a horn was sounded twice; and I saw the postman, girt with two bags, pause a moment at the end of the clachan for letters. It is, perhaps, characteristic of Dunure that none were brought him.

The people at the public-house did not seem well pleased to see me, and though I would fain have stayed by the kitchen fire, sent me “ben the hoose” into the guestroom. This guestroom at Dunure was painted in quite aesthetic fashion. There are rooms in the same taste not a hundred miles from London, where persons of an extreme sensibility meet together without embarrassment. It was all in a fine dull bottle-green and black; a grave harmonious piece of colouring, with nothing, so far as coarser folk can judge, to hurt the better feelings of the most exquisite purist. A cherry-red half window-blind kept up an imaginary warmth in the cold room, and threw quite a glow on the floor. Twelve cockleshells and a halfpenny china figure were ranged solemnly along the mantelshelf. Even the spittoon was an original note, and instead of sawdust contained seashells. And as for the hearthrug, it would merit an article to itself, and a coloured diagram to help the text. It was patchwork, but the patchwork of the poor; no glowing shreds of old brocade and Chinese silk, shaken together in the kaleidoscope of some tasteful housewife’s fancy; but a work of art in its own way, and plainly a labour of love. The patches came exclusively from people’s raiment. There was no colour more brilliant than a heather mixture; “My Johnny’s grey breeks,” well polished over the oar on the boat’s thwart, entered largely into its composition. And the spoils of an old black cloth coat, that had been many a Sunday to church, added something (save the mark!) of preciousness to the material.

While I was at luncheon four carters came in⁠—long-limbed, muscular Ayrshire Scots, with lean, intelligent faces. Four quarts of stout were ordered; they kept filling the tumbler with the other hand as they drank; and in less time than it takes me to write these words the four quarts were finished⁠—another round was proposed, discussed, and negatived⁠—and they were creaking out of the village with their carts.

The ruins drew you towards them. You never saw any place more desolate from a distance, nor one that less belied its promise near at hand. Some crows and gulls flew away croaking as I scrambled in. The snow had drifted into the vaults. The clachan dabbled with snow, the white hills, the black sky, the sea marked in the coves with faint circular wrinkles, the whole world, as it looked from a loophole in Dunure, was cold, wretched, and out-at-elbows. If you had been a wicked baron and compelled to stay there all the afternoon, you would have had a rare fit of remorse. How you would have heaped up the fire and gnawed your fingers! I think it would have come to homicide before the evening⁠—if it were only for the pleasure of seeing something red! And the masters of Dunure, it is to be noticed, were remarkable of old for inhumanity. One of these vaults where the snow had drifted was that “black voute” where “Mr. Alane Stewart, Commendatour of Crossraguel,” endured his fiery trials. On the 1st and 7th of September 1570 (ill dates for Mr. Alan!), Gilbert, Earl of Cassilis, his chaplain, his baker, his cook, his pantryman, and another servant, bound the Poor Commendator “betwix an iron chimlay and a fire,” and there cruelly roasted him until he signed away his abbacy. It is one of the ugliest stories of an ugly period, but not, somehow, without such a flavour of the ridiculous as makes it hard to sympathise quite seriously with the victim. And it is consoling to remember that he got away at last, and kept his abbacy, and, over and above, had a pension from the Earl until he died.

Some way beyond Dunure a wide bay, of somewhat less unkindly aspect, opened out. Colzean plantations lay all along the steep shore, and there was a wooded hill towards the centre, where the trees made a sort of shadowy etching over the snow. The road went down and up, and past a blacksmith’s cottage that made fine music in the valley. Three compatriots of Burns drove up to me in a cart. They were all drunk, and asked me jeeringly if this was the way to Dunure. I told them it was; and my answer was received with unfeigned merriment. One gentleman was so much tickled he nearly fell out of the cart; indeed, he was only saved by a companion, who either had not so fine a sense of humour or had drunken less.

“The toune of Mayboll,” says the inimitable Abercrummie, “stands upon an ascending ground from east to west, and lyes open to the south. It hath one principall street, with houses upon both sides, built of freestone; and it is beautifyed with the situation of two castles, one at each end of this street. That on the east belongs to the Erle of Cassilis. On the west end is a castle, which belonged sometime to the laird of Blairquan, which is now the tolbuith, and is adorned with a pyremide [conical roof], and a row of ballesters round it raised from the top of the staircase, into which they have mounted a fyne clock. There be four lanes which pass from the principall street; one is called the Black Vennel, which is steep, declining to the southwest, and leads to a lower street, which is far larger than the high chiefe street, and it runs from the Kirkland to the Well Trees, in which there have been many pretty buildings, belonging to the severall gentry of the countrey, who were wont to resort thither in winter, and divert themselves in converse together at their owne houses. It was once the principall street of the town; but many of these houses of the gentry having been decayed and ruined, it has lost much of its ancient beautie. Just opposite to this vennel, there is another that leads northwest, from the chiefe street to the green, which is a pleasant plott of ground, enclosed round with an earthen wall, wherein they were wont to play football, but now at the Gowff and byasse-bowls. The houses of this towne, on both sides of the street, have their several gardens belonging to them; and in the lower street there be some pretty orchards, that yield store of good fruit.” As Patterson says, this description is near enough even today, and is mighty nicely written to boot. I am bound to add, of my own experience, that Maybole is tumbledown and dreary. Prosperous enough in reality, it has an air of decay; and though the population has increased, a roofless house every here and there seems to protest the contrary. The women are more than well-favoured, and the men fine tall fellows; but they look slipshod and dissipated. As they slouched at street corners, or stood about gossiping in the snow, it seemed they would have been more at home in the slums of a large city than here in a country place betwixt a village and a town. I heard a great deal about drinking, and a great deal about religious revivals: two things in which the Scottish character is emphatic and most unlovely. In particular, I heard of clergymen who were employing their time in explaining to a delighted audience the physics of the Second Coming. It is not very likely any of us will be asked to help. If we were, it is likely we should receive instructions for the occasion, and that on more reliable authority. And so I can only figure to myself a congregation truly curious in such flights of theological fancy, as one of veteran and accomplished saints, who have fought the good fight to an end and outlived all worldly passion, and are to be regarded rather as a part of the Church Triumphant than the poor, imperfect company on earth. And yet I saw some young fellows about the smoking-room who seemed, in the eyes of one who cannot count himself straitlaced, in need of some more practical sort of teaching. They seemed only eager to get drunk, and to do so speedily. It was not much more than a week after the New Year; and to hear them return on their past bouts with a gusto unspeakable was not altogether pleasing. Here is one snatch of talk, for the accuracy of which I can vouch⁠—

“Ye had a spree here last Tuesday?”

“We had that!”

“I wasna able to be oot o’ my bed. Man, I was awful bad on Wednesday.”

“Ay, ye were gey bad.”

And you should have seen the bright eyes, and heard the sensual accents! They recalled their doings with devout gusto and a sort of rational pride. Schoolboys, after their first drunkenness, are not more boastful; a cock does not plume himself with a more unmingled satisfaction as he paces forth among his harem; and yet these were grown men, and by no means short of wit. It was hard to suppose they were very eager about the Second Coming: it seemed as if some elementary notions of temperance for the men and seemliness for the women would have gone nearer the mark. And yet, as it seemed to me typical of much that is evil in Scotland, Maybole is also typical of much that is best. Some of the factories, which have taken the place of weaving in the town’s economy, were originally founded and are still possessed by self-made men of the sterling, stout old breed⁠—fellows who made some little bit of an invention, borrowed some little pocketful of capital, and then, step by step, in courage, thrift and industry, fought their way upwards to an assured position.

Abercrummie has told you enough of the Tolbooth; but, as a bit of spelling, this inscription on the Tolbooth bell seems too delicious to withhold: “This bell is founded at Maiboll Bi Danel Geli, a Frenchman, the 6th November, 1696, Bi appointment of the heritors of the parish of Maiyboll.” The Castle deserves more notice. It is a large and shapely tower, plain from the ground upwards, but with a zone of ornamentation running about the top. In a general way this adornment is perched on the very summit of the chimney-stacks; but there is one corner more elaborate than the rest. A very heavy string-course runs round the upper story, and just above this, facing up the street, the tower carries a small oriel window, fluted and corbelled and carved about with stone heads. It is so ornate it has somewhat the air of a shrine. And it was, indeed, the casket of a very precious jewel, for in the room to which it gives light lay, for long years, the heroine of the sweet old ballad of “Johnnie Faa”⁠—she who, at the call of the gipsies’ songs, “came tripping down the stair, and all her maids before her.” Some people say the ballad has no basis in fact, and have written, I believe, unanswerable papers to the proof. But in the face of all that, the very look of that high oriel window convinces the imagination, and we enter into all the sorrows of the imprisoned dame. We conceive the burden of the long, lacklustre days, when she leaned her sick head against the mullions, and saw the burghers loafing in Maybole High Street, and the children at play, and ruffling gallants riding by from hunt or foray. We conceive the passion of odd moments, when the wind threw up to her some snatch of song, and her heart grew hot within her, and her eyes overflowed at the memory of the past. And even if the tale be not true of this or that lady, or this or that old tower, it is true in the essence of all men and women: for all of us, some time or other, hear the gipsies singing; over all of us is the glamour cast. Some resist and sit resolutely by the fire. Most go and are brought back again, like Lady Cassilis. A few, of the tribe of Waring, go and are seen no more; only now and again, at springtime, when the gipsies’ song is afloat in the amethyst evening, we can catch their voices in the glee.

By night it was clearer, and Maybole more visible than during the day. Clouds coursed over the sky in great masses; the full moon battled the other way, and lit up the snow with gleams of flying silver; the town came down the hill in a cascade of brown gables, bestridden by smooth white roofs, and sprangled here and there with lighted windows. At either end the snow stood high up in the darkness, on the peak of the Tolbooth and among the chimneys of the Castle. As the moon flashed a bull’s-eye glitter across the town between the racing clouds, the white roofs leaped into relief over the gables and the chimney-stacks, and their shadows over the white roofs. In the town itself the lit face of the clock peered down the street; an hour was hammered out on Mr. Geli’s bell, and from behind the red curtains of a public-house someone trolled out⁠—a compatriot of Burns, again!⁠—“The saut tear blin’s my e’e.”

Next morning there was sun and a flapping wind. From the street corners of Maybole I could catch breezy glimpses of green fields. The road underfoot was wet and heavy⁠—part ice, part snow, part water, and anyone I met greeted me, by way of salutation, with “A fine thowe” (thaw). My way lay among rather bleak bills, and past bleak ponds and dilapidated castles and monasteries, to the Highland-looking village of Kirkoswald. It has little claim to notice, save that Burns came there to study surveying in the summer of 1777, and there also, in the kirkyard, the original of Tam o’ Shanter sleeps his last sleep. It is worth noticing, however, that this was the first place I thought “Highland-looking.” Over the bill from Kirkoswald a farm-road leads to the coast. As I came down above Turnberry, the sea view was indeed strangely different from the day before. The cold fogs were all blown away; and there was Ailsa Craig, like a refraction, magnified and deformed, of the Bass Rock; and there were the chiselled mountaintops of Arran, veined and tipped with snow; and behind, and fainter, the low, blue land of Cantyre. Cottony clouds stood in a great castle over the top of Arran, and blew out in long streamers to the south. The sea was bitten all over with white; little ships, tacking up and down the Firth, lay over at different angles in the wind. On Shanter they were ploughing lea; a cart foal, all in a field by himself, capered and whinnied as if the spring were in him.

The road from Turnberry to Girvan lies along the shore, among sandhills and by wildernesses of tumbled bent. Every here and there a few cottages stood together beside a bridge. They had one odd feature, not easy to describe in words: a triangular porch projected from above the door, supported at the apex by a single upright post; a secondary door was hinged to the post, and could be hasped on either cheek of the real entrance; so, whether the wind was north or south, the cotter could make himself a triangular bight of shelter where to set his chair and finish a pipe with comfort. There is one objection to this device; for, as the post stands in the middle of the fairway, anyone precipitately issuing from the cottage must run his chance of a broken head. So far as I am aware, it is peculiar to the little corner of country about Girvan. And that corner is noticeable for more reasons: it is certainly one of the most characteristic districts in Scotland. It has this movable porch by way of architecture; it has, as we shall see, a sort of remnant of provincial costume, and it has the handsomest population in the Lowlands.⁠ ⁠…

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface this is a travel sketch: a man walks south along the coast of Ayrshire in the snow, stays a night, and walks on. But Stevenson is doing several things at once. The first is painting a landscape, and the opening is full of it. The snow has tucked the fields in like children under a counterpane, the wind has made ripples in it like sand left by the sea, and the road near the cliff edge seems to skirt the shores of creation and void space. That last phrase tells you that this is not a postcard. The world is empty, grey and white, a cold and dolorous sort of shepherd's plaid.

The second thing is human. Stevenson meets an old man gathering cockles, with a face broken into flakes and channels like drying mud, and a faint air of being surprised that life had gone so ill with him. He is seventy-six and cannot find work, because nobody believes a man that age can do it. His reply, that he doubts whether he could, with a sad little chuckle, is the emotional heart of the first half. The narrator imagines the wife or daughter who might have brushed the clay from his coat, and recognises that there is none. Look closely and you see that Stevenson is moved, and that he refuses to hide it, while still letting the old man keep his dignity.

The third layer is history and the uncomfortable truth about the picturesque. The ruined castle at Dunure looks romantic, but it is the place where, in 1570, a powerful earl roasted a church official between an iron chimney and a fire to make him sign away his lands. Stevenson tells the story with a tone that is half horror and half black comedy, noting that the victim survived and kept his post and a pension. The point is that the beauty of ruins sits on top of cruelty.

Then the walk reaches the town of Maybole, where Stevenson sets the quotation of an old topographer's loving description beside his own verdict that the town is tumbledown and dreary. The men boast of their drinking spree like schoolboys, and the talk of religious revivals seems beside the point. Yet he balances this by praising the self-made manufacturers. The Johnnie Faa ballad, about a lady who left her lord to follow gypsies, gives him a moment of real insight: all of us, sooner or later, hear the gipsies singing.

Finally the weather turns, with a thaw and a view of Ailsa Craig and Arran, and the mood lifts. The essay's meaning lies in this range: a winter walk as an education in seeing both the beauty and the hardness of a place.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The strongest quality here is the eye. Stevenson looks at a scene and finds the exact, surprising comparison. The snow roosting in the crannies of the rock like white seabirds, the little cock of snow on an outlying reef like a toy lighthouse, and the lit clock face peering down the street of Maybole under racing clouds are all images that make the scene visible. The moonlit paragraph, with the town coming down the hill in a cascade of brown gables bestridden by smooth white roofs, is a small tour de force of light and shadow. His sentences move with the pace of walking, and the sequence of impressions has the unforced rhythm of someone who is simply noticing.

The humour is also well judged. The description of the inn's guestroom, painted in what he calls aesthetic fashion, with twelve cockleshells and a china figure on the mantelpiece, a spittoon filled with seashells and a patchwork hearthrug made of people's old clothes, is a clever way of making fun of fashionable taste in London while showing real respect for the poor housewife's labour of love. The hearthrug passage is the best joke in the piece because it turns into a tenderness. The four carters finishing four quarts of stout in less time than it takes to write the sentence is pure comic timing.

The structure, however, is loose, and that is partly the nature of the form. The essay is a series of episodes strung along a road, and some are much stronger than others. The long quotation from the old description of Maybole, in antique spelling, is a gift to a topographer but a pause for a general reader. The ballad and castle sections drift into antiquarian detail that depends on the reader's interest in local history. The passage about Scottish drinking and revivals, while lively, borders on a sermon, and the tone shifts uneasily between mockery and moral disapproval.

There is also a question of how well Stevenson handles the poor. The old cockle-gatherer is rendered with real feeling, but the portraits of the drunk carters, the men on the street corners and the people at the inn who do not seem well pleased to see a stranger are seen from outside, with the amused detachment of a young gentleman. The wit sometimes keeps people at arm's length.

Even so, the best passages are first rate. The wicked baron stuck in the ruin all afternoon, who would have heaped up the fire and gnawed his fingers, is a witty way of making a cold room feel cold. Overall it is an uneven but deeply attractive piece, with moments of real power surrounded by good, lively filler.

III. The Impact It Had

This is not one of Stevenson's famous works, and its influence is modest and indirect. It was written when he was beginning to publish regularly in magazines, and it belongs to the group of early travel and landscape essays that trained him as a writer. After his death it was gathered with other pieces of this kind in a posthumous collection of travel essays, which is where most readers meet it today.

Its significance in his career is as a stage in his development. In these years he was learning to combine description, anecdote and reflection in a way that would carry over into his first books of travel. An Inland Voyage appeared in 1878 and Travels with a Donkey in the Cevennes in 1879, and the qualities that make them work, the quick portrait of a stranger, the mocking self-awareness, the sense of landscape as mood, are all practised here. You can see the same eye at work in the passages of his novels set in Scotland, where weather and coastline are almost characters. Kidnapped and The Master of Ballantrae in particular carry the Scottish landscape and the Scottish past, with their mixture of beauty and violence, that this essay already weighs in the balance.

The essay also adds to the picture of Scotland as a place of layered history. Stevenson's interest in the dark stories lying under picturesque ruins, the cruelty of old lords, the zeal of religious factions, the feuds of families, runs through his fiction and his essays. The Dunure episode is a miniature of that habit of mind.

The record of its reception is thin. There is no recorded famous reaction to this particular essay, and it has not been the subject of major critical controversy. It has survived chiefly among Stevenson enthusiasts, Scottish literature scholars and readers interested in Burns country, as a vivid snapshot of the Ayrshire coast in the 1870s. As a record of what Scottish rural life looked like, with its drinking, its revivalist religion, its poverty and its manufacturing beginnings, it has some value for social historians.

In teaching, it appears mainly as an example of Victorian travel writing and of the Scottish essay tradition, and is less often assigned than his more famous works. It remains a pleasant discovery, and a good introduction to his gift for noticing.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing that readers enjoy is the sheer sensory quality of the winter scene. The crunch of snow underfoot, dogs barking at every farm, the profound silence broken only by the sound of oars at sea, and the postman who blows his horn and finds no letters waiting, all give a convincing impression of a cold day on a quiet coast. Stevenson's trick of noticing the small thing, such as the tiny cock of snow on a reef, makes the scene believable and charming. For readers who love the Scottish coast, it is a vivid portrait of a place at a particular moment.

They also love the people. The old man gathering cockles is a small, unforgettable portrait, handled with a delicate mix of humour and pity. The passage ends with the line that it will make your heart ache if you think of his old fingers groping in the snow, and it is the sort of sentence that readers carry away. The contrasting comic scenes, the carters and their stout, the three drunken men in a cart who think it hilarious that a traveller is asking the way to Dunure, give a sense of lively, unvarnished Scottish life.

Readers also admire the way Stevenson plays with the reader. The mock-solemn description of the guestroom, with its cockleshells and its seashell spittoon, is comedy of a high order, and the sly aside that the hearthrug would merit an article to itself, and a coloured diagram to help the text, is a joke aimed at the fashion for aesthetic home decoration.

Another source of affection is the way the piece moves between darkness and light. The gloomy Dunure episode, with its grim history and its savage joke about the wicked baron, gives way to the moonlit night in Maybole and the sunny morning of the thaw, with Ailsa Craig and the peaks of Arran, a colt capering in a field as though spring were in him. The change of weather lifts the essay, and readers feel it. It is a satisfying shape, a journey from stifling cold to open sea air.

The ballad passage about the lady who left her home for the gypsies is also a favourite for many. Stevenson's meditation that all of us hear the gipsies singing, that some resist, most go and are brought back, and a few are never seen again, is a lovely piece of reflection, and one that readers often quote. Admirers of his work say that you can feel the future novelist in this humane, imaginative aside.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Since this essay has not been a prominent subject of criticism, there are no particular reviewers or famous arguments to cite. What can be said honestly is what readers have found less successful, drawn from the text and from general assessments of Stevenson's early work.

The first complaint is about structure. The essay is episodic and, in the version we have, unfinished, breaking off in mid-journey with an ellipsis. Some readers find the stretches of antiquarian description, especially the long quotation from an old description of Maybole in seventeenth-century spelling and the tangents about the bell and the castle, a drag on the pace. A traveller's notebook is not always a shapely essay.

The second concerns tone. Stevenson's comments on Scottish drinking and religious revivals are brisk and judgmental. He calls the Scottish character in these matters emphatic and most unlovely, and he makes fun of clergymen explaining the physics of the Second Coming. For some readers this sounds like the superiority of a young middle-class man from Edinburgh looking down on country people, and a few have seen a streak of snobbery in the way he describes the Maybole men as slipshod and dissipated. Others, of course, find his candour refreshing, and defenders point out that he is also a Scot criticizing his own people, and that he balances the picture with praise of the self-made manufacturers.

A third line of criticism, more common today, concerns the treatment of poverty. Stevenson shows real compassion for the old cockle-gatherer, but he also finds the roasting of the commendator ridiculous and keeps up a comic mood throughout. A modern reader may feel that the essay is too ready to turn hardship into material, or too quick to aestheticise a cold, bare room.

Finally, the essay's reliance on dense local reference, to Burns, to ballads, to particular families and places, makes it harder for readers outside Scotland. Without that knowledge some of the allusions fall flat.

None of this has made the essay a battleground. It is regarded as a minor but charming work, which is exactly how it should be read. The faults are those of a young writer's notebook, and the virtues are those of an observer with a remarkable eye.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This essay belongs to the great nineteenth-century tradition of the walking tour and the travel sketch, a tradition that runs from the Romantic poets, who tramped the Lake District and the Alps, through Hazlitt and Thoreau to Stevenson's own generation. Stevenson was one of the form's finest practitioners, and his best-known travel books helped define it. This early essay is a smaller version of the same enterprise: a solitary walker, a few days on the road, the pleasure of casual meetings and the shift of mood with the weather.

It also sits within Scottish literature, in the long conversation with Robert Burns that the essay makes explicit by repeatedly invoking the poet. The old man who might have sat as the father in The Cottar's Saturday Night, the drunken men who are compatriots of Burns, and the walk through Kirkoswald where Burns studied surveying and where the original of Tam o' Shanter lies buried, all place Stevenson in conscious relation to that heritage. The Scots tradition of mixing humour, sentiment and dark history, the same that you find in Burns, in Walter Scott and later in Stevenson's own fiction, is unmistakable.

Among the works it relates to, perhaps the nearest are Stevenson's other essays of place and his account of walking in the Cevennes. Beyond him, writers of the English and Scottish landscape and of the winter walk, from Dorothy Wordsworth's journals to later nature writers, share this combination of exact description and personal feeling. Writers on rural poverty and social observation, from Dickens in his travel sketches to the later documentary writers, might also be placed alongside the old man and the cockles.

The reader may also notice an early example of what would be called psychogeography, the interest in how a place's history and atmosphere shape the mood of one who passes through it, though Stevenson would not have used the word. The idea that ruins carry buried cruelty beneath their picturesque outlines has a long afterlife.

Why read it now? For the moonlit paragraph, for the cockle-gatherer, for the hearthrug, and for the pleasure of a writer discovering that a walk is a story. It also stands as a reminder of how to travel, with all senses open, and with a willingness to be moved, amused and disturbed by what lies on the road. It is short on polish but long on life, and that is a fine reason to read.
