Look Tin Eli and the Architectural Reinvention of Chinatown After the 1906 Fire

San Francisco — How an American-born civil rights pioneer foiled a city hall plot to evict Chinatown and hired white architects to build a 'stage-set Oriental' fortress of tourism (Post-1906 Reconstruction (1906–1910)) • 10 min read

“This is our ancestral home in America; we will rebuild it so grandly that the whole world will come to walk down our street.” — Look Tin Eli (1906)

Look up at the corner of Grant Avenue and California Street where the cable car crests the hill at the entrance to San Francisco’s Chinatown, and you see the iconic silhouette that defines the oldest Chinatown in North America: the *Sing Chong* and *Sing Fat* buildings, crowned with multi-tiered green-and-gold pagoda towers, upturned dragon-tail eaves (*feiyan*), yellow glazed terracotta brick, and curved wrought-iron balconies. Generations of visitors have assumed that nineteenth-century Chinese immigrants naturally brought this traditional temple architecture with them from Guangdong Province. The truth is far more astonishing: before April 18, 1906, not a single pagoda roof existed in Chinatown, and every one of these buildings was designed by two white American architects hired by a brilliant Chinese-American businessman named Look Tin Eli to save his community from erasure.

Before the 1906 earthquake and fire, nineteenth-century Chinatown—known to its residents as *Tangrenbu* ('Port of the Men of Tang') or *Dai Fow* ('Big City')—occupied twelve blocks along Dupont Street (now Grant Avenue) inside ordinary, unadorned three-story American brick and wood tenements built in the 1850s. For decades under the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, white politicians and real-estate speculators had coveted Chinatown’s prime hillside location just two blocks above the Financial District and Nob Hill, slandering the neighborhood as a sanitary hazard to justify condemnation.

When the April 1906 firestorm leveled Chinatown to ashes and drove its 15,000 residents across the bay to segregated refugee camps in Oakland, San Francisco’s powerful 'Committee of Fifty' and business elite saw their golden opportunity. Led by billionaire sugar-and-railroad heir James D. Phelan and *Chronicle* publisher M. H. de Young, the city’s 'Subcommittee on Permanent Location for Chinatown' voted on April 24, 1906, to permanently ban Chinese residents from returning to Dupont Street and relocate them either to the muddy slaughterhouse marshes of Hunters Point at the southern edge of the city or across the county line to Daly City.

They did not reckon with thirty-six-year-old Look Tin Eli (陸天樂, 1870–1919). Born in Mendocino, California, in 1870 and educated for ten years in China before returning home at age fourteen in 1884, Look Tin Eli was already a hero of American constitutional law. When San Francisco customs officials refused to let the fourteen-year-old boy off the ship *SS Arabic* under the newly passed Chinese Exclusion Act, his father hired lawyers to bring a habeas corpus case before the U.S. Circuit Court (*In re Look Tin Sing*, 1884). Supreme Court Justice Stephen J. Field ruled from the circuit bench that under the Fourteenth Amendment, any child born on United States soil to Chinese parents is a natural-born U.S. citizen who can never be excluded from their own country—setting the foundational precedent for the Supreme Court’s famous 1898 *Wong Kim Ark* birthright-citizenship ruling.

In the smoking ruins of May 1906, Look Tin Eli joined forces with diplomats from the Qing Imperial Consulate (whose Empress Dowager Cixi pointedly warned that if Chinatown moved to Oakland, all lucrative trans-Pacific steamship trade and customs revenue would move to the Port of Oakland too) and with white landlords who panicked at the thought of losing their highest-paying, most reliable commercial tenants. Within weeks, City Hall capitulated and allowed Chinese merchants to return to their burned lots on Dupont Street.

Look Tin Eli knew, however, that legal permission was only a temporary truce unless he permanently changed how white America perceived Chinatown. Instead of rebuilding the dingy, anonymous nineteenth-century brick tenements that politicians had called a 'blight,' he proposed transforming the renamed *Grant Avenue* into an exotic, clean, fireproof 'Oriental' tourist attraction—an architectural spectacle so visually irresistible and economically vital to San Francisco’s tourism industry that the city would never dare bulldoze it again.

Managing the reconstruction of the *Sing Chong Bazaar* at the gateway corner of Grant and California—and simultaneously founding the *Canton Bank of San Francisco* in October 1907 as the first Chinese-owned bank in the United States to finance the neighborhood’s rebuilding—Look Tin Eli hired Oakland-born architect *T. Paterson Ross* and structural engineer *A. W. Burgren*. Neither Ross nor Burgren had ever set foot in China or studied authentic Cantonese vernacular architecture.

Working from Look Tin Eli’s vision and nineteenth-century stereopticon postcards of imperial Beijing palaces and southern Chinese garden pavilions, Ross and Burgren invented a hybrid 'Sino-Western' architectural style from scratch: reinforced steel-and-concrete frames clad in buff firebrick to satisfy the post-1906 building code, topped with purely decorative multi-tiered sheet-metal pagoda towers, imperial yellow and green ceramic tiles, curved eaves, and dragon-motif ironwork.

When the Sing Chong ('Prosperous') Building opened in late 1907 and its twin Sing Fat ('Prosperous Wealth') Building rose directly across California Street in 1908, Look Tin Eli’s strategy succeeded beyond his wildest dreams. Even the formerly hostile *San Francisco Chronicle* praised the 'artistic Oriental splendor' of the new Grant Avenue; sightseeing tour buses ('Rubberneck Wagons') made Chinatown their number-one stop; and behind those theatrical pagoda facades—in buildings like the Chinese Six Companies headquarters, the Oriental Telephone Exchange at 743 Washington Street (where bilingual operators memorized the name, address, and dialect of all 2,400 Chinatown subscribers without numbers), and the Tin How Temple—generations of Chinese Americans preserved a resilient, self-governing community in the heart of San Francisco.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Constitutional Law: 'In re Look Tin Sing' (1884) and the 14th Amendment

Few people walking past the Sing Chong Building today realize that its developer, Look Tin Eli, was the fourteen-year-old plaintiff in In re Look Tin Sing (21 F. 905, C.C.D. Cal. 1884)—the very first federal court case to establish that the Citizenship Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment ('All persons born or naturalized in the United States... are citizens of the United States') applies regardless of parents' race or national origin. Fourteen years later, when San Francisco native Wong Kim Ark took his own exclusion case to the U.S. Supreme Court in 1898, the justices cited young Look Tin Eli's case as the cornerstone precedent for American birthright citizenship.

2. 'Subversive Orientalism': Using Architecture as a Political Shield

Architectural historians such as Philip Choy and Judy Yung describe post-1906 Chinatown as a masterclass in strategic self-fashioning. By commissioning white architects T. Paterson Ross and A. W. Burgren to top American Beaux-Arts commercial blocks with exaggerated imperial pagodas and glazed dragon eaves—features that actual Cantonese village merchants would never have been permitted to build under Qing sumptuary laws—Look Tin Eli and his fellow merchants turned Western Orientalist fantasies into a permanent economic and political shield around their neighborhood. 3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Notice three architectural icons as you enter Chinatown: first, the Sing Chong (601 Grant Ave) and Sing Fat (573 California St) twin gateway buildings designed by Ross & Burgren in 1907–1908; second, the Bank of Canton Building at 743 Washington Street (designed by Ross & Burgren in 1909), which housed the famous Chinatown Telephone Exchange where operators switched calls in five Chinese dialects by name; and third, Waverly Place ('The Street of Painted Balconies'), home to the 1852 Tin How Temple on the top floor.
