Euljiro's Metalwork Labyrinth: Where 1960s Print Shops Meet Neon Speakeasies

Korea — Inside downtown Seoul's gritty 'Hipjiro' alleys, where seventy-year-old lathe masters claim they can build a tank by afternoon and young artists hide bars behind vending machines () • 20 min read

“Bring us a sketch on a cigarette pack at nine in the morning, and by five o’clock you can walk out of Euljiro with a working satellite—or a submarine.” — Old Euljiro machinists' saying

Key Context & Why It Matters

Step off the gleaming glass boulevards of central Seoul into the labyrinthine grid of Euljiro 3-ga and 4-ga, and you enter a living industrial ecosystem where 1960s lathe masters, metal casters, acrylic cutters, and letterpress printers work door-to-door with clandestine third-wave coffee roasters and neon-lit vinyl speakeasies. Anchored by architect Kim Swoo-geun's 1967 brutalist megastructure Sewoon Sangga, Euljiro powered South Korea's post-war manufacturing boom with an interconnected micro-factory network capable of prototyping any machine part, chandelier, or circuit enclosure in a single afternoon. In recent years, young designers and musicians seeking affordable upper-floor lofts gave birth to 'Hipjiro,' turning unmarked steel doors and retro vending machines into portals where industrial grit and contemporary art collide.

Turn off the polished granite boulevards of central Seoul at Euljiro 3-ga Station, duck beneath a tangled canopy of black electrical cables into a brick alleyway barely wide enough for a three-wheeled cargo motorcycle (*samryuncha*), and your senses are ambushed at once. Blue tungsten-inert-gas welding arcs flash behind open steel shutters; the humid air tastes of sulfurized cutting oil, ozone, offset printer’s ink, and sweet *Maxim* instant coffee carried on brass trays by veteran *dabang* waitresses; and from a thousand ground-floor workshops rises the polyrhythmic percussion of CNC lathes, hydraulic sheet-metal brakes, and Heidelberg four-color presses. As the old machinists of the district like to boast to visiting engineers: bring us a sketch on the back of a cigarette pack at nine in the morning, and by five o’clock you can walk out of Euljiro with a working satellite—or a submarine.

The avenue takes its modern name from General Eulji Mundeok, the seventh-century Goguryeo commander who annihilated a million-man Sui Dynasty invasion force at the Battle of Salsu in 612 CE. During the Joseon Dynasty, however, locals knew this low-lying district as *Gurigae* (구리개, 'Copper Hill'), where royal medical dispensaries (*Hyeminseo*) stood beside brassware foundries and metalworkers’ stalls. Renamed *Koganemachi* ('Gold Town') during the Japanese colonial occupation, the neighborhood was bombed into charred rubble during the Korean War (1950–1953). When the armistice was signed in July 1953, displaced mechanics, blacksmiths, and lathe operators from across the peninsula squatted in the ruins between Chungmuro and the Cheonggyecheon stream, stripping abandoned U.S. Army Willys Jeeps, spent brass artillery shells, and steel oil drums to forge South Korea’s first post-war water pumps, farm tools, bicycles, and printing presses.

Over the next seven decades, while giant *chaebol* conglomerates constructed supertanker shipyards in Ulsan and semiconductor fabs in Giheung, Euljiro evolved into an urban manufacturing rainforest of more than ten thousand micro-enterprises arranged like a medieval guild city. Walk west toward Euljiro 2-ga and Inhyeon-dong, and you enter a valley of five thousand printing, color-separation, hot-foil stamping (*bak-insoe*), die-cutting (*domusong*), and bookbinding (*jebeon*) shops where delivery riders balance two-meter towers of freshly guillotined paper on the backs of motorcycles. Cross east into Euljiro 3-ga and Sanlim-dong—the storied 'Iron Alley' (*Cheolgongso Golmok*)—and every doorway houses precision tool-and-die makers, gear cutters, brass spinners, and electroplaters. Continue to Euljiro 4-ga and Bangsan Market, and every chandelier, neon transformer, architectural ceramic tile, and wallpaper sample in Seoul glitters from floor to ceiling.

Slicing north-south through the very heart of this metalworking labyrinth stands *Sewoon Sangga* (세운상가, literally 'The Arcade Where the Energy of the World Gathers')—a one-kilometer-long brutalist megastructure stretching from Jongmyo Royal Shrine toward Namsan Mountain. The strip of land beneath it carries a dramatic wartime origin: in March 1945, terrified that American B-29 bombers would drop incendiary payloads on dense wooden neighborhoods, Japanese colonial authorities demolished a fifty-meter-wide swath of houses to create an air-raid firebreak (*sogaegongji*). After 1953, tens of thousands of refugees packed the vacant scar with timber shanties and open-air electronics stalls until 1966, when Seoul’s 'Bulldozer Mayor' Kim Hyun-ok commissioned thirty-five-year-old architect Kim Swoo-geun (김수근, 1931–1986) to transform the corridor into modern Korea’s first integrated mixed-use megastructure.

Completed between 1967 and 1968 across eight linked concrete blocks—Sewoon, Hyeondae, Cheonggye, Daerim, Sampung, Poongjeon, Sinseong, and Jinyang—Kim Swoo-geun’s design embodied the utopian spirit of Team 10 and Japanese Metabolism. By lifting pedestrian life onto a broad third-floor concrete deck above the noisy street intersections, Sewoon Sangga housed four lower levels of bustling electronics, audio, and machine-part arcades crowned by four upper stories of luxury modern apartments featuring indoor skylit courtyards, central steam heating, a rooftop golf driving range, and Korea’s first glass-walled elevator. For a decade, movie stars, politicians, and industrialists lived directly above stalls smelling of rosin-core solder and machine grease.

During the 1970s and 1980s, those narrow corridors inside Sewoon and Cheonggye Sangga became South Korea’s equivalent of a Silicon Valley garage. Teenagers, university engineering students, and self-taught radio repairmen wandered from stall to stall buying vacuum tubes, hand-wound output transformers, nixie tubes, and photocopied Japanese circuit schematics. In 1980, in a cramped workshop inside Sewoon Sangga, engineer Lee Yong-teh and a small team wired together Korea’s first domestic commercial microcomputer—the 8-bit Sambo SE-8001, launching TriGem Computer and the nation’s personal computing era. At the very same counters, students whispered passwords to dealers who reached beneath floorboards for smuggled Western vinyl LPs, shortwave radios, and pirated technical journals that fueled an underground hunger for the outside world.

Step inside a four-*pyeong* (thirteen-square-meter) machine shop in Sanlim-dong today, and you will meet the human backbone of this ecosystem: the *Jangin* (장인, master craftsman). Many of Euljiro’s lathe and milling masters arrived from the southern provinces as fourteen-year-old apprentices (*sida*) in the late 1960s and have stood before the same cast-iron Hwacheon manual lathe or Bridgeport mill for fifty years. Without consulting a digital readout, a seventy-year-old Sanlim-dong machinist can shave a 304 stainless-steel shaft or a phosphor-bronze bushing to a tolerance of 0.01 millimeters simply by listening to the pitch of the carbide cutter and watching the blue-gold temper color of the curling metal swarf (*chip*). On the grease-darkened wall behind him hang hundreds of hand-ground cutting tools, vernier calipers, and paper calendars frozen in 1988. Young apprentices and visiting engineers watch in hushed respect as these veteran machinists true up a wobbling chuck with a brass hammer, grind custom high-speed-steel form tools on a bench wheel by eye, and solve mechanical tolerances that baffle automated software.

Even the logistics of how raw metal and finished components move through Euljiro’s ninety-centimeter-wide passageways is a study in kinetic choreography. Because standard delivery vans cannot enter the inner veins of Sanlim-dong or Ipjeong-dong, the district relies on a fleet of custom-welded *samryuncha* (three-wheeled motorized flatbed trikes), hand-pulled steel carts (*리어카, rieoka*), and modified Daelim Citi 100 motorcycles fitted with extended rear steel racks. Veteran couriers thread through crowds of sparks with half a ton of brass rods or freshly stamped circuit housings strapped behind their backs, honking a two-note horn that every machinist recognizes by ear. Nobody signs a digital bill of lading; transactions between neighboring shops are recorded in pencil inside dog-eared paper ledgers (*jangbu*) settled at the end of every month on a handshake.

Urban economists point to Euljiro as a living textbook of Alfred Marshall’s 'industrial district agglomeration,' where thousands of tiny workshops achieve astonishing speed not by merging into a single giant factory, but by clustering within a three-hundred-meter radius. Consider how a young hardware startup, a robotics researcher, or a Chungmuro film prop master builds a complex prototype here in a single afternoon: at 9:30 AM, raw aircraft-grade 6061 aluminum bar stock is purchased and sawed in Jugyo-dong; at 10:15 AM, it is CNC-milled two alleys west; by noon, it is hand-polished (*ppari*) and sandblasted next door; at 1:30 PM, it is gold-anodized across the street; at 3:00 PM, custom LED drivers are soldered inside Daelim Sangga; and by 5:00 PM, the finished instrument rests inside a foil-stamped box printed in Inhyeon-dong.

Equally vital to this micro-supply chain is the paper and ink labyrinth of Inhyeon-dong and Euljiro 2-ga. Here, the ground vibrates beneath the four-ton rollers of German and Japanese offset presses running twenty-four hours a day. Master pressmen lean over illuminated inspection desks with 10x magnifiers (*ruppe*), adjusting cyan, magenta, yellow, and black ink keys by fractions of a millimeter while automated guillotines slice reams of coated art paper with a pneumatic *whoosh-thud*. Tucked in the back courts beside digital plotters survive third-generation cast-lead letterpress (*hwalja insoe*) studios, where compositors still pluck tiny lead Hangul and Hanja characters from wooden cases holding ten thousand sorts to press crisp, deeply indented poetry chapbooks, wedding invitations, and theater posters.

A few blocks east in Euljiro 4-ga and Bangsan Market, the sensory register shifts from heavy iron and offset ink to light, glass, and fragrance. Since the 1970s, the ground-floor arcades of Euljiro 4-ga have served as the lighting capital of South Korea: a glittering canopy where crystal hotel chandeliers, hand-blown glass globes, brass mid-century sconces, and custom architectural LED strips hang in dense stalactite forests above the sidewalk. Just across the stream bed in Bangsan Comprehensive Market, wholesalers supply every independent baker and candle-maker in Seoul with copper canelé molds, essential oils, wax blocks, and custom-embossed kraft packaging—completing a circle of material fabrication that covers virtually every physical object needed to furnish a room or open a business.

Around 2014 and 2015, as commercial rents skyrocketed in gentrified neighborhoods like Hongdae, Itaewon, and Garosu-gil, a quiet demographic shift began on the upper floors of Euljiro. Above the roaring ground-floor tool shops sat hundreds of low-ceilinged third- and fourth-floor storage rooms, pattern lofts, and defunct accounting offices—spaces reachable only by narrow, oil-stained concrete stairs that went completely dark and silent at 6:00 PM when the machinists padlocked their rolling shutters. Visual artists, graphic designers, independent magazine editors, and experimental musicians began renting these forgotten lofts for a fraction of Gangnam prices, hauling canvases, silkscreen tables, risograph printers, and analog synthesizers up the steep stairs after sunset.

Rather than treating the machinists downstairs as noisy neighbors to be displaced, pioneering art collectives like *Listen to the City* and curators from the 2016 Seoul Mediacity Biennale forged creative alliances across generations. In 2017, the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s *Dasi Sewoon* ('Makercity Sewoon') urban regeneration project rebuilt the elevated pedestrian bridges linking Sewoon Sangga across Cheonggyecheon and opened collaborative laboratories where seventy-five-year-old vacuum-tube amplifier masters mentored twenty-four-year-old industrial design students. Young designers began commissioning Iron Alley *Jangin* to spin brass lampshades, mill custom camera rigs, weld raw steel gallery furniture, and cast acrylic sculptures—injecting fresh design revenue into aging family workshops while compiling illustrated oral-history directories of the neighborhood’s master artisans.

Out of that collision between 1960s heavy manufacturing and twenty-first-century youth culture emerged the aesthetic phenomenon known across Korea as *Hipjiro* (힙지로, 'Hip Euljiro') and *Newtro* (뉴트로, 'New Retro'). For a generation of Korean Millennials and Gen Z raised inside uniform high-rise apartment complexes and glass shopping malls, Euljiro’s weathered concrete, hand-painted Hangul signboards, green terrazzo staircases, and rippled *moru* privacy glass felt thrillingly authentic. When young bartenders, coffee roasters, and pastry chefs opened venues in the upstairs lofts, they left the peeling 1970s calendar posters, exposed steel ceiling beams, and exterior enamel signage for long-closed pharmacies (*Yangguk*), tailor shops (*Yangbokjeom*), dental laboratories (*Gikongso*), or aluminum sash fabricators completely intact.

By nightfall, this refusal to erase the past turns Euljiro into a theatrical wonderland. Walk down a pitch-black Sanlim-dong alley at 8:30 PM after the last flatbed truck has departed, and you will see twenty-somethings queuing quietly beside stacks of copper pipe and bags of brass turnings. Push open what looks like an unplugged, rust-spotted 1980s commercial refrigerator door or a dented steel fire door stenciled *'Authorized Personnel Only,'* climb a steep flight of terrazzo steps, and you step into spaces like *Seendosi*, *Hotel Seine (Hosseup)*, or *Cheonggyecheon Salon*: crimson velvet drapes, vintage rotary phones, analog turntables spinning 1970s Korean psychedelic rock by Shin Joong-hyun alongside Miles Davis, natural wines poured under stained-glass lamps, and rooftop terraces looking straight out over the neon silhouettes of Sewoon Sangga. Even the daytime *dabang* coffeehouses—such as *Eulji Dabang*, where blue-collar printers have stirred raw egg yolks into steaming mugs of *ssanghwacha* (herbal medicinal tea) since 1985—now welcome young illustrators sketching beside septuagenarian pressmen.

Down at street level near Euljiro 3-ga Exit 4, the night belongs to an older, roaring ritual: *Nogari Golmok* (노가리 골목, 'Nogari Alley'), designated a Seoul Future Heritage site in 2015, alongside the famous working-class culinary alleys of Jung-gu. The beer tradition began in 1980 when a tiny six-table pub named *Eulji OB Bear* started tapping fresh, unpasteurized draft beer delivered straight from the brewery alongside *nogari*—juvenile dried Arctic pollock split by hand, brushed with oil, grilled over glowing *yeontan* coal briquettes until crispy outside and chewy within, and served with a fiery dipping bowl of aged *gochujang* chili paste and malt syrup for a single dollar. Today, establishments like *Manseon Hof* deploy over a thousand red plastic tables and stools across the entire four-way intersection from spring to late autumn, seating grey-haired lathe operators in oil-stained work vests shoulder-to-shoulder with screenwriters, bankers, and university students under a rising cloud of woodsmoke and laughter.

Beyond grilled pollock, Euljiro’s inner passageways preserve the hearty working-class gastronomy that fueled South Korea’s twentieth-century industrial takeoff. After ten hours of breathing metallic dust, paper fibers, and soldering flux, machinists and printers have gathered since the 1960s over heaped platters of *Euljiro Golbaengi*—sweet-and-sour whelk sea snails tossed in a mountain of shredded green onions, crushed garlic, and fiery *gochugaru* with thin wheat noodles—or around cast-iron skillets of *sanbul gogigui* (charcoal-grilled marinated pork belly) and simmering *jeongol* hotpots. Just two blocks east in Jugyo-dong stands the legendary *Woo Lae Oak* (우래옥), founded in 1946 by displaced northern refugees, where third-generation cooks still press 100-percent buckwheat *Pyongyang naengmyeon* noodles into icy, crystal-clear beef brisket broth for both octogenarian craftsmen and Michelin inspectors.

Yet the equilibrium of Euljiro hangs on a knife-edge. Over the past decade, large-scale urban redevelopment zones have already razed entire blocks of century-old tool shops around Ipjeong-dong and Sewoon District 3 to erect forty-story glass office and luxury condominium towers. Urban sociologists and craftsmen warn that when a single specialized guild—such as the wooden pattern-makers, the heat-treaters, or the brass electroplaters—is evicted to distant suburban industrial parks, ten neighboring workshops lose the 300-meter walkability that makes Euljiro work. In response, coalitions of veteran *Jangin*, young architects, and *Hipjiro* studio owners have organized the 'Euljiro Light Way' festival, open-factory tours, and heritage mapping campaigns—fighting to prove that a global megacity is most creative when the blue spark of the machinist’s torch still illuminates the artist’s table upstairs. As dusk settles over Cheonggyecheon and the lathe motors hum alongside upstairs turntables, Euljiro remains Seoul’s most vital workshop—an irreplaceable urban commons where seventy years of industrial grit and new-generation imagination forge the city of tomorrow.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Urban Agglomeration Economics: Why the '300-Meter Supply Chain' Matters

Urban economists point to Euljiro as a textbook case of 'Marshallian industrial districts,' where thousands of tiny, hyper-specialized workshops achieve economies of scale not by merging into a single giant factory, but by clustering within footsteps of one another. If a single link in the chain—such as the electroplaters or the wooden mold-makers—is evicted by real-estate speculation, ten neighboring trades lose their speed advantage. Preserving Euljiro is therefore not mere nostalgia; it is preserving a living rapid-prototyping laboratory within a global megacity. 2. Kim Swoo-geun's Sewoon Sangga and the 'Makercity' Rebirth

When architect Kim Swoo-geun built Sewoon Sangga in 1967 over a World War II firebreak evacuation strip running north-south from Jongmyo Shrine to Namsan, it was Korea's first mixed-use megastructure. After decades of decline when big-box electronics retailers moved to Yongsan in the 1990s, Seoul regenerated Sewoon Sangga in 2017 ('Makercity Sewoon') by rebuilding the elevated pedestrian skywalks across Cheonggyecheon and pairing seventy-year-old audio-amplifier repair masters with young hardware startups and 3D-printing labs.
