Euljiro's Metalwork Labyrinth: Where 1960s Print Shops Meet Neon Speakeasies

Korea — Inside downtown Seoul's gritty 'Hipjiro' alleys, where seventy-year-old lathe masters claim they can build a tank by afternoon and young artists hide bars behind vending machines (Post-War Industrial Era to Present (1953–Present)) • 4 min read

“Bring us a sketch on a cigarette pack at nine in the morning, and by five o’clock you can walk out of Euljiro with a working satellite—or a submarine.” — Old Euljiro machinists' saying

Turn off the wide, polished boulevards of central Seoul at Euljiro 3-ga Station, duck into a narrow brick alleyway barely wide enough for a three-wheeled cargo motorcycle (*samryuncha*), and your senses are immediately ambushed. Blue sparks arc from oxyacetylene welding torches; the air smells of hot cutting oil, fresh offset printer’s ink, and roasted instant coffee (*maxim mix*); and from a thousand open ground-floor workshops rises the rhythmic percussion of CNC lathes, sheet-metal brakes, and Heidelberg printing presses.

Welcome to Euljiro (을지로)—named in 1946 after General Eulji Mundeok, the seventh-century Goguryeo strategist who defeated the Sui Dynasty invasion—and currently known to a new generation of Seoul creatives as *Hipjiro* ('Hip Euljiro'). For seventy years since the Korean War armistice of 1953, this dense grid of two- and three-story concrete buildings between Chungmuro and Cheonggyecheon has functioned as the physical motherboard of South Korea’s industrial miracle. While giant *chaebol* conglomerates built shipyards in Ulsan and semiconductor fabs in Giheung, Euljiro remained an urban manufacturing rainforest of 10,000 micro-enterprises.

The geography of Euljiro is organized like a medieval guild city, block by block. Around Euljiro 2-ga and Inhyeon-dong cluster over 5,000 printing, bookbinding, foil-stamping, and paper-cutting shops, where delivery riders balance towers of freshly cut paper two meters high on the backs of motorcycles. Move east to Euljiro 3-ga and Sanlim-dong—the famous 'Iron Alley' (*Cheolgongso Golmok*)—and you enter a labyrinth of precision tool-and-die shops, acrylic laser-cutters, brass-spinning studios, and gear-makers. Continue to Euljiro 4-ga and Bangsan Market, and every chandelier, LED filament, architectural tile, and wallpaper pattern in South Korea glitters from floor to ceiling.

Anchoring the entire district is *Sewoon Sangga* (세운상가—literally 'The Arcade Where the Energy of the World Gathers'), a kilometer-long modernist megastructure designed in 1966 by Korea’s pioneering modern architect Kim Swoo-geun. Conceived as a city-within-a-city combining ground-floor electronics markets, third-floor elevated pedestrian decks, and upper-story apartments, Sewoon Sangga became the birthplace of Korea’s personal computer and electronics industry in the 1970s and 1980s. Teenagers and engineers wandered its corridors buying vacuum tubes, soldering irons, and pirated circuit-board schematics; Korea’s first commercial microcomputer, the SE-8001 (1980), was engineered right inside its stalls.

Why do industrial designers, film prop masters, and robotics startups still flock to Euljiro’s seventy-year-old master technicians (*Jangin*) instead of ordering parts online? Because in Euljiro, the entire supply chain exists within a three-minute walk. An inventor can sketch a prototype bracket on a scrap of cardboard at 10:00 AM, have it CAD-modeled next door at 10:30, milled out of aircraft aluminum three doors down by noon, anodized across the alley at 2:00 PM, wired with custom LEDs inside Sewoon Sangga at 3:30, and packaged in a custom-printed box in Inhyeon-dong before dinner.

Around 2015, as commercial rents soared in gentrified neighborhoods like Hongdae, Itaewon, and Garosu-gil, young visual artists, graphic designers, and musicians began renting the vacant, low-ceilinged third- and fourth-floor storage lofts above Euljiro’s machine shops—spaces that went dark and silent every evening after 6:00 PM when the lathe operators went home. Rather than erasing the neighborhood’s gritty industrial patina with sterile white drywall, these newcomers embraced the *Newtro* ('New Retro') aesthetic: leaving peeling 1970s calendar posters, exposed concrete beams, and rusty steel doors intact.

By nightfall, Euljiro undergoes a cinematic metamorphosis. After the iron shutters of the hardware stores rattle down and the last flatbed truck pulls out of the alley, young locals line up outside unmarked rusted steel doors, faded signage for defunct 1980s pharmacies or aluminum-window shops, and vintage vending machines that swing open to reveal candlelit vinyl bars, natural-wine bistros, and rooftop terraces overlooking neon signs. Places like *Hosseup* ('Hotel Seine'), *Seendosi*, and *Manseon Hof*—where thousands of plastic tables spill across an entire four-way intersection known as 'Nogari Alley,' serving dried pollock (*nogari*) roasted over briquette fires with ice-cold draft beer for a dollar—blend blue-collar machinists in work vests with twenty-something filmmakers.

Yet this collision between 1960s heavy craft and 2020s nightlife is not without tension: large-scale redevelopment plans periodically threaten to bulldoze blocks of Iron Alley for glass office towers, displacing third-generation machinists and letterpress printers whose interconnected ecosystem cannot survive in isolated suburban industrial parks. In response, collectives of young designers and veteran craftsmen have teamed up to launch 'Euljiro Light Way' festivals, open-studio maps, and collaborative furniture lines—proving that a city’s future is richest when the spark of the welder’s torch illuminates the artist’s table.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Urban Agglomeration Economics: Why the '300-Meter Supply Chain' Matters

Urban economists point to Euljiro as a textbook case of 'Marshallian industrial districts,' where thousands of tiny, hyper-specialized workshops achieve economies of scale not by merging into a single giant factory, but by clustering within footsteps of one another. If a single link in the chain—such as the electroplaters or the wooden mold-makers—is evicted by real-estate speculation, ten neighboring trades lose their speed advantage. Preserving Euljiro is therefore not mere nostalgia; it is preserving a living rapid-prototyping laboratory within a global megacity. 2. Kim Swoo-geun's Sewoon Sangga and the 'Makercity' Rebirth

When architect Kim Swoo-geun built Sewoon Sangga in 1967 over a World War II firebreak evacuation strip running north-south from Jongmyo Shrine to Namsan, it was Korea's first mixed-use megastructure. After decades of decline when big-box electronics retailers moved to Yongsan in the 1990s, Seoul regenerated Sewoon Sangga in 2017 ('Makercity Sewoon') by rebuilding the elevated pedestrian skywalks across Cheonggyecheon and pairing seventy-year-old audio-amplifier repair masters with young hardware startups and 3D-printing labs.
