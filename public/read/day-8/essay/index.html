On Running After One's Hat

G. K. Chesterton (1908) • 8 min read

An adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered; an inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered.

Key Context & Why It Matters

G. K. Chesterton was one of the most prolific and beloved journalists in Edwardian England: a huge, cheerful man in a cape and a crumpled hat who wrote almost every kind of thing, and who turned out newspaper essays at astonishing speed. This one comes from his weekly column in the Daily News and was gathered into the 1908 book All Things Considered. It was prompted by news that London had been flooded while he was away in the country. Rather than complain, he does something typical of him and plays. He asks why we call something an inconvenience when we could call it an adventure. Under the jokes about gondolas, hats blown across Hyde Park and stuck drawers lies a serious belief that Chesterton held all his life, that ordinary life is full of wonder and we lose it through habit and bad temper. Listen for how he uses nonsense to argue for gratitude. The essay's meaning is that your mood, and not the event, decides whether a mishap is a misery or a story.

I feel an almost savage envy on hearing that London has been flooded in my absence, while I am in the mere country. My own Battersea has been, I understand, particularly favoured as a meeting of the waters. Battersea was already, as I need hardly say, the most beautiful of human localities. Now that it has the additional splendour of great sheets of water, there must be something quite incomparable in the landscape (or waterscape) of my own romantic town. Battersea must be a vision of Venice. The boat that brought the meat from the butcher's must have shot along those lanes of rippling silver with the strange smoothness of the gondola. The greengrocer who brought cabbages to the corner of the Latchmere Road must have leant upon the oar with the unearthly grace of the gondolier. There is nothing so perfectly poetical as an island; and when a district is flooded it becomes an archipelago.

Some consider such romantic views of flood or fire slightly lacking in reality. But really this romantic view of such inconveniences is quite as practical as the other. The true optimist who sees in such things an opportunity for enjoyment is quite as logical and much more sensible than the ordinary “Indignant Ratepayer” who sees in them an opportunity for grumbling. Real pain, as in the case of being burnt at Smithfield or having a toothache, is a positive thing; it can be supported, but scarcely enjoyed. But, after all, our toothaches are the exception, and as for being burnt at Smithfield, it only happens to us at the very longest intervals. And most of the inconveniences that make men swear or women cry are really sentimental or imaginative inconveniences—things altogether of the mind. For instance, we often hear grown-up people complaining of having to hang about a railway station and wait for a train. Did you ever hear a small boy complain of having to hang about a railway station and wait for a train? No; for to him to be inside a railway station is to be inside a cavern of wonder and a palace of poetical pleasures. Because to him the red light and the green light on the signal are like a new sun and a new moon. Because to him when the wooden arm of the signal falls down suddenly, it is as if a great king had thrown down his staff as a signal and started a shrieking tournament of trains. I myself am of little boys' habit in this matter. They also serve who only stand and wait for the two fifteen. Their meditations may be full of rich and fruitful things. Many of the most purple hours of my life have been passed at Clapham Junction, which is now, I suppose, under water. I have been there in many moods so fixed and mystical that the water might well have come up to my waist before I noticed it particularly. But in the case of all such annoyances, as I have said, everything depends upon the emotional point of view. You can safely apply the test to almost every one of the things that are currently talked of as the typical nuisance of daily life.

For instance, there is a current impression that it is unpleasant to have to run after one's hat. Why should it be unpleasant to the well-ordered and pious mind? Not merely because it is running, and running exhausts one. The same people run much faster in games and sports. The same people run much more eagerly after an uninteresting, little leather ball than they will after a nice silk hat. There is an idea that it is humiliating to run after one's hat; and when people say it is humiliating they mean that it is comic. It certainly is comic; but man is a very comic creature, and most of the things he does are comic—eating, for instance. And the most comic things of all are exactly the things that are most worth doing—such as making love. A man running after a hat is not half so ridiculous as a man running after a wife.

Now a man could, if he felt rightly in the matter, run after his hat with the manliest ardour and the most sacred joy. He might regard himself as a jolly huntsman pursuing a wild animal, for certainly no animal could be wilder. In fact, I am inclined to believe that hat-hunting on windy days will be the sport of the upper classes in the future. There will be a meet of ladies and gentlemen on some high ground on a gusty morning. They will be told that the professional attendants have started a hat in such-and-such a thicket, or whatever be the technical term. Notice that this employment will in the fullest degree combine sport with humanitarianism. The hunters would feel that they were not inflicting pain. Nay, they would feel that they were inflicting pleasure, rich, almost riotous pleasure, upon the people who were looking on. When last I saw an old gentleman running after his hat in Hyde Park, I told him that a heart so benevolent as his ought to be filled with peace and thanks at the thought of how much unaffected pleasure his every gesture and bodily attitude were at that moment giving to the crowd.

The same principle can be applied to every other typical domestic worry. A gentleman trying to get a fly out of the milk or a piece of cork out of his glass of wine often imagines himself to be irritated. Let him think for a moment of the patience of anglers sitting by dark pools, and let his soul be immediately irradiated with gratification and repose. Again, I have known some people of very modern views driven by their distress to the use of theological terms to which they attached no doctrinal significance, merely because a drawer was jammed tight and they could not pull it out. A friend of mine was particularly afflicted in this way. Every day his drawer was jammed, and every day in consequence it was something else that rhymes to it. But I pointed out to him that this sense of wrong was really subjective and relative; it rested entirely upon the assumption that the drawer could, should, and would come out easily. “But if,” I said, “you picture to yourself that you are pulling against some powerful and oppressive enemy, the struggle will become merely exciting and not exasperating. Imagine that you are tugging up a lifeboat out of the sea. Imagine that you are roping up a fellow-creature out of an Alpine crevass. Imagine even that you are a boy again and engaged in a tug-of-war between French and English.” Shortly after saying this I left him; but I have no doubt at all that my words bore the best possible fruit. I have no doubt that every day of his life he hangs on to the handle of that drawer with a flushed face and eyes bright with battle, uttering encouraging shouts to himself, and seeming to hear all round him the roar of an applauding ring.

So I do not think that it is altogether fanciful or incredible to suppose that even the floods in London may be accepted and enjoyed poetically. Nothing beyond inconvenience seems really to have been caused by them; and inconvenience, as I have said, is only one aspect, and that the most unimaginative and accidental aspect of a really romantic situation. An adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered. An inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered. The water that girdled the houses and shops of London must, if anything, have only increased their previous witchery and wonder. For as the Roman Catholic priest in the story said: “Wine is good with everything except water,” and on a similar principle, water is good with everything except wine.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The essay begins with a joke about envy. Chesterton has heard that London has been flooded while he is stuck in the country, and he says he feels an almost savage envy. His own district, Battersea, now has the added splendor of great sheets of water, and he pictures the butcher's boat gliding like a gondola and the greengrocer leaning on the oar with the unearthly grace of a gondolier. Then comes the line that carries the rest of the essay: there is nothing so perfectly poetical as an island, and when a district is flooded it becomes an archipelago. A drowned street, seen differently, is a map of islands.

This is not just whimsy. He goes on to claim that his romantic view is as practical as the grumbling view of what he calls the Indignant Ratepayer. Real pain, like toothache, is real and cannot be talked away. But most of what makes men swear and women cry, he says, are sentimental or imaginative inconveniences, things altogether of the mind. The example of the railway station is the heart of the argument. A grown man complains about waiting for a train. A small boy sees the signal's red and green lights as a new sun and a new moon, and the falling wooden arm as a king dropping his staff to begin a shrieking tournament of trains. The station is the same. The difference is in the eye.

Then comes the title subject. Why is running after your hat unpleasant? It is not the running, since the same people sprint after a small leather ball in games. It is the idea that it is humiliating, which means that it is comic. Chesterton accepts that it is comic and says so what: eating is comic, and the most comic things, like making love, are the things most worth doing. A man chasing a hat is not half so ridiculous as a man chasing a wife. He proposes that hat-hunting may become a sport of the upper classes, with a meet on a gusty morning and professional attendants who have started a hat in a thicket.

The drawer is the funniest example. A friend curses a jammed drawer daily, and Chesterton advises him to imagine he is hauling a lifeboat from the sea, or roping a person out of an Alpine crevasse, or playing tug-of-war between the French and the English. The jammed drawer is the same, but the mind has been given a story.

All of this builds to the maxim that closes the argument: an adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered, and an inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered. It sounds like a paradox, and it is, but it is meant seriously. A first-time reader might miss that the essay is also a quiet case for gratitude, the idea Chesterton explored at length in his book Orthodoxy that same year, that the world is a surprising gift and the proper response is wonder. The final joke, about water being good with everything except wine, adds a last wink.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a piece of comic writing, this essay is a small triumph of control. Chesterton starts with a tall tale, the flooded Venice of Battersea, and the absurd specificity of the details, the cabbages at the corner of Latchmere Road, makes the joke work. He moves from the flood to the railway station to the hat to the drawer, and each step is a new demonstration of one idea, so that the essay feels like a juggler adding balls. The pace is brisk and the examples escalate in absurdity until they reach the lifeboat and the Alpine crevasse, where the language swells into mock-heroic grandeur and then deflates with the picture of a man tugging a drawer with a flushed face and bright eyes.

The key strength is the central reversal, the claim that the inconvenience is a matter of the point of view. It is memorable because it is a little true. Anyone who has laughed at their own missed bus after the fact knows that a mishap can become a story, and Chesterton's formulation captures this in a line short enough to carry around. It is also genuinely funny without being cruel. He teases the Indignant Ratepayer, but the real target is the part of every reader that likes to complain.

Yet the essay has weaknesses worth admitting. The argument is a bit of a sleight of hand. By setting aside "real pain" in a single sentence and then talking only about trivial annoyances, Chesterton avoids the hard case. A flood is a joke for a man in the country who is not in it, and a reader may reasonably feel that his romance of the flood depends on never having lost a home. He does say that nothing beyond inconvenience seems to have happened, which is a statement of faith as much as fact, and the confident tone in the face of real hardship can feel glib.

The method of paradox, which was his trademark, can also wear thin. His habit of flipping a common view and declaring the opposite to be sensible is delightful in small doses, but across a whole column it can feel mechanical, as if the conclusion were chosen first and the evidence arranged afterward. Some critics over the years have felt that he sometimes let cleverness stand in for argument, and this essay shows a trace of that tendency.

Some of the jokes are also dated. The reference to being burnt at Smithfield, the Ratepayer figure, and the notion of hats as a gentleman's necessity belong to Edwardian London, and a modern listener may need a moment to catch them. But these are small blemishes. The essay's rhythm, its confident comic voice, and its sheer inventiveness make it sparkle. It works best as what it is, a light, joyful intervention in a bad mood, and by that modest standard it is nearly perfect.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be wrong to claim that this single column changed history. Its influence is quieter and wider, and it lies in what it shows about Chesterton's whole approach. The essay appeared in his weekly column in the Daily News, and was collected in All Things Considered in 1908, one of the books that made him a household name. That same year saw the publication of Orthodoxy and the novel The Man Who Was Thursday, a burst of creativity that fixed his public image as the great defender of common sense, wonder and tradition, clothed in humor.

In terms of his career, pieces like this one established the Chesterton voice: the genial paradox, the joyful nonsense that turns out to be sense. The idea that you could write serious moral thinking disguised as a joke about hats and drawers became a signature. It prepared the ground for his Father Brown stories, which began in 1910 and used the same trick of seeming simplicity to hide deep insight.

Other writers have admired and borrowed the approach. W. H. Auden was a great fan. Jorge Luis Borges praised Chesterton's gift for turning paradox into story, and Marshall McLuhan, among others, cited him as an influence. The Catholic writers of the following generations, such as Evelyn Waugh and Graham Greene, were aware of his example, and the modern tradition of the humorous newspaper columnist who slips philosophy into a small domestic anecdote owes something to him. Writers of light essays on everyday life, from Robert Benchley to later columnists, work a territory he helped open.

The essay's central idea has also entered the culture in a way that people often do not trace back. The saying that an adventure is an inconvenience rightly considered is frequently quoted, sometimes in slightly altered form, in travel writing, scouting and outdoor education, where it is used to encourage young people to treat a rained-out camping trip as part of the fun. Therapists and writers on resilience arrive at similar notions of reframing, though they use different language. Chesterton got there by joke rather than by theory.

Today the essay is chiefly known through anthologies of his work, and through the large society of Chesterton readers, including the American Chesterton Society and many online groups. It is sometimes assigned in classes on the personal essay as an example of comic argument. It has not become a canonical text of politics or philosophy, but it is a favorite of those who want a good-humored way into Chesterton's thought.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers love about this essay begins with its sheer joy. Reading it feels like being in the company of someone who is delighted with the world and wants to share the delight. There are essays that argue for gratitude in solemn language, and then there is this one, which argues for it by imagining a jolly huntsman pursuing a wild hat across Hyde Park. The humor is the vehicle for the message, and many readers find that they take the message in more deeply because they are laughing.

They also love the images. A flooded South London becomes Venice. A railway station is a cavern of wonder. A man hauling on a stuck drawer imagines the roar of an applauding ring. These images are so vivid and unexpected that they stay in memory, and they reward being repeated aloud, which is part of why the essay works so well when heard rather than read. Chesterton wrote with a speaker's ear, and his sentences have a musical bounce.

Another source of affection is its kindness. For all its teasing, the essay never sneers at ordinary people. The old gentleman running after his hat in the park is told he is giving unaffected pleasure to the crowd, which turns what looks like humiliation into a gift. Readers have found a dignity in that, an invitation to forgive yourself for looking foolish. Many have said it is a good essay to read on a bad day, because it makes minor misfortune feel lighter.

Writers have admired the sheer technique. The sentence that closes the argument, with its neat mirror structure, is a model of epigram. Admirers point to the way Chesterton can turn a phrase so that it seems obvious only after he has said it. This is why it is so quoted. Fans of the essay as a form value it as a demonstration of what the genre does best: taking a trivial occasion, here a news item about a flood, and spinning from it a small piece of philosophy.

Finally, readers respond to the generous worldview. The essay says that the world is more interesting than we allow, that the child's eye is the right eye, and that wonder is a discipline we can practice. In a culture that often rewards cynicism and complaint, that is a refreshing offer. Many people return to it as a tonic, and it has survived over a century because the mood it cures has not gone away.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Chesterton was loved and criticized in about equal measure, and the objections to this sort of essay are fairly consistent. The most common is that he is glib. Critics have long complained that his paradoxes can be shallow, that he gets his effects by turning a statement on its head without really proving the reverse. In this essay the claim that most annoyances are imaginary skates quickly over the difficult cases. One can accept that the hat chase is comic and still feel that the argument gets a free pass by choosing small examples.

A second criticism is about class and comfort. To treat a flood as a poetic opportunity requires being safe and dry. Chesterton wrote the piece from the countryside, away from the water, and some readers have found the whimsy a little insensitive, since a flooded home is a serious loss for people with little money. The essay does acknowledge real pain, but it does so briefly and moves on, and a socially minded reader may wish for more recognition of the difference between choosing an adventure and having a disaster thrust upon you. Chesterton cared about the poor, and was a critic of industrial capitalism, but this column does not show that side.

There is also the old complaint that his cheerfulness is a form of evasion. George Bernard Shaw, his friend and sparring partner, teased him about his optimism, and others thought he treated serious problems too lightly. Later writers, shaped by the grimmer experiences of the twentieth century, sometimes find the Edwardian good humor naive, though Chesterton himself lived through the First World War and did not lose his convictions.

Another line of criticism concerns the broader Chesterton legacy. Some of his other writings include attitudes, especially toward Jews, that have been rightly criticized as prejudiced, and readers coming to his work today often keep that in mind. It is not part of this essay, which is entirely free of such matter, but it colors some of the modern conversation about him, and it is fair to mention that a reader's enjoyment of the humor should not mean ignoring the larger record.

Finally, a purely literary objection holds that the essay is slight. It is a newspaper column, written quickly, and some critics regard it as a pleasant trifle rather than a lasting work. That is probably the fairest statement of its limits. It does not try to be profound in structure, and judged as a trifle it succeeds spectacularly. Its weaknesses are the weaknesses of lightness, and the trade-off is part of its appeal.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This essay belongs to the English tradition of the familiar essay, the short, personal, conversational piece that takes up an ordinary subject and turns it over until it yields a surprise. That tradition runs from Michel de Montaigne in France and Francis Bacon in England, through the eighteenth century essayists Joseph Addison and Richard Steele, to the Romantic essayists Charles Lamb and William Hazlitt, and on to Robert Louis Stevenson. Chesterton inherited the form and added his own gift for paradox and comic exaggeration.

It also belongs to the wider Edwardian moment, when newspapers and magazines gave writers enormous platforms. Chesterton wrote constantly for papers, and along with his friends Hilaire Belloc and George Bernard Shaw he was part of a vigorous public argument about progress, religion and modern life. Where many contemporaries, including some of the great Victorian pessimists, were gloomy about industrial society, Chesterton insisted on wonder. His thinking descends partly from Charles Dickens, whose love of ordinary people and whose comic sense of the city shaped him deeply, and from the child's sense of enchantment that Romantic poets prized.

What descends from it? The line of humane, humorous social commentary in newspapers, which includes writers like E. B. White in America, owes something to this model, as does the practice of finding philosophical themes in minor events. Chesterton's idea of gratitude and wonder also feeds into later Christian writers such as C. S. Lewis, who credited Chesterton's book The Everlasting Man with changing his thinking. And his emphasis on seeing the familiar as strange anticipates the modern interest in defamiliarization, the literary technique of making ordinary things look new.

Compared with a similar piece such as Thoreau's essays on attention, Chesterton is funnier and less solitary. Where Thoreau retreats to the woods to see freshly, Chesterton finds the same freshness in the railway station and the street. Compared with Charles Lamb's gentle humor, Chesterton is louder and more argumentative. He writes with a boxer's relish for a fight with the grumbling spirit.

Why read it today? Because the grievance culture it teases is still with us, and the remedy it offers remains remarkably practical. The next time the train is late or the drawer sticks or your hat is racing toward the pond, the essay suggests a small experiment: change the story and see whether the mood changes. It will not fix a real tragedy, and Chesterton did not say it would. But for the large, steady stream of minor irritations that make up much of a day, it is a cheerful and genuinely useful piece of advice, delivered with a wink and a rolling laugh.
