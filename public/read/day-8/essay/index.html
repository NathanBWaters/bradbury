On Running After One's Hat: Romanticism in Everyday Inconvenience

G. K. Chesterton (1908) • 8 min read

An adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered; an inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered.

I feel an almost savage envy on hearing that London has been flooded in my absence, while I am in the mere country. My own Battersea has been, I understand, particularly favoured as a meeting of the waters. Battersea was already, as I need hardly say, the most beautiful of human localities. Now that it has the additional splendour of great sheets of water, there must be something quite incomparable in the landscape (or waterscape) of my own romantic town. Battersea must be a vision of Venice. The boat that I am told is paddling down the High Street probably carries a classic load of lovers; and the old gentleman whom I have seen nodding above the tobacconist's counter now doubtless chants an eerie boat-song as he punts a gondola past the chemist's.

Yet I have little doubt that while I sit here in the country envying them their unexpected Venetian carnival, the actual inhabitants of Battersea are standing on their chairs and tables grumbling about the damp and the delay of the omnibus. There is nothing more strange in human nature than this almost universal refusal to see the poetry of an interruption until fifty years after it is over. Whenever the routine of a great modern city is broken by a snowstorm, a thick fog, or an overflowing river, nine men out of ten treat it merely as a breakdown of the machinery, instead of recognizing that they have been dropped without charge into the middle of a fairy tale.

There is a current impression that it is unpleasant to have to run after one's hat. Why should it be unpleasant to the well-ordered and pious mind? Not merely because it is running, and running exhausts the lungs; for the very same people who curse the wind when they chase a silk hat down the Strand will pay heavy subscriptions to a club in order to run after a little leather ball across a muddy field. There is an idea that it is humiliating to run after one's hat; and when people say it is humiliating they mean that it is comic. It certainly is comic; but man is a very comic creature, and most of the things he does are comic—eating, for instance. And the most comic things of all are exactly the things that are most worth doing—such as making love. A man running after a hat is not half so ridiculous as a man running after a wife.

The truth is that a man running after a hat is actually performing a great public and philanthropic service. Far more than the dry judges on the bench or the solemn members of Parliament, he is contributing directly to the innocent merriment of the commonwealth. When I last saw an elderly gentleman of portly build pursuing his own shiny top-hat down Victoria Street before a brisk March gale, I felt that my heart went out to him with a gratitude that words could hardly express. Every time his fingers almost closed upon the brim and the hat gave a playful, kitten-like bound of another ten yards down the pavement, the faces of exhausted clerks, anxious mothers, and sour-visaged cabmen along the street lit up with a sudden, brotherly radiance. If we were truly philosophical, we would take off our hats to the man who has lost his hat.

Indeed, a man might choose to look at his own runaway hat not as a runaway servant, but as a wild and agile quarry in a royal chase. Why should a gentleman travel all the way to Scotland to stalk a stag, or to India to hunt a tiger, when heaven provides him at his own front gate with a creature far more capricious, swift, and elusive in the shape of a bowler hat caught in a gust around the corner of Sloane Square? Suppose that as you dash forward, you imagine that the hat is not yet captured, not yet tamed to the domestic hat-peg, but is a free child of the tempest which only the swiftest huntsman can bring to bay.

It is exactly the same with every other petty annoyance of modern travel and domestic life. Take, for example, the universal complaint of being kept waiting for an hour at a small country railway junction because the train has been missed. The ordinary traveler paces up and down the platform fuming at his watch, glaring at the time-table, and cursing the directors of the railway company as if they had conspired against his birthright. Yet why? He has been given an unscheduled hour of pure, unpurchasable liberty in a place he would never otherwise have visited in his entire life. Look at the posters on the waiting-room wall; talk to the porter about his cabbages; inspect the strange architecture of the signal-box; read the local newspaper's account of the vicar's flower-show; and you will find that the missed train was the only train worth catching.

Suppose a tube lifts breaks down between floors, or a drawer sticks in your writing-desk when you are in a hurry to find an envelope. If you tug at the drawer with blind, red-faced rage, you feel as if all the malignity of the cosmos were concentrated in two inches of mahogany. If, on the other hand, you step back and regard the stubborn drawer as an ingenious fortification defended by wood-elves, or as a contest of patience between human wit and the ancient stubbornness of the oak forest, your irritation vanishes in an instant. The inconvenience has not changed by a hair's breadth; only your angle of vision has shifted.

For an inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered; an adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered. The water that girdled the houses and shops of Battersea was an inconvenience to the grocer who wanted to deliver his potatoes dry-shod; but it was the very substance of romance to the child looking out of the nursery window who saw the street transformed into a pirate sea, and it is the very substance of romance to the poet. Look back at any great epic—at the Odyssey of Homer or the voyages of Sinbad—and ask yourself what those immortal adventures actually consisted of when they were happening. Shipwrecks, missed connections, bad weather, lost luggage, and uncooperative monsters! Odysseus was simply a gentleman who took ten years to get home because the transport service in the Aegean was abominably disorganized.

On every side of us the modern world is full of people who are starving for romance while standing knee-deep in the materials of romance, simply because they insist that an adventure must wear the plumed helmet of the Middle Ages or carry a cutlass on the Spanish Main, while their own daily existence must run on oiled rails without a jolt. If you want your life to be a great story, you must stop demanding that nothing should ever go wrong with the timetable. When the wind blows your hat down the street, run after it with the joy of a boy chasing a butterfly; and when the flood-waters rise around the lamp-post, launch your wash-tub upon the tide and thank God that Venice has come to your door.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

Published in his 1908 collection All Things Considered and drawn from his weekly columns for The Illustrated London News, 'On Running After One's Hat' is the signature familiar essay of Gilbert Keith Chesterton (1874–1936)—the rotund, cape-wearing, sword-stick-carrying prince of Edwardian paradox. Writing in the golden age of British journalism when Londoners commuted by omnibus and underground train amidst rapid industrial standardization, Chesterton waged a lifelong campaign against modern cynicism, pessimism, and boredom, arguing that the ordinary world is far stranger and more magical than any fairy land. Every night you spend weighing an essay's central metaphor against a short story and a poem trains your own intellect to see beneath the surface of convention and discover original connections across centuries of human thought.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

Chesterton's entire philosophy of life and perception is distilled into a single immortal chiasmus near the close of the essay: 'An inconvenience is only an adventure wrongly considered; an adventure is only an inconvenience rightly considered.' Why do wealthy people pay club fees to chase a leather ball across mud while cursing when they have to chase their own silk hat down the street? Only because ego and false dignity blind them to the comic comedy of being human. A flooded suburb becomes Venice; a runaway bowler hat becomes a royal stag-hunt; a missed train at a country junction becomes an unscheduled hour of freedom; a stuck desk drawer becomes a contest with the oak forest. Reality does not need to change—only the story our imagination tells about it.

3. Craft, Form & Rhetorical Technique

Chesterton is the supreme master of the comic reversal and the philosophical paradox. Notice how he takes the most mundane, slightly embarrassing spectacle in Edwardian London—a portly gentleman chasing his windblown top-hat down Victoria Street—and elevates it through mock-heroic diction into a 'great public and philanthropic service' and a royal hunt. He pairs high literary allusion (Odysseus taking ten years to get home because 'the transport service in the Aegean was abominably disorganized') with slapstick physical comedy ('A man running after a hat is not half so ridiculous as a man running after a wife'), ending with an exuberant call to wonder.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Jorge Luis Borges—one of the most Chesterton-obsessed writers of the twentieth century—praised Chesterton for never allowing a single page to sink into melancholy or dullness, calling him a master of 'lucid joy.' Alongside C. S. Lewis, Dorothy L. Sayers, and Neil Gaiman, Ray Bradbury himself cited Chesterton as one of his favorite essayists and storytellers, admiring how Chesterton discovered magic inside the everyday hardware of modern towns—barbershops, lampposts, railway stations, and top hats. 5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 8, Chesterton's 'On Running After One's Hat' collides with Anton Chekhov's 'The Lady with the Dog' and John Keats's 'Ode on a Grecian Urn.' Look at the three ways these masters treat time, romance, and imperfection: on Keats's urn, romance is preserved only by freezing the lovers in marble before they can ever kiss or suffer a single inconvenience; in Chekhov's story, Gurov and Anna discover real, enduring love precisely in the midst of inconvenient train journeys, provincial hotels, and aging; and Chesterton hands you the secret key: stop waiting for an unblemished Grecian urn of a day—your missed train and windblown hat ARE the Odyssey.
