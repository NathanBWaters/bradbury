Ulleungdo & Dokdo: Deep-Sea Volcanic Seamounts, Udegi Snow Houses, and East Sea Ecology

Korea — Where Two-Thousand-Meter Submarine Stratovolcanoes Pierce the East Sea, An Yong-bok Sailed in 1693, and Hemlock-Timber Cabins Endure Three-Meter Winter Blizzards (Korean Historical & Cultural Era) • 12 min read

“In the sixth month of the thirteenth year of King Jijeung (512 CE), Usan-guk submitted to Silla; its island rises precipitous and sheer from the eastern deep.” — Kim Bu-sik, Samguk Sagi (1145)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rising more than 2,000 meters from the abyssal floor of the East Sea (Ulleung Basin) without a single meter of continental bridge, Ulleungdo and its eastern rocky sister Dokdo (87.4 kilometers farther seaward) are the exposed summits of massive Cenozoic submarine stratovolcanoes. Because neither island was ever connected to the Eurasian mainland during Pleistocene glacial sea-level lowstands, Ulleungdo evolved through rare anagenetic speciation into an oceanic Galapagos of towering Ulleung beech (Nerdobamnamu), hemlock-pine forests, and nearly forty endemic plant species inside the collapsed Nari Basin caldera. Recorded as Usan-guk in the Samguk Sagi (512 CE) and defended diplomatically by the Busan fisherman-scholar An Yong-bok in 1693–1696, the islands fostered a remarkable maritime folk architecture—the double-walled Udegi snow house inside the Nari caldera—built to survive three-meter winter snowfalls driven by Siberian-East Sea lake-effect blizzards.

If you drain the waters of the East Sea in a bathymetric simulation, the true scale of Ulleungdo and Dokdo shocks the eye. What appear above the waves as a pentagonal emerald fortress crowned by Seonginbong Peak (984 meters above sea level) and a pair of jagged volcanic crags eighty-seven kilometers to the southeast are in fact the subaerial tips of colossal submarine mountains rising more than 2,200 to 3,000 meters straight up from the abyssal floor of the Ulleung Basin. In total vertical relief from ocean floor to summit, Ulleungdo (~3,000 meters) is taller than Mount Baekdusan (2,744 meters). Dokdo, erupted along a seafloor fracture zone between 4.6 and 2.5 million years ago during the Pliocene epoch, is the older brother by more than two million years—a vast flat-topped guyot seamount over ten kilometers wide at its submarine base, of which only two main trachyte-and-tuff peaks, Dongdo (East Islet, 98.6m) and Seodo (West Islet, 168.5m), together with eighty-nine surrounding sea stacks and reefs, remain stubbornly above the surf.

Because a deep oceanic trough over two kilometers deep separates Ulleungdo from the Korean Peninsula 130 kilometers to the west, neither Ulleungdo nor Dokdo was ever linked to the Asian continent by a land bridge, even during the Last Glacial Maximum 20,000 years ago when global sea levels dropped by 120 meters. Every spore, seed, insect, and bird that colonized these jagged basalt, trachyte, and phonolite cliffs had to cross open ocean on typhoons, migratory feathers, or drifting logs. Once stranded on Ulleungdo's steep, fog-drenched slopes, plant populations underwent what evolutionary biologists call anagenetic speciation (phyletic transformation without branching): instead of splitting into multiple competing species as on the Galapagos or Hawaiian archipelagos, single mainland founder species transformed uniformly into giant, thick-leaved island endemics adapted to maritime gales and deep winter snow. Today, nearly forty vascular plant species—including the Ulleung beech, Ulleung hemlock, Ulleung lily, and the fragrant Seombaekrihyang thyme—exist naturally nowhere else on Earth.

At the heart of Ulleungdo's northern highlands lies its only flat expanse of land: the Nari Basin, a dramatic oval caldera 1.5 kilometers wide formed roughly 19,000 years ago when the volcano's central magma chamber emptied in catastrophic pumice eruptions and collapsed inward. A final, gas-poor effusion of viscous trachyte lava later squeezed up through the caldera floor around 5,000 years ago to form Albong (611m), an 'egg-within-a-nest' secondary dome covered in virgin primeval forest. Beneath the porous volcanic pumice and scoria of the Nari Basin, rainwater and snowmelt filter downward until they hit an impermeable stratum of older basaltic tuff, gushing forth at the southern edge of the caldera as the icy Yongchulso artesian spring, which discharges thousands of tons of crystal-clear water daily at a constant five degrees Celsius even in August.

Living inside the Nari Basin required one of the most ingenious vernacular architectural adaptations on the Korean Peninsula: the Udegi house, exemplified by the preserved Tuinmak-jip (Important Folklore Cultural Heritage No. 256). Because the cold, dry winter monsoon blowing off the Siberian anticyclone picks up tremendous latent heat and moisture as it crosses the warm Tsushima Current in the East Sea, Ulleungdo experiences extreme 'sea-effect' blizzards—ranking among the snowiest inhabited places on the planet, with winter drifts routinely burying the Nari Basin under two to four meters of wet snow for four months. Pioneers who settled the caldera after King Gojong lifted the Joseon island-evacuation policy (Sutoje) in 1882 felled massive native hemlock and beech logs, notching them horizontally in Gwiteul log-cabin joinery and chinking the gaps with volcanic clay.

Around this inner log cabin—roughly a meter outside the timber walls, beneath deep overhanging eaves thatched with giant bamboo grass or cleft cedar shakes —the builders erected the Udegi: a floor-to-eave outer screen woven tightly from split bamboo and tall Eulalia miscanthus reeds. When three-meter snowbanks piled against the Udegi curtain in January, the compacted snow itself became a super-insulating thermal blanket around the house, while the one-meter gap between the outer Udegi reed wall and the inner log cabin created an enclosed perimeter corridor called the Gaetdam (imet). Inside this snow-lit indoor walkway, protected from howling sub-zero gales, families stored cured squid, dried myeongyi wild garlic leaves, chopped firewood, drew water, wove straw rope, and moved freely between kitchen, bedrooms, and livestock stalls all winter without ever stepping outdoors.

In historical chronicles, these twin volcanic outposts entered Korean state consciousness fifteen centuries ago. The Samguk Sagi (1145) records that in the sixth month of 512 CE (the thirteenth year of King Jijeung of Silla), the general and governor of Haseulla-ju, Isabu, sailed against the fiercely independent island kingdom of Usan-guk. Knowing the islanders took refuge atop inaccessible volcanic cliffs, Isabu carved ferocious wooden lions, painted them in war colors, loaded them onto the prows of his warships, and threatened to unleash the beasts unless Usan-guk yielded; awed by the stratagem, the islanders entered tributary alliance with Silla, sending annual offerings of seal skins, marine ginseng, and timber. Nine hundred years later, the geographical treatise of King Sejong's annals, the Sejong Sillok Jiriji (1454), recorded with crystalline visual precision under Uljin Prefecture: 'The two islands of Usan [Dokdo] and Mureung [Ulleungdo] lie in the sea due east of the county. The two islands are not far apart, such that on a clear day when the wind is calm, they can be seen from one another.'

That geographic truth—that from Ulleungdo's eastern ridges at 200 meters elevation, the twin peaks of Dokdo pierce the southeastern horizon like a dark whaleback whenever atmospheric visibility is clear—anchored the heroic life of An Yong-bok, a low-born naval oarsman and fisherman from Dongnae in Busan who had learned fluent Japanese at the Waegwan trading post. In the spring of 1693, when Japanese fishermen from the Oya and Murakawa merchant families of Yonago (Tottori Domain) abducted An Yong-bok and his fishing crew from Ulleungdo waters to Japan, An stood fearless before the feudal lord of Tottori and officials in Edo, presenting coastal charts and citing Joseon jurisdiction over both Ulleungdo (Jukdo) and Dokdo (Songdo/Usando).

When Oya fishermen returned in 1696, An Yong-bok pursued them from Ulleungdo past Dokdo all the way to the Oki Islands and Tottori Domain on his own initiative, displaying the Paldo Jido (Map of the Eight Provinces) and compelling the Tokugawa Shogunate in January 1696 to issue the Ulleungdo Tokai Kinshirei —explicitly banning Japanese vessels from sailing to the islands after the Tottori Domain formally testified in December 1695 that neither Ulleungdo nor Matsushima (Dokdo) belonged to Tottori territory. In the spring, as the three-meter snowpack melts across the Nari Basin, the forest floor beneath the ancient Ulleung beech trees carpets itself with the emerald broadleaves of Seom-maneul (Allium ochotense), universally beloved in Korea as Myeong-i-namul (life-preserving herb). When the late-nineteenth-century pioneers ran out of stored barley in February while still trapped by snowdrifts, they dug through a meter of crusted snow to harvest the garlicky green shoots of Myeong-i already sprouting beneath the ice; because the plant literally saved their lives (myeong, life), the islanders named it Myeong-i. Rich in allicin and sulfur compounds, its pickled leaves remain the quintessential Ulleungdo delicacy.

As summer twilight falls over Dodong and Jeodong harbors, another ecology awakens: the great nocturnal convergence of the East Sea. Just offshore, the warm, saline North Korea Warm Current (a branch of the Tsushima Current) collides head-on with the frigid, oxygen-rich Liman Cold Current flowing down from the Sea of Okhotsk, creating a permanent thermocline front teeming with euphausiid krill, anchovies, saury, and Pacific flying squid (Todarodes pacificus). Hundreds of white-painted squid-jigging boats steam outward into the indigo dark, switching on banks of incandescent and high-output LED metal-halide bulbs (Jibeodeung) that glow with the combined wattage of a medium-sized city, luring phototactic plankton and hunting squid from the mesopelagic depths while illuminating the silhouette of Ulleungdo's basalt ramparts against the stars.

Literary & Historical Analysis

Anagenetic Speciation on an Oceanic Stratovolcano

To biogeographers and evolutionary botanists, Ulleungdo is one of the world's premier natural laboratories for anagenesis—evolutionary divergence without cladogenesis (lineage splitting). On large, topographically fragmented archipelagos like Hawaii or the Canary Islands, a single colonizing plant species radiates into dozens of niche-specific sibling species (adaptive radiation). On compact, steep-sided Ulleungdo (72.9 square kilometers), where gene flow circulates continuously around the conical volcano via wind and pollinators, a mainland progenitor arriving by long-distance dispersal evolved as a single cohesive population into a wholly new endemic species—such as Fagus engleriana / Fagus multinervis (Ulleung beech) or Rubus takesimensis (thornless island raspberry, which lost its defensive thorns in the total absence of native mammalian herbivores like deer or boar). Over eighty-five percent of Ulleungdo's endemic plants arose through this pure anagenetic pathway.

Thermodynamics of the Udegi Snow-Curtain and Gwiteul Log Architecture

The Nari Basin Udegi house demonstrates how vernacular builders turn a hostile meteorological force into a structural asset. On the mainland, Korean Hanok rely on timber post-and-lintel frames infilled with thin wattle-and-daub mud walls—a design that would buckle under the lateral weight and freeze-thaw saturation of Ulleungdo's three-meter sea-effect snowpack. By constructing an inner core of interlocking horizontal Solsongnamu (hemlock) logs and surrounding it with a permeable outer reed curtain one meter out from the wall, island builders created a double-envelope buffer zone. Once winter snow packs against the Udegi reeds, the snow itself (whose trapped air pockets have a low thermal conductivity of ~0.15 W/m·K) seals out convective wind chill, maintaining the Gaetdam corridor just above freezing while the inner log cabin stays warm from the kitchen Agung-i hearth.

Intervisibility Physics and the 1454 Sejong Sillok Jiriji Testimony

For decades, armchair commentators unfamiliar with atmospheric refraction questioned whether Dokdo (168.5m high) could truly be seen with the naked eye from Ulleungdo across 87.4 kilometers of open water, as recorded in the 1454 Sejong Sillok Jiriji ('on a clear day when the wind is calm, they can be seen from one another'). Geometric earth-curvature calculations confirm that while Dokdo lies below the sea-level horizon from Ulleungdo's shoreline, an observer standing at 120 meters elevation on Ulleungdo's eastern coastal road—or at 300 meters on Seokpo ridge—has an unobstructed geometric line of sight to the upper ninety meters of Dokdo's Seodo and Dongdo peaks.

An Yong-bok's Subaltern Maritime Diplomacy in the Genroku Era

In the rigidly stratified society of late-seventeenth-century Joseon, foreign diplomacy with the Tokugawa Shogunate was the exclusive monopoly of court Tongsinsa embassies and Dongnae magistrates; unauthorized overseas voyages carried the death penalty under maritime border laws. That An Yong-bok—a low-born naval oarsman from Busan—twice sailed across the East Sea in 1693 and 1696, confronted Tottori Domain officials in their own language using provincial maps, and catalyzed the Shogunate's 1696 Tottori Answer confirming that neither Ulleungdo nor Dokdo belonged to Japan represents an extraordinary triumph of subaltern maritime agency. Even though the Joseon court exiled An Yong-bok for acting without royal credentials, it simultaneously adopted his strategic warning, instituting the triennial Sutoje naval inspection system that dispatched state inspectors to Ulleungdo every three years until the modern resettlement decree of 1882.
