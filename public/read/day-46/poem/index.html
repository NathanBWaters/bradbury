Home Burial

Robert Frost (1914) • 7 min read

On a farmhouse staircase overlooking their firstborn child’s grave, a grieving mother and father confront the tragic chasm between two ways of mourning.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Home Burial, by Robert Frost, appeared in his 1914 book North of Boston, the collection that made him famous in America after years of neglect. It is a long poem, mostly dialogue, about a husband and wife after the death of their first child. Frost wrote it while living in England, but its roots lay in his own life. His son Elliott died of cholera at age three, in 1900, and Frost and his wife Elinor were never quite the same together afterward. Frost also knew a family in which a similar loss had split a marriage. The poem is important because it takes grief out of the realm of grand speeches and puts it in the kitchen and on the staircase, in the awkward, wounding things two people actually say. Listen for what each person cannot hear in the other. What the poem is really about is how the same loss can drive two people apart, because they mourn in different languages.

He saw her from the bottom of the stairs

Before she saw him. She was starting down,

Looking back over her shoulder at some fear.

She took a doubtful step and then undid it

To raise herself and look again. He spoke

Advancing toward her: “What is it you see

From up there always—for I want to know.”

She turned and sank upon her skirts at that,

And her face changed from terrified to dull.

He said to gain time: “What is it you see,”

Mounting until she cowered under him.

“I will find out now—you must tell me, dear.”

She, in her place, refused him any help

With the least stiffening of her neck and silence.

She let him look, sure that he wouldn’t see,

Blind creature; and a while he didn’t see.

But at last he murmured, “Oh,” and again, “Oh.”

“What is it—what?” she said.

“Just that I see.”

“You don’t,” she challenged. “Tell me what it is.”

“The wonder is I didn’t see at once.

I never noticed it from here before.

I must be wonted to it—that’s the reason.

The little graveyard where my people are!

So small the window frames the whole of it.

Not so much larger than a bedroom, is it?

There are three stones of slate and one of marble,

Broad-shouldered little slabs there in the sunlight

On the sidehill. We haven’t to mind those.

But I understand: it is not the stones,

But the child’s mound—”

“Don’t, don’t, don’t, don’t,” she cried.

She withdrew shrinking from beneath his arm

That rested on the banister, and slid downstairs;

And turned on him with such a daunting look,

He said twice over before he knew himself:

“Can’t a man speak of his own child he’s lost?”

“Not you! Oh, where’s my hat? Oh, I don’t need it!

I must get out of here. I must get air.

I don’t know rightly whether any man can.”

“Amy! Don’t go to someone else this time.

Listen to me. I won’t come down the stairs.”

He sat and fixed his chin between his fists.

“There’s something I should like to ask you, dear.”

“You don’t know how to ask it.”

“Help me, then.”

Her fingers moved the latch for all reply.

“My words are nearly always an offence.

I don’t know how to speak of anything

So as to please you. But I might be taught

I should suppose. I can’t say I see how.

A man must partly give up being a man

With womenfolk. We could have some arrangement

By which I’d bind myself to keep hands off

Anything special you’re a-mind to name.

Though I don’t like such things ’twixt those that love.

Two that don’t love can’t live together without them.

But two that do can’t live together with them.”

She moved the latch a little. “Don’t—don’t go.

Don’t carry it to someone else this time.

Tell me about it if it’s something human.

Let me into your grief. I’m not so much

Unlike other folks as your standing there

Apart would make me out. Give me my chance.

I do think, though, you overdo it a little.

What was it brought you up to think it the thing

To take your mother-loss of a first child

So inconsolably—in the face of love.

You’d think his memory might be satisfied—”

“There you go sneering now!”

“I’m not, I’m not!

You make me angry. I’ll come down to you.

God, what a woman! And it’s come to this,

A man can’t speak of his own child that’s dead.”

“You can’t because you don’t know how.

If you had any feelings, you that dug

With your own hand—how could you?—his little grave;

I saw you from that very window there,

Making the gravel leap and leap in air,

Leap up, like that, like that, and land so lightly

And roll back down the mound beside the hole.

I thought, Who is that man? I didn’t know you.

And I crept down the stairs and up the stairs

To look again, and still your spade kept lifting.

Then you came in. I heard your rumbling voice

Out in the kitchen, and I don’t know why,

But I went near to see with my own eyes.

You could sit there with the stains on your shoes

Of the fresh earth from your own baby’s grave

And talk about your everyday concerns.

You had stood the spade up against the wall

Outside there in the entry, for I saw it.”

“I shall laugh the worst laugh I ever laughed.

I’m cursed. God, if I don’t believe I’m cursed.”

“I can repeat the very words you were saying.

‘Three foggy mornings and one rainy day

Will rot the best birch fence a man can build.’

Think of it, talk like that at such a time!

What had how long it takes a birch to rot

To do with what was in the darkened parlour.

You couldn’t care! The nearest friends can go

With anyone to death, comes so far short

They might as well not try to go at all.

No, from the time when one is sick to death,

One is alone, and he dies more alone.

Friends make pretence of following to the grave,

But before one is in it, their minds are turned

And making the best of their way back to life

And living people, and things they understand.

But the world’s evil. I won’t have grief so

If I can change it. Oh, I won’t, I won’t!”

“There, you have said it all and you feel better.

You won’t go now. You’re crying. Close the door.

The heart’s gone out of it: why keep it up.

Amy! There’s someone coming down the road!”

“You—oh, you think the talk is all. I must go—

Somewhere out of this house. How can I make you—”

“If—you—do!” She was opening the door wider.

“Where do you mean to go? First tell me that.

I’ll follow and bring you back by force. I will!—”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem opens like a small play. A husband stands at the bottom of a staircase and watches his wife at the top. She is looking out a window, and something she sees frightens her. He climbs toward her, asking what she is looking at, and she answers him only with a stiff neck and silence. She lets him look, sure that he will not see it, and for a while he does not. Then he says, quietly, "Oh." What she has been staring at is the little family graveyard visible from the window, and in it the small mound of their dead child.

From there the poem is a quarrel that has clearly happened many times before. The husband, whose speech is plain and a little clumsy, insists that a man ought to be able to speak of his own lost child. The wife, Amy, hears every word he says about the grave as a kind of trespass. He talks about the stones as if they were furniture, calling them "broad-shouldered little slabs there in the sunlight," and she cannot bear it. He tries to be reasonable and offers what sounds like a business arrangement, a list of topics he will keep his hands off. He says he does not like such rules between people who love each other, and then delivers his bleak little paradox: two who do not love cannot live together without such rules, and two who do cannot live together with them.

Then comes the heart of it. Amy tells what she saw from that same window on the day of the burial: her husband digging the grave, the gravel leaping and landing lightly and rolling back down the mound. She did not recognize him. Worse, when he came in from the grave with the fresh earth still on his shoes, he talked about everyday things. He said that three foggy mornings and one rainy day will rot the best birch fence a man can build. To her, a man who could say that at such a moment cannot have loved the child.

The reader should notice what the husband was probably doing. Digging is what a man does when he cannot do anything else, and talk about fences may be how he kept himself upright. Frost never tells us this. He lets the husband's comment about fences stand as evidence for the prosecution and leaves us to wonder whether it is also a defense. His wife is not wrong that something is missing between them, but her reading of him is a verdict reached from the top of a staircase, at a distance.

The ending refuses to settle anything. She is opening the door, he threatens to bring her back by force, and the poem stops. The title is worth a second look. A "home burial" is literally a burial at home, in the family plot. It is also a burial of the home itself, the marriage laid in the ground beside the child.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Home Burial is one of the finest things Frost ever wrote, and the reasons are mostly craft. The first is the use of speech. The poem is written in blank verse, which is unrhymed lines of five beats, the old line of Shakespeare and Milton. But Frost makes it sound like two people talking. Sentences break in the middle of lines and pick up again in the next, interruptions land exactly where interruptions would, and the rhythm of the line is stretched against the rhythm of a voice. This is what Frost himself called the sound of sense, the idea that you can hear the meaning of a sentence even through a closed door. Think of the line "Don't, don't, don't, don't," where the repetition does the work of a whole speech.

The second great strength is staging. The geography of the staircase carries the argument. He is below, then rising; she is above, cornered, then sliding down; then both are near the door. Every move shifts the power between them. Even small objects matter, from the latch her fingers move without lifting, to the hat she says she does not need, to the spade he stood against the wall. Frost gives us a drama with no narrator's comment on who is right, and the fairness of that is what makes it hurt.

Now the weaknesses, and there are a few. The husband's speech about men and "womenfolk" can sound like a dated stereotype, though Frost may be pointing at the husband's limits rather than endorsing them. The poem does lean toward the husband in one respect. His wife's long accusation is made in a charged, almost hysterical register, and a reader can feel that Frost the male author has given the better lines of sympathy to the man who "can't say" what he feels. Others feel the opposite, that Amy is given the poem's single most powerful passage, the gravel leaping in the air, and that the man never answers it.

There is also something a little stagey about the last third. The husband's closing threat of force, followed by the interjection about someone coming down the road, can feel melodramatic, and the exit of the poem is abrupt. A reader may wish for one more speech or one more silence. And at about a hundred and twenty lines the poem asks for patience; the ear gets tired of the quarrel in the way you get tired of overhearing a real one.

Yet these flaws are small against the achievement. Few poems manage to make two people both wrong and both sympathetic without a trace of a lecture. The cruelty of the ending is that it feels true: grief rarely resolves, and a quarrel in this state is a loop, not a plot.

III. The Impact It Had

Home Burial came out in North of Boston in 1914, and that book changed Frost's life. He had spent most of his thirties and early forties writing poems that American magazines mostly would not take. In 1912 he moved his family to England, where he found a publisher and met poets like Edward Thomas and Ezra Pound. When North of Boston appeared, reviewers on both sides of the Atlantic noticed that it did something new: it made poems out of New England talk. Home Burial was the darkest and most dramatic example of that method, and it helped establish Frost as the poet of the rural and the ordinary who was also, underneath, a poet of terror and pain.

The poem also changed how American poets could handle domestic subjects. Before it, marriage and bereavement in poetry were often idealized or kept at arm's length by lofty language. Frost showed that the flat, halting speech of a farmhouse could carry as much feeling as any elegy. You can follow that line forward to the poets of the mid-twentieth century who wrote about marriages and family wreckage in plain voices, among them Robert Lowell and, later, poets of the confessional movement who made private pain public. Frost was no confessional poet, since he disguised his own story behind invented characters, but he opened the door they would walk through.

Much of what we know about the origin of the poem comes from his biographers. The death of his three-year-old son Elliott in 1900 was a wound that never closed, and Elinor reportedly struggled for years with depression and with her sense that her husband had not grieved as she did. Frost also knew of another family that had come apart after a child's death. Whatever exact proportion of these ingredients went into the poem, it is hard not to read it as an act of conscience, a poet putting himself on trial in the person of the clumsy husband.

In the classroom, Home Burial is a staple of poetry courses, often taught alongside Frost's other dramatic poems like The Death of the Hired Man. Teachers use it to show how dialogue can function as poetry and how a reader can draw conclusions from small physical details. It is also one of the few Frost poems widely staged by actors, and it is ideal for a reading aloud, since its power depends on timing and tone. The Nobel-winning poet Joseph Brodsky gave it a long and passionate analysis in an essay, treating it as a study of grief and of the failure of reason to console, which shows how seriously other poets take it.

Its lasting lesson is that some of the strongest work comes from the subjects a writer would rather leave alone.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved Home Burial first for its honesty. Anyone who has lost someone, or lived with someone who has, recognizes the pattern the poem lays out: one person wants to talk and the other cannot, one grieves openly and the other in work or silence, and each takes the other's way as a judgment. The poem does not offer a solution, and that refusal is exactly what makes people trust it. It does not flatter either side.

They have also loved the sheer theater of it. Because the poem is almost entirely dialogue, it works like a short play, and audiences respond to it the way they respond to a good scene in a film. You can picture the stairway, the open door, the man with his chin between his fists. Readers often remember the image of the gravel leaping from the spade. It is vivid and terrible, because it fixes in a single picture how Amy saw her husband at that moment: a stranger, rhythmically busy, flinging earth over her child. The detail of the spade left standing in the entry, and the stains on his shoes, are the kind of precise, ordinary things that make an imagined scene feel true.

Fellow poets have admired the technical mastery. The way Frost ran ordinary talk through a traditional line struck many as a feat. The poem shows that blank verse could belong to a farmhouse, and that sentences broken in odd places could carry feeling more directly than any elevated diction. Writers and critics who care about the sound of English have returned to it as a model of how rhythm can express character. The husband's careful, rather stiff syntax and the wife's rapid, cutting phrases are distinct voices, though they share the same meter.

Readers also admire how much the poem trusts them. Frost never tells us whose side to take. He does not tell us that the digging was grief, or that the remark about the birch fence was an attempt at ordinary life. He sets out the evidence and steps back. This leaves room for different readers to take different views, and it is not unusual for a class to split almost evenly, some defending the husband and some the wife. That reaction is probably what Frost wanted.

Finally, many readers love the poem because it is not sentimental about love. The husband's paradox, that two who do not love cannot live together without rules and two who do cannot live together with them, is one of those lines that sticks in the mind long after the plot fades. It is bitter, funny, and strangely wise all at once, and it has been quoted by people thinking about their own marriages ever since.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Reception of Home Burial was mostly admiring from the start, but it has drawn real objections over the years, and the most persistent concern its sympathies. Some readers, especially in later decades, have argued that the poem is unfair to Amy. They see a woman presented as fragile, accusing, and close to hysterical, while the man is given the voice of patient reason. His line about having to "partly give up being a man with womenfolk" is read as the poem's own attitude by some, and as a dig at it by others. The disagreement is not settled, and it is part of how the poem is now taught.

A mirror-image objection has also been made: that the husband is the real victim of the poem's cruelty, shown as emotionally inarticulate and then threatened with abandonment. Readers who feel this way say Frost has stacked the case against a man who is, after all, trying to talk. The fact that critics can build opposite cases from the same text suggests either great balance or a certain slipperiness, and different critics read it differently.

Another line of criticism concerns the biography. Because the poem so clearly draws on the Frosts' loss of Elliott, some commentators have been uncomfortable with how it might use a real wife's grief as material. Biographers describe Elinor as unhappy about the poem's publication, though the details of what she said come to us through secondhand accounts and should be treated carefully. The question it raises is an old one in literature: does an artist have the right to make art from the people closest to him, and does the quality of the result excuse the exposure?

On the craft side, a few critics have found the poem overlong or a bit overheated, especially in the final stretch where the quarrel escalates into threats. The line "I'll follow and bring you back by force" can read as a theatrical flourish rather than a believable ending. Others have said that the poem's sustained drama sits uneasily with Frost's reputation as a quiet poet, and that it shows an element of the melodramatic in him that he usually kept under control.

Finally, there is the more general complaint that Frost's rural world is too bleak or too tidy. In a book that was often praised for its realism, some contemporaries thought that Frost leaned toward harshness for effect. Later critics have reversed this, praising that harshness as the antidote to the sweet pastoral that people expected from him. In short, the main pushback is not that the poem fails but that it is hard to be sure whom it blames, and that uncertainty has made some readers uneasy and others deeply loyal.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Home Burial sits at the meeting point of two traditions. The first is the dramatic monologue and dialogue poem, which runs from Browning, who wrote long poems in the voices of characters who reveal more than they know, through to Frost's own contemporaries. The second is the elegy, the poem of mourning that goes back to antiquity. Frost's trick is to fuse them. Instead of the poet lamenting in a lofty voice, we have two people in a house, and the elegy happens inside an argument. The dead child is hardly mentioned directly. He is the silent third party in the room.

It also belongs to the movement we call modernism, though Frost is a peculiar modernist. Poets like Eliot and Pound, writing around the same time, broke forms and used difficult allusion. Frost kept the old meters and the plain speech of ordinary people, but he shared the modern conviction that the world is not guaranteed to make sense and that consolation is not owed to us. You can feel the same bleakness in his better-known short poems, but here it has room to breathe. The tone also links to the realistic drama of the period, such as the plays of Ibsen and Chekhov, where the real action is what people cannot say to each other.

Looking backward, the poem descends from Wordsworth's rural tragedies like Michael, in which a country family's loss is told in a plain style, and from Thomas Hardy, whose poems of failed love and marital estrangement Frost admired. Looking forward, its children are many. The poetry of marriage and its breakdown in the later twentieth century, from Lowell to Sharon Olds to Louise Glück, owes something to the model of speech and subject that Frost proved could work. Short fiction by writers like Raymond Carver, in which couples talk past each other in kitchens, shares the same sensibility, even if it is hard to prove a direct line.

A fair comparison is with Frost's own The Death of the Hired Man, from the same book, which also stages a quarrel between husband and wife, but resolves it gently. Home Burial is the harder, darker sibling. Its ending is less like a scene's ending than like a door left open on a house on fire.

Why read it today? Because it is one of the clearest pictures in English of how grief can isolate people who share it. The details are of an old farmhouse, but the situation is entirely current. We still have our versions of the staircase, the fence, the unspoken rule about which subjects are off limits. The poem does not fix any of it. What it gives is recognition, and perhaps a little tenderness toward the husband and wife who, in a poem more than a century old, are still standing on opposite sides of a door.
