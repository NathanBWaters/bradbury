Bradbury 1000 • Night 46 • Nightly Poem

Home Burial

Robert Frost (1914) • 7 min read

On a farmhouse staircase overlooking their firstborn child’s grave, a grieving mother and father confront the tragic chasm between two ways of mourning.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in 1912–1913 and published in North of Boston (1914), 'Home Burial' was born from the deepest tragedy of Robert and Elinor Frost’s marriage—the death of their three-year-old firstborn son Elliott from cholera infantum in July 1900—as well as the marital estrangement of Elinor’s sister Leona and Nathaniel Harvey after losing their child. Belonging to the American Pastoral Modernism tradition and categorized under Pastoral & Elegy, 'Home Burial' (1914) unfolds across 120 verbatim lines in 19 stanzas. Nobel Laureate Joseph Brodsky devoted an entire seminal essay to 'Home Burial,' calling it one of the greatest tragic dialogues since Euripides and Shakespeare for its refusal to villainize either the inconsolable wife or the physically grieving husband.

He saw her from the bottom of the stairs

Before she saw him. She was starting down,

Looking back over her shoulder at some fear.

She took a doubtful step and then undid it

To raise herself and look again. He spoke

Advancing toward her: “What is it you see

From up there always—for I want to know.”

She turned and sank upon her skirts at that,

And her face changed from terrified to dull.

He said to gain time: “What is it you see,”

Mounting until she cowered under him.

“I will find out now—you must tell me, dear.”

She, in her place, refused him any help

With the least stiffening of her neck and silence.

She let him look, sure that he wouldn’t see,

Blind creature; and a while he didn’t see.

But at last he murmured, “Oh,” and again, “Oh.”

“What is it—what?” she said.

“Just that I see.”

“You don’t,” she challenged. “Tell me what it is.”

“The wonder is I didn’t see at once.

I never noticed it from here before.

I must be wonted to it—that’s the reason.

The little graveyard where my people are!

So small the window frames the whole of it.

Not so much larger than a bedroom, is it?

There are three stones of slate and one of marble,

Broad-shouldered little slabs there in the sunlight

On the sidehill. We haven’t to mind those.

But I understand: it is not the stones,

But the child’s mound—”

“Don’t, don’t, don’t, don’t,” she cried.

She withdrew shrinking from beneath his arm

That rested on the banister, and slid downstairs;

And turned on him with such a daunting look,

He said twice over before he knew himself:

“Can’t a man speak of his own child he’s lost?”

“Not you! Oh, where’s my hat? Oh, I don’t need it!

I must get out of here. I must get air.

I don’t know rightly whether any man can.”

“Amy! Don’t go to someone else this time.

Listen to me. I won’t come down the stairs.”

He sat and fixed his chin between his fists.

“There’s something I should like to ask you, dear.”

“You don’t know how to ask it.”

“Help me, then.”

Her fingers moved the latch for all reply.

“My words are nearly always an offence.

I don’t know how to speak of anything

So as to please you. But I might be taught

I should suppose. I can’t say I see how.

A man must partly give up being a man

With womenfolk. We could have some arrangement

By which I’d bind myself to keep hands off

Anything special you’re a-mind to name.

Though I don’t like such things ’twixt those that love.

Two that don’t love can’t live together without them.

But two that do can’t live together with them.”

She moved the latch a little. “Don’t—don’t go.

Don’t carry it to someone else this time.

Tell me about it if it’s something human.

Let me into your grief. I’m not so much

Unlike other folks as your standing there

Apart would make me out. Give me my chance.

I do think, though, you overdo it a little.

What was it brought you up to think it the thing

To take your mother-loss of a first child

So inconsolably—in the face of love.

You’d think his memory might be satisfied—”

“There you go sneering now!”

“I’m not, I’m not!

You make me angry. I’ll come down to you.

God, what a woman! And it’s come to this,

A man can’t speak of his own child that’s dead.”

“You can’t because you don’t know how.

If you had any feelings, you that dug

With your own hand—how could you?—his little grave;

I saw you from that very window there,

Making the gravel leap and leap in air,

Leap up, like that, like that, and land so lightly

And roll back down the mound beside the hole.

I thought, Who is that man? I didn’t know you.

And I crept down the stairs and up the stairs

To look again, and still your spade kept lifting.

Then you came in. I heard your rumbling voice

Out in the kitchen, and I don’t know why,

But I went near to see with my own eyes.

You could sit there with the stains on your shoes

Of the fresh earth from your own baby’s grave

And talk about your everyday concerns.

You had stood the spade up against the wall

Outside there in the entry, for I saw it.”

“I shall laugh the worst laugh I ever laughed.

I’m cursed. God, if I don’t believe I’m cursed.”

“I can repeat the very words you were saying.

‘Three foggy mornings and one rainy day

Will rot the best birch fence a man can build.’

Think of it, talk like that at such a time!

What had how long it takes a birch to rot

To do with what was in the darkened parlour.

You couldn’t care! The nearest friends can go

With anyone to death, comes so far short

They might as well not try to go at all.

No, from the time when one is sick to death,

One is alone, and he dies more alone.

Friends make pretence of following to the grave,

But before one is in it, their minds are turned

And making the best of their way back to life

And living people, and things they understand.

But the world’s evil. I won’t have grief so

If I can change it. Oh, I won’t, I won’t!”

“There, you have said it all and you feel better.

You won’t go now. You’re crying. Close the door.

The heart’s gone out of it: why keep it up.

Amy! There’s someone coming down the road!”

“You—oh, you think the talk is all. I must go—

Somewhere out of this house. How can I make you—”

“If—you—do!” She was opening the door wider.

“Where do you mean to go? First tell me that.

I’ll follow and bring you back by force. I will!—”

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in 1912–1913 and published in North of Boston (1914), 'Home Burial' was born from the deepest tragedy of Robert and Elinor Frost’s marriage—the death of their three-year-old firstborn son Elliott from cholera infantum in July 1900—as well as the marital estrangement of Elinor’s sister Leona and Nathaniel Harvey after losing their child. Within the broader trajectory of American Pastoral Modernism, Robert Frost composed 'Home Burial' (1914) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"He saw her from the bottom of the stairs"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Robert Frost grounded these 120 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Home Burial' lies the incommunicability of grief and the tragic collision between two ways of coping with loss: the mother (Amy), who cannot bear how easily the world returns to everyday affairs, is horrified that her husband dug their baby’s grave with his own hands and then spoke outside the kitchen of how 'Three foggy mornings and one rainy day / Will rot the best birch fence a man can build'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Tell me about it if it’s something human."—Robert Frost develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Robert Frost constructs 'Home Burial' using a 120-line dramatic dialogue in blank verse where stage-like physical blocking on the staircase (she above looking out the small window at the family graveyard, he climbing from below) and jagged, shared pentameter lines ('“What is it—what?” she said. / “Just that I see.”') turn iambic meter into raw emotional theatre. Spanning 120 lines across 19 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Robert Frost balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "He saw her from the bottom of the stairs" and "I’ll follow and bring you back by force. I will!—”" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Nobel Laureate Joseph Brodsky devoted an entire seminal essay to 'Home Burial,' calling it one of the greatest tragic dialogues since Euripides and Shakespeare for its refusal to villainize either the inconsolable wife or the physically grieving husband. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Home Burial' has remained a touchstone of the Pastoral & Elegy canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Robert Frost’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
