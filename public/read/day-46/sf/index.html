1. Cadastral Surveying vs. Topography: Why 'Paper Streets' Became Stairways

Why did nineteenth-century San Francisco surveyors refuse to curve streets gently around hills the way European hillside towns or Frederick Law Olmsted's suburban designs did? In 1847, Jasper O'Farrell was hired by real-estate speculators who wanted uniform rectangular fifty-vara and hundred-vara lots that could be bought and sold unseen on a flat paper map. Where those straight street rights-of-way intersected thirty-to-forty-degree Franciscan rock slopes, the city could never grade a roadway for vehicles—leaving over 700 permanent public pedestrian corridors. 2. Ceramic & Mosaic Engineering of the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps

How do 75,000 ceramic, stained-glass, and mirror fragments survive twenty years of outdoor coastal weather on a public concrete staircase? Artists Aileen Barr and Colette Crutcher fired the custom relief tiles in high-fire stoneware clay so water could not penetrate the clay body, bonded the mosaic panels to the vertical concrete risers using high-flexibility polymer-modified exterior thin-set mortar, and confined the glazed artwork strictly to the vertical risers so foot traffic treads only on the non-slip horizontal concrete steps.
