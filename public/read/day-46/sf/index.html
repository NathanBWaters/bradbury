San Francisco's 700 Public Stairways: The 16th Avenue Tiled Steps and the Car-Free Vulcan Stairway

San Francisco — Why mapping an unbending 1840s grid over forty-three steep hills created a secret vertical pedestrian city—and how 220 neighbors turned 163 gray steps into a river of 75,000 ceramic tiles (19th-Century Paper-Street Platting to 2005 Community Mosaic Renaissance) • 8 min read

“Where the 1847 street grid met a cliff too steep for horses or automobiles, San Francisco built a stairway—and inside those stairways lives a secret garden city without a single car.” — Adah Bakalinsky, Stairway Walks in San Francisco (1984)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Because nineteenth-century surveyors Jasper O'Farrell and William Eddy laid a rigid right-angle checkerboard street grid straight across forty-three steep hills without regard to topography, San Francisco possesses more than 700 public stairways—vertical pedestrian rights-of-way officially maintained by the Department of Public Works where 'paper streets' were too precipitous for horses or automobiles. Two landmarks capture the magic of this vertical commons: the car-free Vulcan Stairway on the eastern flank of Mount Olympus (where cottages built after 1906 have no driveways and receive mail along a cascading botanical garden path), and the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps on Moraga Street in the Golden Gate Heights neighborhood, where ceramicists Aileen Barr and Colette Crutcher led 220 neighbors in 2003–2005 to transform 163 concrete risers into a breathtaking sea-to-stars mosaic of 75,000 handmade tiles and stained glass.

Why does San Francisco have more than 700 public stairways—totaling miles of vertical wooden and concrete steps tucked between houses from Telegraph Hill and Russian Hill to Corona Heights, Potrero Hill, and the Sunset? The answer begins with an 1840s cartographic obsession: the rectilinear grid. When surveyor Jean-Jacques Vioget (1839) and Irish civil engineer Jasper O'Farrell (1847) —followed by the Outside Lands surveyors of the 1860s—platted the streets of San Francisco to sell rectangular real-estate lots as fast as possible, they refused to curve streets around the contours of the city's forty-three hills, instead drawing straight lines straight up and over thirty-five-percent cliffs.

On a flat map in City Hall, every one of those streets looked like a normal sixty-foot-wide thoroughfare; out in the real world of Franciscan chert and sand dunes, however, dozens of blocks were 'paper streets'—vertical grades so steep that no horse wagon, cable car, or automobile could ever climb them without tumbling backward. Still, because those 'paper streets' remained legal public rights-of-way owned by the City and County of San Francisco, neighborhood residents demanded a way to walk down to the streetcar lines and back up to their homes—prompting carpenters in the 1870s–1900s and Works Progress Administration (WPA) concrete crews in the 1930s to build hundreds of public stairways down the center of the unpassable street rights-of-way.

Walk into the Vulcan Stairway (running four blocks along the eastern slope of Corona Heights and Ashbury Heights between Ord and Levant streets) or nearby Saturn Street Steps, and you step into an urban world that surprises even lifelong Californians: rows of charming 1906–1915 cottages and apartments whose official street address is a stairway without a single automobile or curb. Along the Vulcan Stairway—originally an 1880s dirt lane called Vulcan Lane before neighbors planted loquat trees, nasturtiums, tree ferns, and calla lilies around its concrete landings—the letter carrier walks mail from porch to porch on foot, moving vans must carry sofas up thirty steps by hand, and the only sound at noon is hummingbirds and wind chimes.

In 1984, hiker and urban historian Adah Bakalinsky published the first edition of her classic guide Stairway Walks in San Francisco, cataloging hundreds of these hidden vertical corridors and inspiring neighborhood volunteers across the city to adopt neglected weeds-choked weed lots around their local steps. Five miles west of downtown, on the steep western flank of Golden Gate Heights (a dramatic ridge of red radiolarian chert and ancient windblown sand dunes rising above the Sunset District toward Grandview Park), a stark 163-step concrete stairway climbed straight up the Moraga Street right-of-way between 16th and 15th avenues.

In 2003, Golden Gate Heights residents Jessie Audette and Alice Yee Xavier looked at the gray, graffiti-tagged concrete risers of the Moraga Street stairway—and, inspired by Brazilian artist Jorge Selarón's famous tiled steps (Escadaria Selarón) in Rio de Janeiro—asked a bold neighborhood question: what if we covered all 163 risers in a continuous handmade ceramic mosaic? To design and fabricate a mosaic capable of withstanding decades of damp Pacific salt air, winter rainstorms, and thousands of daily footsteps without cracking or delaminating, the neighbors recruited two master Bay Area mosaic and ceramic artists living right in the Sunset: Irish-born ceramicist Aileen Barr and mosaic sculptor Colette Crutcher.

Over the next two years, Barr and Crutcher designed a sweeping vertical narrative rising up the 163 risers from the bottom of the hill at 16th Avenue to the crest near 15th Avenue: beginning at the bottom in the deep blue waters of the Pacific Ocean teeming with tiled sea stars, octopuses, and fish, flowing upward through coastal hills, streams, birds, and frogs, and culminating at the top steps in a swirling night sky of silver mirror-glass stars and a radiant golden sun. Instead of ordering industrial factory tiles, Barr and Crutcher turned the project into a neighborhood barn-raising: more than 300 Sunset District neighbors sponsored handmade animal, fish, shell, and star tiles bearing their families' names, and 220 community volunteers spent hundreds of hours cutting glass, glazing stoneware clay in Barr's studio, and kneeling on the concrete steps to mortar 2,000 handmade tiles and 75,000 individual ceramic and mirror fragments into place.

Look closely at the engineering behind the beauty on Moraga Street: the tiles are mounted exclusively on the vertical risers rather than the horizontal treads—both so walkers never slip on wet glaze and so you only see the full, seamless 163-step tapestry when you stand at the bottom of the hill looking up. When the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps were officially unveiled on August 27, 2005, the transformation was electric: a forgotten gray municipal staircase instantly became one of the most beloved landmarks in San Francisco—and proved so inspiring that neighbors subsequently teamed up with Barr and Crutcher to create the 212-step Hidden Garden Steps one block away on 16th Avenue between Kirkham and Lawton streets in 2013, and the Lincoln Park Steps in the Richmond.

Climb all 163 risers of the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps today, pausing at the succulent gardens tended by neighborhood volunteers along the flanks, and then continue one block further up the Grandview Park ('Turtle Hill') wooden stairs to the 666-foot chert summit. Standing atop Grandview Park looking west over the checkerboard roof grid of the Sunset District all the way to the breaking white surf of Ocean Beach while the 75,000 ceramic tiles shimmer below your feet, you see how San Francisco's steepest obstacles always become its greatest works of art.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Cadastral Surveying vs. Topography: Why 'Paper Streets' Became Stairways

Why did nineteenth-century San Francisco surveyors refuse to curve streets gently around hills the way European hillside towns or Frederick Law Olmsted's suburban designs did? In 1847, Jasper O'Farrell was hired by real-estate speculators who wanted uniform rectangular fifty-vara and hundred-vara lots that could be bought and sold unseen on a flat paper map. Where those straight street rights-of-way intersected thirty-to-forty-degree Franciscan rock slopes, the city could never grade a roadway for vehicles—leaving over 700 permanent public pedestrian corridors.

2. Ceramic & Mosaic Engineering of the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps

How do 75,000 ceramic, stained-glass, and mirror fragments survive twenty years of outdoor coastal weather on a public concrete staircase? Artists Aileen Barr and Colette Crutcher fired the custom relief tiles in high-fire stoneware clay so water could not penetrate the clay body, bonded the mosaic panels to the vertical concrete risers using high-flexibility polymer-modified exterior thin-set mortar, and confined the glazed artwork strictly to the vertical risers so foot traffic treads only on the non-slip horizontal concrete steps.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three vertical landmarks in mind: first, the 16th Avenue Tiled Steps (163 risers on Moraga Street, completed August 2005 by Aileen Barr, Colette Crutcher, and 220 neighbors); second, the Hidden Garden Steps (16th Avenue between Kirkham and Lawton, completed 2013); and third, the car-free Vulcan Stairway and Filbert/Greenwich Steps, where cottages open directly onto pedestrian gardens.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take the N-Judah Muni Metro or the 28-19th Avenue bus and walk to the foot of Moraga Street at 16th Avenue. Stand at the bottom of the 163 steps first so the vertical risers align into a single unified mosaic from ocean to stars; then climb the steps to 15th Avenue and ascend the wooden stairway into Grandview Park for a 360-degree view from Golden Gate Park to the Pacific Ocean.
