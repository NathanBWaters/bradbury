Sonnet 29: When, in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes

William Shakespeare (1609) • 3 min read

From bitter social outcast status and professional envy, a single thought of the beloved lifts the speaker’s spirit like a lark at break of day.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Sonnet 29 is one of Shakespeare's most human poems, because it is about a feeling almost everyone has had: looking at your own life and deciding that everyone else is doing better. It belongs to the collection of 154 sonnets published in 1609, though it was probably written in the 1590s, during Shakespeare's early career as an actor and playwright in London. That was also a period when theaters were repeatedly shut because of plague, and a writer's reputation and income could feel fragile. The poem is addressed to a young man who is the subject of the first part of the sequence, but the situation it describes is general and easy to recognize. The speaker begins in self-pity, cursing his luck and envying others for their looks, friends, and talents. Then, in the middle of that spiral, a single thought of the person he loves lifts him up, and the poem soars. Its meaning is simple and generous: love can restore a person's worth when everything else seems to have taken it away.

When, in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes,

I all alone beweep my outcast state

And trouble deaf heaven with my bootless cries

And look upon myself and curse my fate,

Wishing me like to one more rich in hope,

Featured like him, like him with friends possess’d,

Desiring this man’s art and that man’s scope,

With what I most enjoy contented least;

Yet in these thoughts myself almost despising,

Haply I think on thee, and then my state,

Like to the lark at break of day arising

From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven’s gate;

For thy sweet love remember’d such wealth brings

That then I scorn to change my state with kings.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is built as a single long sentence that falls and then rises, and understanding that shape is the key to reading it. The first eight lines are the fall. "When, in disgrace with fortune and men's eyes, / I all alone beweep my outcast state." The word disgrace here has a double edge. It means out of favor with luck, which the poem calls fortune, and out of favor with other people's opinion, their "eyes." The speaker feels both unlucky and looked down on. He weeps, alone, over his "outcast state."

Then he prays, but the prayer goes nowhere. He "troubles deaf heaven with my bootless cries." Bootless is an old word meaning useless, with no profit in it. So heaven is deaf and his cries are pointless. He looks at himself and curses his fate. The next lines make the cause of the misery clear, and it is envy. He wishes he were "like to one more rich in hope," a man good-looking ("featured like him"), a man with friends all around him. He desires "this man's art and that man's scope," meaning one person's skill and another's range of opportunities. And the final line of that stretch is the saddest: "With what I most enjoy contented least." The things he used to love, presumably his own work, no longer satisfy him. Envy has soured even his gifts.

At line nine the poem turns on the single word "Yet." Here is the moment a thoughtful reader should linger on. The speaker is "almost despising" himself, and then "haply," meaning by chance or luckily, he thinks of the beloved. The change is not argued or reasoned. It simply happens. The beloved is remembered, and everything changes.

Now come the poem's most famous images. "Then my state, / Like to the lark at break of day arising / From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven's gate." The speaker's mood is compared to a lark, a small bird that rises at dawn from the dark ground and sings as it climbs. The earth is "sullen," the heaven is now not deaf but a gate where hymns are sung. The same sky that ignored his cries now receives his song. That is a quiet reversal worth noticing.

The final couplet states the result: the memory of this person's love "such wealth brings / That then I scorn to change my state with kings." Notice the financial language, wealth and change, as in exchange. The poem began by envying others who were richer in hope, and ends by refusing to trade places even with a king. The true subject is a reordering of values. What he lacked, he now realizes, is not what he needed, because being loved outweighs everything he was envying.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The first thing to admire is the poem's structure, which acts out its meaning. The sonnet is fourteen lines, and the first eight are a closely packed descent through self-pity. The sentence is long and keeps adding clauses, like a person who cannot stop complaining. Then the sentence changes direction at "Yet," and the last six lines rush upward. The reader experiences the lift in the body of the poem, because the syntax itself seems to rise. This kind of match between form and feeling is Shakespeare at his best.

The sounds also do real work. The opening lines are thick with long vowels and the heavy sounds of "disgrace," "beweep," "outcast," and "bootless." The sequence of "d" and "b" sounds in "deaf" and "bootless" feels like a drumming of fists. Then the lark passage lightens everything. "Like to the lark at break of day arising" lifts, in part because the words are lighter and the rhythm moves faster. The comparison is one of Shakespeare's most celebrated, partly because it needs no explanation. You immediately feel what the lark is doing, rising from the dirt into song.

The poem is also smart about psychology. It does not pretend that envy is noble. The speaker admits to wishing he were someone else, to looking at others and finding himself lacking, and to being unable to enjoy even his own strengths. That honesty makes the redemption believable. A poem that moved from happiness to more happiness would be dull. This one begins in an ugly, recognizable place.

On the other hand, there are weaknesses. The rescue is perhaps too sudden and too easy. The speaker has been in the pit for eight lines and is lifted out by a single thought, with no struggle. A skeptical reader might feel that the poem rigs the outcome, and that real despair does not lift on command. The last line, scorning to trade places with kings, is also a little conventional. The idea that love is worth more than royal wealth was a familiar one, and the final couplet, while graceful, leaves a feeling of neatness that the opening quatrains did not promise.

There is another matter, which is that the beloved is a blank. As in many of the sonnets, we learn nothing about this person. They are a name for a feeling, a kind of light switch. The poem's emotional power belongs to the speaker's despair and recovery, not to a real relationship. It is possible to find that a little cold, and to wish the poem had given the beloved a face.

Even so, these are small complaints. The poem is a model of how to compress an entire emotional cycle, from misery to joy, into fourteen lines. Few poems capture the sensation of mood changing so cleanly, and few move so precisely at the right moment.

III. The Impact It Had

Sonnet 29 has had an impact that is quieter than the fame of Sonnet 18, but it is deeply rooted in the way English speakers describe low moods and recoveries. Phrases from it have become part of the language. The line "With what I most enjoy contented least" is often quoted to describe a kind of dissatisfaction that spoils even our pleasures. And the phrase "Like to the lark at break of day arising" has become a kind of shorthand for sudden spiritual uplift. The image of the lark singing at heaven's gate has been borrowed in many later poems and songs.

The poem has attracted musical settings in many styles. Composers have set it for choir, solo voice, and contemporary song, drawn by its clear emotional arc, which moves naturally from low to high. It is a favorite for readings at memorial services and for gatherings where people want to speak of what sustains them in hard times, because it takes the sting out of failure without pretending failure does not hurt.

Among writers, its psychological honesty has been influential. The poem offers one of the earliest and clearest depictions in English of what we would now call a depressive spiral followed by relief, and it does so without any medical or religious vocabulary. Later poets and novelists writing about envy and self-doubt often echo its movement. The idea that the cure for envy is a different kind of attachment, rather than success, is a theme that runs through much later literature.

In education, it is a standard example of the volta in the Shakespearean sonnet, the turn in thought, because the word "Yet" makes the turn so explicit. Teachers often pair it with Sonnet 30, since the two poems look alike in their structure and both move from grief to consolation through the thought of the beloved. Together they are often used to show how Shakespeare handles emotion with a sort of rhetorical architecture.

It has also been important for readers interested in Shakespeare's own life. Because the speaker complains of being in disgrace and envies others' "art" and "scope," many people have read it as autobiography, a glimpse of the actor-playwright in a low moment. Scholars tend to be cautious about that, since the sonnets are crafted performances, not diary entries. But the temptation says something about the poem's effect. It feels so personal that readers want it to be true, and that feeling of intimacy has helped keep it alive for more than four hundred years. Its influence is less about changing literature and more about changing how people talk to themselves when they feel they are failing, which is a kind of influence that is hard to measure but very real.

IV. What Readers Loved

The great strength of this poem, for ordinary readers, is its recognizability. Most of us have had the experience of comparing ourselves unfavorably with others, and the poem names that feeling exactly. "Desiring this man's art and that man's scope" captures the particular envy of wanting a bit of everyone else's life, their talent here, their opportunity there, without wanting the whole of any one person's. Readers across centuries have felt seen by that line.

Readers also love the honesty about the speaker's state. He does not present himself as noble in suffering. He is whining, he knows it, and he is "almost despising" himself for it. A poem that admits the smallness of its own misery is more trustworthy, and people tend to be moved by it more than by a more dignified complaint. It gives readers permission to feel petty without feeling alone.

The lark is, understandably, a favorite image. It is simple and vivid, and it carries a double meaning, since the bird is rising literally and the speaker's spirit is rising figuratively. Readers who know the sound of larks in the countryside feel the image physically, and even those who have never heard one understand the idea of a song that climbs. Many people recall the line "sings hymns at heaven's gate" long after forgetting the rest of the poem.

Another reason for its popularity is the sense of consolation. In a world where many poems about love are about longing or loss, this one is about love as a kind of rescue. The idea that thinking of someone who loves you can lift you from despair rings true to anyone who has gone through a hard time and been helped by a friend, a spouse, or a child. The poem offers a model of gratitude that does not feel sentimental, because it is earned by so much preceding gloom.

Finally, many readers admire the way the poem scorns status. In a society that has always cared about wealth, rank, and reputation, the closing claim that the speaker would not trade places with a king is bracing. It is not a rejection of ambition so much as a reminder that private happiness is a separate currency. People who feel overlooked by the world find real comfort in that idea, which is why the poem is so often chosen for reading aloud in difficult moments and why it has stayed in print and in memory without effort.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a poem this admired has attracted reservations. The most common is the objection that the resolution is too neat. Critics have noted that the poem spends eight lines on despair and then dissolves it with a single remembered thought. Real depression, they point out, does not lift because one thinks of a loved one. The poem's consolation can look like a quick fix, and some readers find the final couplet a little glib, an easy rhyming flourish that settles a problem the opening lines made seem serious.

A second objection involves the ambiguity of the beloved and the relationship. Like the other sonnets addressed to the young man, Sonnet 29 has been the subject of a long scholarly argument about who this person was and what the bond between them really means. Some critics have suggested that the poem's rhetoric of love is partly a rhetoric of patronage, the language an artist might use to a wealthy supporter on whom his fortunes depend. In that reading, "thy sweet love" might include material support, and the claim to scorn kings might be flattery as much as devotion. Others reject this and insist the poem is simply an expression of deep personal affection. The poem itself does not settle the question.

There is also some debate about the speaker's complaint. Because the poem is so general, readers can supply their own sorrows, but critics sometimes say that this vagueness is a weakness. We never learn what the disgrace is. Is it a social failure, a professional setback, a loss of reputation? The poem is more about the mood than the cause. For readers who prefer specific detail, that can make the first eight lines feel like a catalogue of stock complaints rather than a real situation.

A further criticism has concerned the poem's conventions. The notion of an "outcast" crying to a deaf heaven, the lark at heaven's gate, and the contrast between love and kingly wealth all have precedents in earlier poetry and in the Psalms and love lyrics that Shakespeare knew. Some scholars regard these as stock pieces expertly assembled, rather than discoveries. The lark itself had been a common poetic figure.

Last, the inward focus of the poem has drawn mild censure. The speaker is the star of the poem, and the beloved is a means to his recovery. The poem never asks what the beloved might need. In that light, it can seem self-absorbed, a poem about the speaker's emotions rather than about love as a two-way relationship. That is a fair point, although it is also true of almost every lyric poem written in the first person, and it does not prevent the poem from being moving.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Sonnet 29 belongs to the English Renaissance sonnet tradition, the same world as Sidney, Spenser, and Donne, but it is distinctive in subject. Most Elizabethan sonnet sequences concerned a cold or unreachable lady, and the poets complained of rejection. Shakespeare's sequence instead addresses a young man and is concerned with friendship, devotion, jealousy, and time. Sonnet 29 stands in the early part of that sequence, where the mood is mostly warm and the speaker is grateful.

Its roots in earlier poetry include the Psalms, with their cries from the depths to a heaven that seems silent, and the courtly tradition of lovers who find their worth in a lady's regard. The idea of love as spiritual rescue also owes something to Petrarch and to Christian devotion, though Shakespeare uses religious words, "heaven's gate," "hymns," in a human context. This habit of borrowing religious language for human love is a feature of much Renaissance poetry, and it continues in the metaphysical poets, such as John Donne, who came a generation later.

Poems that descend from it are many. Any poem that moves from despair to consolation through a sudden change of thought owes something to this shape. You can hear it in the Romantic odes, in Keats's moments of anguish that turn toward a vision, and in more recent poems about depression and recovery. Gerard Manley Hopkins's so-called terrible sonnets are darker cousins that refuse the easy lift. Contemporary poets continue to write sonnets about envy and self-doubt, and many are in conversation with this one, whether they know it or not.

The best comparison is probably with Sonnet 30, which has a similar structure but a different emotional register. Where Sonnet 29 is about envy and present failure, Sonnet 30 is about grief and the burden of remembering the past. Reading them together shows how Shakespeare can use the same architecture, a long complaint followed by a rescuing thought, to explore different parts of human unhappiness. Compared with the tightly argued Sonnet 18, they are more private and less showy.

Why still read it? Because few poems are as honest about an everyday, shameful emotion. We rarely speak about envy, and we rarely admit how much a bad mood can shape what we see. This poem does both, and then offers a believable, if brief, way out. It does not claim that love fixes everything. It says that sometimes a single thought of someone who matters can break the grip of self-contempt. For anyone who has had a bad night and a better morning, the lark in these lines is an old and trusted friend.
