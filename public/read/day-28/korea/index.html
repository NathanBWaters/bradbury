Hwangnyongsa's Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda (645 CE): The 80-Meter Colossus Built Without a Single Iron Nail

Korea — How Queen Seondeok of Silla hired a rival Baekje master carpenter to raise a 225-cheok wooden skyscraper anchored on a thirty-ton granite heart-stone (Silla Kingdom (553–645 CE to 1238 Mongol Invasion)) • 10 min read

“If Your Majesty builds a nine-story wooden pagoda at Hwangnyongsa, the nine neighboring nations will bow in tribute and Silla will enjoy lasting peace.” — Monk Jajang to Queen Seondeok (Samguk Yusa, 643 CE)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk across the open grassy plain of Guhwang-dong in central Gyeongju today, and you see an array of sixty-four massive granite pillar bases arranged around a colossal central foundation boulder measuring 4.2 meters across and weighing thirty metric tons. From 645 CE until Mongol troops torched it in 1238, those stones held up the Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda—soaring roughly eighty meters (the height of a modern twenty-six-story skyscraper) into the sky without a single structural iron nail. Commissioned by Korea's first female sovereign, Queen Seondeok of Silla, on the advice of the pilgrim monk Jajang and engineered by master carpenter Abiji of rival Baekje, it stood for 593 years as one of the tallest timber structures ever built by human hands.

Stand in the center of the vast, windswept meadow of Guhwang-dong in Gyeongju—just east of Bunhwangsa Temple and north of Wolseong Palace—and look down at the square terrace of compacted earth and granite beneath your shoes. Arranged in an eight-by-eight grid spanning 22.2 meters on each side sit sixty-four dressed granite column bases (chuseok). At the exact geometric intersection of the grid rests a single titanic block of rough granite measuring 4.2 by 3.0 meters and weighing over thirty metric tons, hollowed out in the center for a reliquary casket. This is the Simchuseok (Heart-Stone) of the Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda.

For nearly six centuries—from its completion in 645 CE until Mongol cavalry burned it to the ground in 1238—anyone standing on this exact spot had to crane their neck backward until their hat fell off just to see the golden spire (chalju) piercing the clouds. Recorded in both the Samguk Sagi and Monk Iryeon’s Samguk Yusa as measuring 225 Tang cheok in total height (183 cheok for the nine timber stories plus 42 cheok for the bronze finial mast), modern architectural historians calculate that the pagoda stood between 79 and 82 meters tall—equivalent to a modern twenty-six-story office building, built entirely of interlocking red pine timber without a single structural iron nail!

The story of Hwangnyongsa ('Temple of the Imperial Yellow Dragon') begins in the second lunar month of 553 CE, when King Jinheung of Silla ordered his architects to build a new royal palace beside Half-Moon Castle (Banwolseong). When workmen excavated the marshy ground, a miraculous yellow dragon appeared at the site. Taking the omen as a command from Heaven, King Jinheung immediately canceled the palace and converted the 28,000-square-meter precinct into Silla’s grand state Buddhist monastery, housing a colossal five-meter gilt-bronze triad of Shakyamuni Buddha cast in 574 CE. Ninety years later, in the 640s, the Kingdom of Silla faced an existential geopolitical crisis. Surrounded by its fierce Korean rivals—Baekje to the west and Goguryeo to the north—as well as Wa (Japan) across the strait and nomadic tribes beyond the frontier, Silla was ruled by its first female sovereign: Queen Seondeok (r. 632–647), the same brilliant monarch who built the 362-stone Cheomseongdae astronomical observatory.

In 643 CE, the high monk Jajang Yulsa returned from a seven-year pilgrimage to Mount Wutai in Tang China and requested an urgent audience with Queen Seondeok. Jajang reported that a divine spirit at Taihe Lake had revealed to him that the dragon guardian of Hwangnyongsa was his eldest son, and that if Silla constructed a nine-story wooden pagoda at Hwangnyongsa, the kingdom would symbolically subdue the Nine Neighboring Enemies (Gu-i)—one enemy nation assigned to each of the nine stories, from Japan on the first story and China on the second to Baekje, Goguryeo, and the Mohe on the upper tiers.

Queen Seondeok convened her royal council and approved the audacious project, appointing her cousin Prince Kim Yong-chun (father of future King Muyeol) to oversee construction with two hundred skilled Silla artisans. Yet there was a fatal engineering problem: seventh-century Silla stonecutters knew how to carve granite and brick (as at nearby Bunhwangsa in 634 CE), but nobody in Silla possessed the structural carpentry mastery required to raise an eighty-meter multi-story timber tower that would not buckle under its own weight or blow over in coastal typhoons. Only one kingdom on the Korean peninsula possessed that supreme timber-pagoda engineering: Silla’s bitter military rival Baekje, which had already built the monumental wooden pagoda of Mireuksa at Iksan under King Mu. Swallowing diplomatic pride for the sake of architectural perfection, Queen Seondeok sent chests of gold and silk across enemy lines to the Baekje court, inviting Baekje’s greatest master architect-carpenter (daemokjang), Abiji, to come to Gyeongju.

Wait—imagine the psychological torment of Master Abiji after arriving in the Silla capital of Seorabeol in 643 CE! He was an artist of Baekje hired to build a nine-story monument explicitly designed by Monk Jajang to pray for the subjugation of Baekje (assigned to the fourth story of the pagoda!). According to the Samguk Yusa, on the very night Abiji completed the engineering blueprints, he dreamed that his homeland of Baekje was collapsing into flames and ruins. Horrified that his own hands were building the talisman of his country’s doom, Abiji threw down his carpenter’s ink-line (meokjul) and refused to raise the central pillar (simju). Suddenly, the earth shook, darkness fell over the temple yard, and out of the golden sanctuary doors stepped an aged monk and a giant guardian warrior (Geumgang-yeoksa) who lifted the massive central pine column onto the thirty-ton Simchuseok heart-stone with effortless grace. Realizing that the work transcended human borders and belonged to the Dharma, Abiji picked up his adze and completed the nine stories in 645 CE.

How did Abiji keep an eighty-meter wooden skyscraper standing through six centuries of Korean typhoons and earthquakes? Modern structural excavations conducted by the National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage between 1976 and 1983 decoded his foundation engineering: Pantuk-gichobeop (rammed-earth and boulder raft foundation). Before laying a single stone, Abiji’s crew excavated the marshy ground several meters deep and built an underground pyramid of alternating layers of pure clay, quartz sand, and river boulders pounded rock-hard with wooden rammers. That subterranean raft acted as a seismic shock absorber. Above it, the central timber mast (simju) rose continuously through the core of the pagoda like a modern skyscraper’s tuned mass damper, while the sixty-four outer columns stepped inward story by story using deep cantilevered bracket clusters (gongpo) that converted horizontal typhoon wind gusts into harmless elastic sway!

Even when violent lightning strikes set the upper stories ablaze in 698, 718, 868, and 953 CE, Goryeo and Silla kings immediately rebuilt the tower—and each time they opened the thirty-ton Simchuseok heart-stone to re-enshrine the Sarira relics of the Buddha, they added a fresh gilt-bronze narrative plaque. During the 1960s, before the Korean government protected the Guhwang-dong ruins, grave robbers tunneled under the thirty-ton Simchuseok heart-stone and stole the octagonal gilt-bronze reliquary box. Miraculously, South Korean police recovered the looted treasures two years later, including the 872 CE Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda Sarihamgi (Chaljubongi—the gilt-bronze inscription plate commissioned by Silla King Gyeongmun during the 872 CE rebuilding), which confirmed word-for-word that Queen Seondeok, Monk Jajang, Prince Kim Yong-chun, and Master Abiji had built the pagoda in 645 CE!

Over eight years of scientific excavation at the Hwangnyongsa site (1976–1983), archaeologists unearthed more than 40,000 artifacts, including a grotesque 1.82-meter-tall Chi-mi —thelargest ceramic roof-ridge finial ever found in East Asia, decorated with lotus blossoms and a smiling human face sculpted by the potter’s thumb into the wet clay—which once perched atop the main Golden Hall roof. Finally, in the autumn of 1238, during the third Mongol invasion of Korea, Mongol horsemen put the entire Hwangnyongsa complex and Abiji’s nine-story wooden colossus to the torch. Chroniclers wrote that the resinous pine timbers of the eighty-meter tower burned for days, turning the night sky over Gyeongju blood-red.

Walk today into the Hwangnyongsa History and Culture Center right beside the Guhwang-dong foundation stones, where a 1:10 scale wooden model—already eight meters tall!—shows Abiji’s nine-tiered brackets soaring overhead, and then step back out onto the grass beside the thirty-ton Simchuseok stone. Even in its absence, the shadow of the eighty-meter wooden skyscraper still stretches across the Gyeongju plain.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Seismic & Wind Engineering: Pantuk Foundations and the Central 'Simju' Mast

Structural engineering simulations of the Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda demonstrate why an 80-meter timber tower survived 593 years in Gyeongju's seismic fault zone before being burned by war in 1238. First, Master Abiji's rammed-earth and boulder foundation (pantuk) dissipated ground-shear waves before they reached the 64 granite column bases. Second, the independent central wooden mast (simju) anchored on the 30-ton Simchuseok heart-stone oscillated out of phase with the outer nine-tiered timber frame during earthquakes and typhoons—functioning exactly like the pendulum damper inside Taipei 101.

2. The 872 CE Gilt-Bronze 'Chaljubongi' Plaque: When Archaeology Proved Legend

For centuries, skeptics wondered whether the Samguk Yusa's thirteenth-century account of Queen Seondeok, Monk Jajang, and Baekje architect Abiji building the pagoda in 645 CE had been embellished by folklore. When the gilt-bronze reliquary casket (Sariham) engraved during King Gyeongmun's 872 CE reconstruction of the pagoda was recovered from the hollow beneath the Simchuseok stone, its 900-character inscription (Chaljubongi) verified every single historical name, date, and measurement recorded by Monk Iryeon four centuries later.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Remember three architectural terms: first, Simchuseok, the 30-ton central granite foundation boulder hollowed out to hold the Buddha's relics and support the central mast; second, Abiji, the seventh-century Baekje master carpenter who transcended wartime enmity to build Silla's greatest monument; and third, Chimi, the 182-centimeter ceramic owl-tail roof ridge ornament unearthed at Hwangnyongsa and now displayed at the Gyeongju National Museum.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Walk the open archaeological plain of the Hwangnyongsa Temple Site in Guhwang-dong, Gyeongju, where you can stand directly beside the 64 granite column bases and the 4.2-meter Simchuseok heart-stone. Immediately west stands the Hwangnyongsa History and Culture Center housing the three-story 1:10 timber replica, while the Gyeongju National Museum displays the original 872 CE gilt-bronze Sariham and the giant Chimi roof finial.
