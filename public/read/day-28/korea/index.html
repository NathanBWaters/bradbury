Hwangnyongsa's Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda (645 CE): The 80-Meter Colossus Built Without a Single Iron Nail

Korea — How Queen Seondeok of Silla hired a rival Baekje master carpenter to raise a 225-*cheok* wooden skyscraper anchored on a thirty-ton granite heart-stone (Silla Kingdom (553–645 CE to 1238 Mongol Invasion)) • 20 min read

“If Your Majesty builds a nine-story wooden pagoda at Hwangnyongsa, the nine neighboring nations will bow in tribute and Silla will enjoy lasting peace.” — Monk Jajang to Queen Seondeok (Samguk Yusa, 643 CE)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk across the open grassy plain of Guhwang-dong in central Gyeongju today, and you see an array of sixty-four massive granite pillar bases arranged around a colossal central foundation boulder measuring 4.2 meters across and weighing thirty metric tons. From 645 CE until Mongol troops torched it in 1238, those stones held up the Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda—soaring roughly eighty meters (the height of a modern twenty-six-story skyscraper) into the sky without a single structural iron nail. Commissioned by Korea's first female sovereign, Queen Seondeok of Silla, on the advice of the pilgrim monk Jajang and engineered by master carpenter Abiji of rival Baekje, it stood for 593 years as one of the tallest timber structures ever built by human hands.

Stand in the center of the vast, windswept meadow of **Guhwang-dong** in Gyeongju—just east of Bunhwangsa Temple and north of Wolseong Palace—and look down at the square terrace of compacted earth and granite beneath your shoes. Arranged in an eight-by-eight grid spanning 22.2 meters on each side sit sixty-four dressed granite column bases (*chuseok*, 초석). At the exact geometric intersection of the grid rests a single titanic block of rough granite measuring 4.2 by 3.0 meters and weighing over thirty metric tons, hollowed out in the center for a reliquary casket. This is the **Simchuseok** (심초석, 'Heart-Stone') of the **Hwangnyongsa Nine-Story Wooden Pagoda** (황룡사 9층 목탑). Recorded in both the *Samguk Sagi* and Monk Iryeon’s *Samguk Yusa* as measuring **225 Tang *cheok*** in total height (183 *cheok* for the nine timber stories plus 42 *cheok* for the bronze finial mast), modern architectural historians calculate that the pagoda stood **between 79 and 82 meters tall**—equivalent to a modern twenty-six-story office building, built entirely of interlocking red pine timber without a single structural iron nail! The story of Hwangnyongsa ('Temple of the Imperial Yellow Dragon') begins in the second lunar month of 553 CE, when King Jinheung of Silla ordered his architects to build a new royal palace beside Half-Moon Castle (*Banwolseong*). When workmen excavated the marshy ground, a miraculous yellow dragon appeared at the site. Taking the omen as a command from Heaven, King Jinheung immediately canceled the palace and converted the 28,000-square-meter precinct into Silla’s grand state Buddhist monastery, housing a colossal five-meter gilt-bronze triad of Shakyamuni Buddha cast in 574 CE. Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Hwangnyongsa Temple Site in Gyeongju stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Abiji (아비지 Baekje master architect-carpenter) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Simchuseok (심초석 30-ton central granite heart-stone) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

Ninety years later, in the 640s, the Kingdom of Silla faced an existential geopolitical crisis. Surrounded by its fierce Korean rivals—Baekje to the west and Goguryeo to the north—as well as Wa (Japan) across the strait and nomadic tribes beyond the frontier, Silla was ruled by its first female sovereign: **Queen Seondeok** (선덕여왕, r. 632–647), the same brilliant monarch who built the 362-stone *Cheomseongdae* astronomical observatory. At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. In 643 CE, the high monk **Jajang Yulsa** (자장율사) returned from a seven-year pilgrimage to Mount Wutai in Tang China and requested an urgent audience with Queen Seondeok. Jajang reported that a divine spirit at Taihe Lake had revealed to him that the dragon guardian of Hwangnyongsa was his eldest son, and that if Silla constructed a **nine-story wooden pagoda** at Hwangnyongsa, the kingdom would symbolically subdue the **Nine Neighboring Enemies** (*Gu-i*, 구이)—one enemy nation assigned to each of the nine stories, from Japan on the first story and China on the second to Baekje, Goguryeo, and the Mohe on the upper tiers. Yet there was a fatal engineering problem: seventh-century Silla stonecutters knew how to carve granite and brick (as at nearby Bunhwangsa in 634 CE), but nobody in Silla possessed the structural carpentry mastery required to raise an eighty-meter multi-story timber tower that would not buckle under its own weight or blow over in coastal typhoons.
