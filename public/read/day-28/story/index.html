The Fight of the Good Ship Clarissa

Ray Bradbury (1938) • 4 min read

“The field was silent. The ship lay gleaming in the pink light of dawn that was just blooming over the mountains like a pale flower.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Fight of the Good Ship Clarissa is one of the earliest things Ray Bradbury ever published. It appeared in June 1938 in Imagination!, the fan magazine of the Los Angeles Science Fantasy Society, when he was seventeen and still a student at Los Angeles High School. It is not a story in the usual sense. It is a burlesque, a piece of deliberate nonsense that makes fun of the heroic space adventures filling the pulp magazines of the day, the ones with fearless rocket crews, hostile aliens and last-ditch battles. Bradbury had grown up devouring those magazines, and in the late thirties he and a circle of young fans in Los Angeles wrote, traded and teased each other about them. So this is a joke told by a fan to other fans, full of puns, wild similes and in-jokes about magazine editors. Read it as a teenager's playful goodbye to the clichés he loved, and listen for the sound of a future master testing out his voice.

The space rocket Clarissa was nine days out from Venus. The members of the crew were also out for nine days. They were hunters, fearless expeditionists who bagged game in Venusian jungles. At the start of our story they are busy bagging their pants, not to forget their eyes. A sort of lull has fallen over the ship (Note: a lull is a time warp that frequently attacks rockets and seduces its members into a siesta). It was during this lull that Anthony Quelch sat sprawled at his typewriter looking as baggy as a bag of unripe grapefruit. Anthony Quelch, the Cosmic Clamor Boy, with a face like turned linoleum on the third term, busy writing a book: Fascism Is Communism with a Shave for which he would receive 367 rubles, 10 pazinkas and incarceration in a cinema showing Gone with the Wind.

The boys upstairs were throwing a party in the control room. They had been throwing the same party so long the party looked like a worn out first edition of a trapeze artist. There is doubt in our mind as to whether they were trying to break the party up or just do the morning mopping and break the lease simultaneously. Arms, legs and heads littered the deck. The boys, it seems, threw a party at the drop of a chin. Sort of a space cataclysm with rules and little regulation⁠—kind of an atomic convulsion in the front parlor. The neighbors never complained. The neighbors were 450 million miles away. And the boys were tighter than a catsup bottle at lunchtime. The last time the captain had looked up the hatch and called to his kiddies in a gentle voice, “Hell!” the kiddies had thrown snowballs at him. The captain had vanished. Clever way they make these space bombs nowadays. A few minutes previous the boys had been tearing up old Amazings and throwing them at one another, but now they contented themselves with tearing up just the editors. Palmer was torn in half and he sat in a corner arguing with himself about rejecting a story for an hour before someone put him through an orange juice machine killing him. (Orange juice sorry, now?)

And then they landed on Venus. How in heck they got back there so quick is a wonder of science, but there they were. “Come on, girls!” cried Quelch, “put on your shin guards, get out there and dig ditches for good old W.P.A. and the Rover Boys Academy, earth branch 27!”

Out into the staggering rain they dashed. Five minutes later they came back in, gasping, reeling. They had forgotten their corsets! The Venusians closed in like a million landlords. “Charge, men!” cried Quelch, running the other way. And then⁠—battle! “What a fight, folks,” cried Quelch. “Twenty thousand earth men against two Venusians! We’re outnumbered, but we’ll fight!” Bloosh! “Correction⁠—ten thousand men fighting!” Kerblom! “One hundred men from earth left!” Boom! “This is the last man speaking, folks! What a fight. I ain’t had so much fun since⁠—Help, someone just clipped my corset strings!” Bwom! “Someone just clipped me!”

The field was silent. The ship lay gleaming in the pink light of dawn that was just blooming over the mountains like a pale flower.

The two Venusians stood weeping over the bodies of the Earthlings like onion peelers or two women in a bargain basement. One Venusian looked at the other Venusian, and in a high-pitched, hoarse, sad voice said: “Aye, aye, aye⁠—this⁠—hit shooden heppen to a dog⁠—not a doidy leedle dog!” And dawn came peacefully, like beer barrels, rolling.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

There is almost no plot to decipher, and that is the point. The rocket Clarissa is nine days out from Venus, and the crew, who are supposed to be fearless big-game hunters, are busy getting drunk at a party in the control room. The narrator keeps interrupting himself with fake explanations, such as the note that a lull is a time warp that attacks rockets and seduces the crew into a siesta. A man named Anthony Quelch, called the Cosmic Clamor Boy, sprawls at a typewriter writing a book called Fascism Is Communism with a Shave. Then, with no real transition, the ship has landed on Venus, the crew rushes out into the rain, rushes back in because they forgot their corsets, and a battle begins that is narrated by Quelch as a sports broadcaster would call it, with the number of surviving earthmen dropping from twenty thousand to ten thousand to one hundred to one. When the dust settles, two Venusians stand weeping over the bodies.

The best way to read this is as a parody of a type of story, not of any single story. The pulp space adventure of the thirties had a familiar set of moves: the brave crew, the strange planet, the monstrous natives, the desperate fight, the grim aftermath. Bradbury runs all of these through a blender. The brave crew is drunk and ridiculous. The monstrous natives are two small figures who speak in a Brooklyn-style dialect and cry. The desperate fight is a series of sound effects and shouted announcements. The grim aftermath, which a serious pulp would play for pathos, is played for laughs with a Venusian saying that this should not happen to a dog, not a dirty little dog. It is a story that deflates its own genre.

The details are also a map of what a teenager in 1938 had on his mind. The mention of Gone with the Wind and the W.P.A. and the Rover Boys places the joke in the popular culture of the time, and the references to old copies of Amazing and an editor named Palmer who is torn in half and then put through an orange juice machine are shots at the real editorial world of the science fiction magazines. Palmer is almost certainly Raymond Palmer, who had taken over Amazing Stories in 1938. The joke is that the fans who adore these magazines also love to imagine tearing up the people who reject their stories.

Beneath all this nonsense there is a serious insight: a genre can only be parodied by someone who loves it. The jokes are affectionate. The final image, the dawn blooming over the mountains like a pale flower, followed instantly by a comic cry and a simile about beer barrels rolling, shows a young writer who cannot resist beautiful language even when he is mocking it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged as a finished story, The Fight of the Good Ship Clarissa is a mess, and its author would probably have agreed. Judged as a stunt, it is surprisingly lively, and an honest assessment has to hold both of those truths at once.

What works is the sheer inventiveness of the sentences. Bradbury throws out comparisons at a reckless rate, and a fair number of them land. A man looks as baggy as a bag of unripe grapefruit. A face is like turned linoleum on the third term. The boys were tighter than a catsup bottle at lunchtime. The Venusians close in like a million landlords. Some of these are wonderful in the way absurd comparisons can be, because they are specific and unexpected. The parenthetical asides, like the claim that a lull is a time warp that seduces a crew into a siesta, mimic the pompous scientific explanations of the pulps and then undercut them. The cheerful violence toward language is exactly what a young writer with a gift for metaphor would do when given permission to be silly.

The sound-effects battle is also a well-judged piece of comic timing. Quelch announcing the shrinking numbers, with explosions in between, is a compact little routine that captures the way pulp action turns into noise. The final twist, with the victors being two crying Venusians, is a good reversal because it is delivered so quickly that the reader barely has time to see it coming.

But the weaknesses are everywhere. The humor is relentlessly scattershot, and the jokes come so fast that many of them miss. There are puns that are mostly noise, topical references that are impossible to follow without a footnote, and long runs where the metaphors seem to be there for their own sake. The story has no characters beyond the barest sketches and no narrative drive; the leap from the party to the landing on Venus is acknowledged as nonsense in the text itself, with the narrator wondering aloud how they got back so quickly. The political joke in the book title is cryptic and the story does nothing with it. A great deal of the humor depends on knowing the world of 1930s fandom, which makes it opaque to most modern readers.

There is also a sense in which the whole thing is a one-note performance. A parody works best when it has something to say about its target, and this one mostly shouts that the target is silly. Still, the energy is real, and the experiment is a revealing one. Many young writers copy their heroes, but fewer have the nerve to wreck them, and the wreckage here is full of life.

III. The Impact It Had

It is important to be straightforward: this story had essentially no public impact. It appeared in a fan magazine with a tiny circulation, and no review, adaptation or major critical discussion of it is on record. Its importance is in what it tells us about a famous writer's beginnings.

In 1938, Bradbury was seventeen and part of a lively circle of science fiction fans in Los Angeles. The Los Angeles Science Fantasy Society, whose magazine Imagination! printed this story, was one of the places where young enthusiasts met, argued and published each other. Bradbury was a regular at its gatherings, where he mingled with other fans and with established writers who came through town. The society's fanzines were a kind of training ground, offering a place to see your name in print, to receive immediate feedback and to try out styles without the pressure of selling to a professional editor. Bradbury would graduate from high school in 1938 and go on to sell newspapers on the street while writing in the evenings, and these fanzine pieces were part of the long apprenticeship that preceded his professional sales.

In that light, this story is a document of a particular stage. It shows a young writer playing with voice, using exaggeration and wordplay to find what he could do. Bradbury himself later talked often about the importance of writing many bad stories to get to the good ones, and a piece like this is an example of the early, unpolished output that made that process possible. The habits it displays, the love of vivid comparisons, the willingness to turn a sentence on its head for the sake of a picture, and the instinct to take a familiar scenario and twist it, never left him. They would be harnessed to much more serious purposes in the stories of the forties and fifties.

It also reflects the culture of fandom that nurtured him. The science fiction community of the thirties was a small, gossipy world, and in-jokes about editors and writers were a form of belonging. Bradbury's later fame did not erase that: he remained a lifelong champion of fans and libraries.

As for how it is read today, it is mostly of interest to collectors and Bradbury scholars. It occasionally turns up in discussions of his juvenilia, and it is useful for teaching because it shows the raw material from which a style grows. It is not a story that anyone has argued changed literature, but it is a small piece of evidence that a seventeen-year-old with a typewriter and a strong sense of humor can begin to find a voice.

IV. What Readers Loved

Reactions to this story are not well documented. A fanzine piece from 1938 would have been read by a few dozen or a few hundred people who knew one another, and their responses, if recorded at all, are not part of any easily accessible record. What I can offer is an account of what a reader might find enjoyable in it, particularly a reader who comes to it now as a Bradbury fan.

The first pleasure is the language. Bradbury's gift for metaphor shows even in this absurd setting, and some of the lines are plainly delightful. The comparison of a face to turned linoleum, the party that looks like a worn-out first edition of a trapeze artist, the neighbors who never complained because they were four hundred and fifty million miles away: these are lines that make a reader smile because they are surprising and compressed. Readers who love Bradbury for his prose will find in this story a rough, comic version of the same pleasure.

The second is the fearless silliness. The story does not apologize for itself. It dives into its own nonsense with a straight face, which is a quality that readers of comedy appreciate. The fake scientific note about the lull, the narrator who wonders how they got to Venus so quickly, and the battle that is announced like a sports event all show a writer who is having fun, and the fun is catching.

The third is the in-joke warmth. For people who knew the pulp magazines, a story that threw a party in which the crew tears up old issues of Amazing and then the editors themselves is a way of laughing at something they loved. The affection comes through even in the violence. Fans have always enjoyed poking at the things they care most about, and this story is an early example of that tradition.

The fourth is the final image. After all the noise, the story closes on a quiet picture of dawn over the mountains and two weeping figures, and then ruins it with a joke about beer barrels. It is a nice small demonstration of how a writer can hold beauty and comedy in the same hand. Many readers find that moment charming precisely because it is so brief.

Above all, readers who care about the history of a great writer value the story as a rare glimpse of the beginning. There is something endearing about a seventeen-year-old who already cannot stop making metaphors, and the story gives a sense of how much joy he took in words from the start.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the story is so obscure, there is no recorded body of criticism to describe. What follows are the objections that a fair-minded reader today might make, which are also the ordinary complaints about juvenile burlesque.

The first is the obvious one: it is not really a story. There is no plot to speak of, no character with any depth, and no emotional stake. The reader cannot care about the crew, because the crew is a joke, and cannot care about the Venusians, because they are punchlines. A story that is only a string of gags will always feel slight, and this one lasts only a few hundred words before it runs out of steam.

The second is the density of the jokes. In a short space the story crams puns, parodies, topical references and wild similes, and a good number of them do not work. Many references, such as the allusions to long-forgotten magazines and the offhand jokes about rubles and pazinkas and corsets, are hard to understand without knowing the context. Even a reader who appreciates the humor may find the noise exhausting, and the strain of trying to be funny in every sentence can make the piece feel forced.

The third is the matter of taste. Some of the humor depends on jokes about women's clothing, about foreigners and about accents. The Venusian speaks in a thick comic dialect, which was a common device in the humor of the day but is not likely to delight everyone now. A reader sensitive to such things may find the story dated, though it is hardly more offensive than much popular comedy of the period. It is more fair to say that it is bound to its time than to condemn it.

A fourth objection is about the political joke. The title of Quelch's book, which equates fascism and communism, is thrown in without any follow-through, and it is unclear whether Bradbury intends it as a real opinion, as a parody of the kind of glib political slogans that were in the air, or simply as a joke. The story does not help us decide.

None of these complaints should be pressed too hard. A seventeen-year-old's comic sketch for a fan club cannot be held to the same standard as a published novel. The criticisms are really observations about why it remains a curiosity and not a classic.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Fight of the Good Ship Clarissa belongs to two overlapping traditions: the fan culture of 1930s science fiction, and the long history of literary burlesque. The first explains why it exists. The second explains how it works.

Science fiction fandom began in the late twenties and thirties, when readers of the pulp magazines started writing to the letter columns, found each other, and began forming clubs. By the time Bradbury joined the Los Angeles group, there was an established tradition of fan magazines, little publications typed or mimeographed by enthusiasts, where members could publish stories, articles, jokes and gossip. Much of this material was, frankly, amateur, but some of it was a training ground for professionals. Many of the writers and editors who shaped the genre over the next decades got their start in this world, and the habit of teasing the magazines they loved was part of it. A parody like this one is a fan's way of showing how well he knows the formulas.

Burlesque is an old art. The idea of mocking a heroic genre by exaggerating its conventions goes back to Cervantes and Don Quixote, and it includes works like Henry Fielding's Shamela, Jane Austen's Northanger Abbey, which gently mocks the Gothic novel, and many later parodies of adventure and romance. In America, the humorists of the early twentieth century, with their love of absurd metaphors and mock-learned digressions, were a likely influence on a young writer with an ear for comic patter. The pulp space opera was an especially tempting target, because it was so earnest. Later science fiction would parody the field with more sophistication, as in the comic work of writers such as Douglas Adams, whose spaceships and aliens deflate the grand conventions in the same spirit, though with far more polish.

Bradbury's later career took him away from this kind of joke. Within a few years he was writing the weird and moving stories that became Dark Carnival and then The Martian Chronicles, where Mars is a place of nostalgia and loss and not a battlefield. But you can see in this early burlesque some of the qualities that remained: the fascination with metaphor, the sense of play, and the love of the genre he was mocking.

Why read it now? Not because it is a lost masterpiece, but because it is short, odd and revealing. It is one of the few places you can watch a great writer in his first public attempts, throwing words around with delight, long before he learned how to make them hurt. For anyone who loves Bradbury, it is a small and cheerful piece of the foundation.
