Nightly Poem • Romantic Ode & Lyric

When You Are Old

W. B. Yeats (1892) • 3 min read

Imagining his unrequited love Maud Gonne nodding by the fire in old age, Yeats urges her to take down this book and remember the one man who loved her pilgrim soul.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Yeats wrote this poem around 1891, when he was in his mid-twenties and hopelessly in love with Maud Gonne, a beautiful, fiery Irish nationalist and actress who admired his mind but would not marry him. It is a free adaptation of a French sonnet by Pierre de Ronsard, written in the sixteenth century, in which an old poet tells the woman he loves that she will regret refusing him. Yeats keeps the setup but changes the mood. Instead of scolding, his speaker gently imagines the woman years from now, old, drowsy by the fire, opening a book of his poems and remembering the one man who loved her true self and not just her beauty. It appeared in his collection The Rose, in the Irish Revival style of the time. It is short, simple and moving, and it has become one of the most loved love poems in English. Notice how tenderly it asks for nothing, and how sad that makes it.

When you are old and grey and full of sleep,

And nodding by the fire, take down this book,

And slowly read; and dream of the soft look

Your eyes had once, and of their shadows deep;

How many loved your moments of glad grace,

And loved your beauty with love false or true,

But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you,

And loved the sorrows of your changing face;

And bending down beside the glowing bars

Murmur, a little sadly, how love fled

And paced upon the mountains overhead

And hid his face amid a crowd of stars.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a letter to the future. The speaker pictures a woman, years from now, old and grey and full of sleep, nodding by a fire. He asks her to take down this book, meaning the book of his poems, and read slowly. As she reads, she will dream of the soft look her eyes once had and the deep shadows in them. In other words, the poem begins by imagining the aging of the person he loves, and it does so without any cruelty. It makes the picture warm: a fire, a chair, a book, a quiet evening.

The second stanza is the center of the argument. Many loved her moments of glad grace, he says, and loved her beauty, with love false or true. This is delicately worded. Some of those admirers were sincere and some were not, and the speaker does not say which. But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you, and loved the sorrows of your changing face. The line about the pilgrim soul is the key. A pilgrim is someone on a long, difficult spiritual journey. To love someone's pilgrim soul is to love their inner searching, their restlessness, their unfinished quest, not their surface charm. And to love the sorrows of her changing face is to love her as she ages, to see time and pain as part of her beauty rather than damage to it. That is a stronger claim than most love poems make. He is not saying he loves the woman at her best. He is saying he loves her all the way through.

Yeats was writing about Maud Gonne, who was widely admired for her striking looks, and the poem seems partly aimed at a woman used to being valued for her appearance. He is quietly saying: I saw you differently.

The last stanza turns toward loss. She is bending beside the glowing bars of the fire and murmuring, a little sadly, how love fled. Love is personified as a figure who paced upon the mountains overhead and hid his face amid a crowd of stars. This is one of the most beautiful images in the poem. Love does not die; he escapes upward, becomes remote and starlike, and is lost in a crowd. It suggests that love, once missed, does not end but becomes unreachable, like something seen in the sky.

A reader might miss how the poem manages its tone. It is a love poem, but it is also a gentle reproach, and an offering, and a prediction. The speaker never asks her to change her mind. He simply leaves behind a record of his love, trusting that she will eventually understand it. The poem's deepest wish is not to win her but to be known.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

It is hard to criticize this poem without feeling slightly ungracious, because its virtues are so plain. But a fair appraisal should try, and it should begin with what is genuinely excellent.

The craft is nearly flawless. The three quatrains use a rhyme scheme in which the outer lines of each stanza rhyme with each other and the inner lines rhyme with each other: sleep and deep, book and look, in the first stanza, for example. This enclosed pattern gives the poem a feeling of being wrapped, almost cradled, which suits its theme. The rhythm is a smooth iambic line of ten syllables, with a slow movement that suits the nodding old woman. Even the sounds are chosen with care. The long, drowsy vowels and soft consonants of the opening, grey, sleep, nodding, fire, put the reader to sleep along with the subject, and the sharper, brighter sounds of the last line, crowd of stars, bring us suddenly awake.

The poem is also a model of structure. The first stanza sets a scene, the second delivers the argument, and the third turns the argument into an image. Nothing is wasted. The two-part thought of the second stanza, many loved the beauty but one loved the soul, is stated with such economy that it feels inevitable.

Where does it falter? To a modern reader, the poem's gender dynamics can feel dated. The woman is a silent object of the speaker's contemplation, imagined and arranged by him. She does not speak, she does not get to say whether she wanted to be loved this way, and her future is written for her. There is something quietly presumptuous in a poem that tells her what she will feel when she is old. It can also be read as a subtle form of emotional pressure: the loving man who says, you will regret this. Yeats softens it, but the pressure is not entirely absent.

There is also a certain sweetness verging on sentimentality. The image of the fire, the book, the old woman's gentle sorrow, is perhaps the most familiar picture in romantic poetry. Compared with the more complex and bitter poems Yeats later wrote about the same woman, this one can seem a little easy. Its final image, though lovely, is also a touch ornamental, and it belongs to the soft, decorative style that Yeats himself later came to distrust.

Finally, because it is an adaptation, it is fair to ask how original it is. The central situation comes from Ronsard. What is Yeats's, however, is the tonal transformation, and it is substantial. The question is not whether he borrowed but whether he improved, and most readers feel that on emotional grounds he did. Even so, the poem owes much of its structure to another man's idea, and it remains a work of grace more than of daring.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem came early in Yeats's career, and its place in his life story is as important as its place in literary history. It was written to and about Maud Gonne, the great obsession of his youth and the muse behind much of his best poetry. The proposals he made to her over the years, and her repeated refusals, shaped not only his love life but his sense of himself as a poet. This poem is one of the first and gentlest results. It appeared in The Rose, a book that helped establish him as a leading figure of the Irish Literary Revival and, through its lyric beauty, as one of the most promising young poets in English.

Its larger influence has been on the way love poems are written. The central move, imagining the beloved's old age and offering a love that will survive it, has become one of the most copied gestures in romantic verse. It is echoed in countless wedding readings, greeting cards and love letters. People who have never heard of Yeats may know the opening line, or hear it as a lyric in song. The poem has been set to music by a great many composers and folk and pop musicians over the years, and its first line has served as the title or inspiration for songs, novels and films.

The poem also had an influence on how readers think of Yeats and Gonne. Their story, a poet's lifelong devotion to a woman who did not return it, became part of the legend of modern Irish literature, and this poem is one of the pieces of evidence cited. Biographers and scholars have used it to discuss the connection between life and art, and the question of how much a poem can be read as autobiography.

In education, it is often among the first Yeats poems taught. Its clear structure and manageable length make it ideal for introducing ideas about rhyme, tone, address and adaptation. Teachers use it alongside Ronsard to show how a writer can borrow a situation and make it new, and how a single change of emphasis can alter a poem's whole meaning.

It also stands as an example of how the Irish Revival worked. Yeats and his circle wanted to create a distinctly Irish literature, but they also drew freely on European sources. This poem, rooted in French Renaissance verse and spoken in an English voice with an Irish heart, is an illustration of how local and international traditions could meet.

Perhaps the poem's most lasting impact is simply emotional. For many readers it has been the first poem that made them feel what poetry could do, and for some it has been the poem they read to someone they love. That cultural afterlife, in weddings, anniversaries and quiet bedside moments, is a form of influence that no scholarly measure can capture.

IV. What Readers Loved

What have people loved about this poem? Above all, its tenderness. Most love poems either praise the beloved or plead with her. This one does something rarer: it makes a promise that asks for nothing in return. The speaker does not say, love me or you will regret it. He says, in effect, when you are old and reading this, remember that someone saw you truly. That generosity is what readers return to. It feels like love that has already given up the hope of being repaid and has turned into something purer.

Readers also love the central idea that there is a difference between being admired and being loved. Many people know what it is like to be praised for how they look or what they achieve, and to wonder whether anyone sees what is underneath. The line about the pilgrim soul speaks directly to that longing. Being loved for the sorrows of a changing face is a deeply consoling idea, particularly for anyone who has felt the pressure of aging, appearance or expectation. The poem tells us that wrinkles and sorrow can be part of what is loved, not what must be forgiven.

There is also the sheer music. The lines move so smoothly that the poem sounds like a lullaby, and it is among the easiest of Yeats's poems to memorize. Its opening, with its slow rhythm and sleepy vowels, creates an immediate hush. Readers describe it as one of those poems that seems to have always existed.

Writers and critics have admired the economy of the poem. In twelve lines it contains a scene, an argument and a vision. Many have noted how effectively Yeats turned Ronsard's brisk warning into something warmer and sadder. Where the French poem pushes, the Irish one waits. Readers who know both often prefer Yeats's version for that reason, finding it more humane.

The final image has a special fascination. Love pacing on the mountains and hiding his face among a crowd of stars is a picture that lingers long after the rest of the poem fades. It is dreamlike, a little mysterious, and it gives the poem a touch of the sublime. Readers often say they cannot fully explain it but feel its meaning, which is the sign of a successful image.

Finally, the poem is loved for its use. It has been read aloud at weddings, anniversaries and funerals. It appeals to anyone who wants to say something about long love, lost love or the passing of time without finding the words themselves. A poem that serves such human needs earns a special kind of affection, one that goes beyond critical approval into something like gratitude.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because the poem is so well loved, serious objections to it are less often heard, but they exist and are worth knowing.

The first has to do with its attitude toward the woman. A number of modern critics, especially those writing from feminist perspectives, have noted that the poem silences her. She is the object of a male speaker's fantasy, and her inner life is something he claims to understand better than anyone. The poem's claim that only one man loved the pilgrim soul in her is a bold assertion of exclusive insight, and some readers find it presumptuous, even self-flattering. The speaker positions himself as the one who truly saw her, and by implication everyone else, including perhaps her actual partners, as shallow. It is a tender form of vanity.

A related criticism concerns the emotional pressure. For all its softness, the poem is still a plea, a way of saying that she will regret not choosing him. It was written to a woman who had refused him, and it can be read as a gentle form of guilt. Maud Gonne herself, according to the accounts of those who knew her, had a more complicated and less romantic view of her relationship with Yeats than the poem suggests, and her independence is something the poem does not acknowledge.

On the literary side, some critics have felt that the poem belongs to the weaker, more decorative phase of Yeats's early work. The Celtic Twilight style, with its dreamy, vaguely mournful atmosphere, was later criticized by Yeats himself, who saw it as too pale and too far from real life. Judged against the harder, more complex poems of his later years, this one can look like a lovely but slight piece, polished rather than deep. Its images, firelight, stars, mountains, belong to a stock of romantic scenery.

Questions of originality have also been raised. Since the poem is a free adaptation of Ronsard, a few readers have felt it should be understood as a translation or a variation and not as a fully independent work. Yeats did not hide the connection, and he made significant changes, but the debt is real.

Finally, a few readers find the poem's sadness slightly too sweet. They feel that it lingers over its own pathos, enjoying the picture of a future regret, and that a harsher honesty would be more convincing. This is a matter of taste, but it explains why the poem is sometimes admired more for its beauty than for its force.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To see where this poem sits, start with its ancestors. It belongs to an ancient and very large family: the carpe diem poem, the poem that tells a beloved to seize the day because beauty fades. Roman poets such as Horace gave that theme its name, and Renaissance poets in France, England and Italy made it a staple. Ronsard, writing to a young woman at the French court in his Sonnets pour Helene, took the theme and gave it a sharp point: you will be old by candlelight, and you will regret having been cold to me. English poets such as Robert Herrick, with his advice to gather rosebuds, and Andrew Marvell, with his argument that if there were world enough and time, wrote variations on the same idea.

Yeats's poem takes that tradition and turns it on its head. The carpe diem poem normally says: hurry. His poem says: remember. It substitutes memory and a quiet fidelity for urgency and persuasion. That shift is what makes it distinctive, and it connects it to another great tradition, the poem of faithful, unrequited love, stretching back through Petrarch and the courtly poets to Dante's love for Beatrice. In that tradition, love that is not returned becomes a spiritual discipline, and the poet's devotion is a kind of pilgrimage. Yeats's use of the word pilgrim is therefore not accidental.

Within its period, the poem is a representative piece of the Celtic Twilight and Irish Revival, with its dreamy tones, its melancholy, and its fascination with fading beauty. It descends from the Romantic and Pre-Raphaelite poets Yeats admired, such as Shelley and Rossetti. Its descendants are the many later love poems that address someone in the future, including the countless modern lyrics that promise to love through age and loss. Within Yeats's work, it can be compared with later poems to and about Maud Gonne, such as No Second Troy and Adam's Curse, which are far tougher and more bitter, and which show how much his view of her, and of himself, changed.

It is also a good example of the way one poet can respond to another across centuries. Reading the Ronsard sonnet and the Yeats poem together is one of the best short lessons in how poetry works: the same situation can be turned into a threat or a blessing by changing a few key words and the angle of feeling.

Why does it still matter? Because it speaks to a need that has not gone away. In a world that celebrates youth and surfaces, here is a poem that insists a person can be loved for their journey and their sorrows. It is short enough to carry in the mind, and it offers a small, steady argument that depth of feeling can outlast looks, success and even hope. It is also a reminder that the most powerful gift a poem can give is not persuasion but witness: the sense that somebody saw you.
