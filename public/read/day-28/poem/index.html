When You Are Old

W. B. Yeats (1892) • 3 min read

Imagining his unrequited love Maud Gonne nodding by the fire in old age, Yeats urges her to take down this book and remember the one man who loved her pilgrim soul.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Composed in October 1891 when twenty-six-year-old W. B. Yeats was consumed by his lifelong, unrequited passion for the fiery Irish nationalist actress and revolutionary Maud Gonne, 'When You Are Old' creatively adapts Pierre de Ronsard’s famous 1578 French Renaissance sonnet 'Quand vous serez bien vieille' from Sonnets pour Hélène. Belonging to the Celtic Twilight / Irish Revival tradition and categorized under Romantic Ode & Lyric, 'When You Are Old' (1892) unfolds across 12 verbatim lines in 3 stanzas. Whereas Ronsard’s original Renaissance sonnet ended with a sharp carpe diem warning ('gather the roses of life today'), Yeats transforms the poem into a tender, non-coercive elegy that became one of the most cherished love poems in the English language.

When you are old and grey and full of sleep,

And nodding by the fire, take down this book,

And slowly read; and dream of the soft look

Your eyes had once, and of their shadows deep;

How many loved your moments of glad grace,

And loved your beauty with love false or true,

But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you,

And loved the sorrows of your changing face;

And bending down beside the glowing bars

Murmur, a little sadly, how love fled

And paced upon the mountains overhead

And hid his face amid a crowd of stars.

From its unforgettable opening cadence—"When you are old and grey and full of sleep,"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 12 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'When You Are Old' lies the distinction between superficial infatuation with youthful physical beauty ('How many loved your moments of glad grace') and unconditional devotion to a person’s restless, evolving inner spirit ('But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you, / And loved the sorrows of your changing face'). As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you,"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Whereas Ronsard’s original Renaissance sonnet ended with a sharp carpe diem warning ('gather the roses of life today'), Yeats transforms the poem into a tender, non-coercive elegy that became one of the most cherished love poems in the English language. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'When You Are Old' has remained a touchstone of the Romantic Ode & Lyric canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
