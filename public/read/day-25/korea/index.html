Baekyangsa's Chunjinam Hermitage: Venerable Jeong Kwan and the Mindful Science of Temple Cuisine

Korea — Why Korean Seon Buddhist nuns cook without garlic, onions, or meat, turning thirty-year-old soy sauce and mountain roots into enlightened nourishment (Baekje Kingdom (632 CE) to Contemporary Seon Monasticism) • 20 min read

“I am not a chef; I am a bhikkhuni (nun). When I cook with water, wind, sunlight, and time inside the jangdokdae jars, food becomes the dharma that connects your body to the mountain.” — Venerable Jeong Kwan, Chunjinam Hermitage

Key Context & Why It Matters

Tucked into a valley of 700-year-old nutmeg yew trees and baby-palm maples beneath the white limestone cliffs of Baekhakbong Peak in Naejangsan National Park, Baekyangsa Temple (founded in 632 CE) and its secluded Chunjinam Hermitage are the spiritual heart of Sachal Eumsik—Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine. Brought to global acclaim by Seon (Zen) Buddhist nun Venerable Jeong Kwan (featured on Netflix's Chef's Table and mentored by culinary icons from Eric Ripert to René Redzepi), Korean temple cuisine strictly abstains from all animal flesh, seafood, and the five pungent allium roots (oshinchae: garlic, onion, scallion, chive, and wild leek) to keep the meditating mind tranquil, relying instead on wild mountain greens (namul), medicinal roots, and multi-decade fermented jang.

Follow the mountain stream past the reflecting pond of *Ssanggyeru* Pavilion at **Baekyangsa Temple** (백양사, founded in 632 CE during the Baekje Kingdom) in Jangseong County, South Jeolla Province, where the white limestone crags of *Baekhakbong* ('White Crane Peak') tower above a forest of 5,000 ancient *Bija-namu* (nutmeg yew) trees and tiny three-lobed *Aegi-danpung* maples. Climb ten minutes higher up the shaded forest road to **Chunjinam Hermitage** (천진암), and you will likely find a sixty-seven-year-old Seon Buddhist nun in gray monastic robes and rubber shoes crouching in the garden soil, whispering to a patch of wild *chamnamul* or lifting the heavy earthenware lid off an *onggi* jar of soy sauce brewed the year she entered the monastery. Nothing in this setting is accidental: the orientation of the ground, the seasonal angle of sunlight, and the acoustic stillness surrounding Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) establish an immediate sensory bridge between twenty-first-century observers and the historical artisans who worked here.

This is **Venerable Jeong Kwan** (정관스님, b. 1957), whom *The New York Times* and Michelin-starred chefs from New York’s Le Bernardin (Eric Ripert) to Copenhagen’s Noma (René Redzepi) have called 'the philosopher-chef of the twenty-first century.' Yet whenever visitors call her a chef, Jeong Kwan laughs and shakes her shaved head: 'A chef cooks to excite the diner’s desires and craving; a Seon nun cooks to quiet the mind so the person eating can wake up to who they truly are.' In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Baekje Kingdom (632 CE) to Contemporary Seon Monasticism, the rise of Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) and Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

This 1,700-year-old culinary tradition is known as **Sachal Eumsik** (사찰음식, Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine). Unlike commercial restaurant food, where ingredients arrive wrapped in plastic from anonymous industrial supply chains, every dish in a Korean mountain monastery is governed by two strict spiritual and ecological boundaries laid down in the *Brahmajala Sutra* (*Beommanggyeong*) and the *Surangama Sutra* (*Neung-eomgyeong*). Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Baekyangsa Temple in Jangseong stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Whether working with calloused hands on Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) before daybreak or debating the finer points of Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) by oil lamp, these pioneers combined empirical observation with a fierce ethical commitment to the communities they served.

First, Korean temple cuisine is 100 percent plant-based (*chaesik*), abstaining completely from meat, poultry, eggs, and even the fermented anchovy or shrimp *jeotgal* used in secular Korean kimchi, out of the vow of *Bul-salsaeng* (불살생, non-harming of sentient beings). Second—and far more surprising to outsiders accustomed to secular Korean cooking—temple cuisine strictly forbids the **Oshinchae** (오신채, 五辛菜): the **Five Pungent Roots** of the *Allium* family: **garlic** (*maneul*), **onions** (*yangpa*), **scallions/green onions** (*pa*), **chives** (*buchu*), and **wild rocambole/leeks** (*dallae*). At the philosophical core of this achievement lay a distinctively Korean synthesis of practical utility and cosmic harmony. Rather than forcing nature into rigid, abstract templates, the creators of Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) aligned Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) with local microclimates, topography, and material physics, demonstrating how the principles of Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) could be translated directly into enduring physical form.

Why would Korean monks and nuns banish garlic and scallions—the very ingredients that form the aromatic foundation of secular Korean cuisine? In Buddhist physiological psychology, eating the five pungent *oshinchae* raw stirs up fiery anger, irritability, and aggression (*jin-sim*, 嗔心) in the nervous system, while eating them cooked excites sexual craving and restlessness (*eum-sim*, 婬心), disturbing the crystal-clear stillness required during twelve hours of daily *Chamnseon* (seated Seon meditation) and breaking harmony in communal sleeping halls. Material selection proved decisive at every stage of the process. Every raw element gathered near Baekyangsa Temple in Jangseong had to be tested for density, moisture response, and structural resilience before it could be incorporated into Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine), ensuring that the interplay between Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) and Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) would withstand centuries of monsoon humidity and freezing winter temperatures.

Without meat broth, fish sauce, beef tallow, garlic, or scallions, how does Venerable Jeong Kwan create dishes of such astonishing umami depth that world-renowned chefs weep at her wooden table? Her answer is **Time, Sunlight, and Mountain Biodiversity**. Closer inspection of the primary mechanism reveals a level of quantitative precision that still astonishes contemporary engineers. By calibrating the tolerances of Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) and balancing the physical forces acting across Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots), the builders eliminated points of mechanical failure while maximizing the functional efficiency of Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) under real-world operating conditions.

Step onto the *jangdokdae* terrace at Chunjinam Hermitage, and you stand before a library of earthenware *onggi* jars holding *ganjang* (soy sauce), *doenjang* (soybean paste), and *gochujang* (chili paste) aged for five, ten, twenty, and even fifty years. Because temple *ganjang* is brewed exclusively from domestic soybeans, roasted bamboo salt (*jukyeom*) or aged Sinan solar sea salt, and pure mountain spring water from Baekhakbong, aging it for a decade under the sun concentrates free glutamates and caramelizes natural soy sugars into a velvety dark elixir that tastes like aged balsamic vinegar crossed with black truffles. Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. When temperature, airflow, or human movement shifted around Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots), the subtle geometry of Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual) absorbed the stress smoothly, turning potential environmental hazards into sources of long-term stability.

Consider one of Jeong Kwan’s signature dishes: **Yeon-geun Jorim** (연근조림, braised nine-hole lotus root). First, crisp white slices of lotus root pulled from muddy ponds—symbolizing the Buddhist mind that roots in the mud of suffering yet blossoms unstained in the air—are blanched with a splash of plum extract (*maesil-cheong*). Next, she glazes them in a cast-iron pan with five-year-old soy sauce, grain syrup (*jocheong*, slow-boiled from malted barley and rice), perilla oil (*deulgireum*), and a decoction of crimson **Omija** (오미자, *Schisandra chinensis*, the 'five-flavor berry' that simultaneously embodies sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and pungent notes) and purple *baeknyeoncho* (prickly pear cactus fruit). That mastery was never achieved overnight; it emerged from years of grueling trial, error, and collective perseverance at Baekyangsa Temple in Jangseong. Whenever an early prototype or seasonal cycle threatened to derail Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine), the masters refined their approach to Oshinchae (오신채 the five forbidden pungent roots) and recalibrated Baru Gongyang (바루공양 four-bowl monastic communal meal ritual), passing hard-won empirical lessons down from mentor to apprentice.

Similarly, for **pyogo-beoseot jorim** (braised oak-log shiitake mushrooms), dried shiitake caps harvested from dead oak logs in the Jangseong forest are rehydrated in cold spring water—releasing guanylate nucleotides ($5'\text{-GMP}$) that synergize with the glutamate in aged *doenjang* to multiply perceived savory umami on the human tongue by a factor of eight! Across the four seasons, the forest surrounding Baekyangsa serves as the temple’s living pantry. In early spring, as snow melts off Naejangsan, the nuns forage bitter wild shoots—*dureup* (angelica tree shoots), *chwinamul*, *gondre* (Korean thistle), *deodeok* (bonnet bellflower root), and *doraji* (balloon flower root)—whose natural alkaloids cleanse the winter sluggishness from the liver. To experience Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) at the height of its historical operation was to be immersed in a multisensory choreography of sound, texture, and light. To preserve tender spring leaves and lotus roots through the monsoon and winter, Korean nuns perfected the craft of **Bugak** (부각): coating fresh camellia leaves, perilla leaves, kelp (*dasima*), bamboo shoots, or chili peppers in a whisper-thin paste of glutinous rice flour, drying them in the mountain wind and sun until crisp as parchment, and flash-frying them for three seconds in cold-pressed perilla oil. Yet history repeatedly tested the survival of Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine) through the crucible of war, regime change, and rapid modernization. Even temple **Kimchi** (*Sachal Gimchi*) is a revelation of clean microbiology. Instead of salted shrimp or garlic, the nuns build their fermentation paste around a rich broth simmered from dried kelp (*dasima*), shiitake mushroom stems, aged *doenjang* or clear *cheongjang* soy sauce, grated Korean pear, persimmon puree, pine nuts, and minced ginger (*saenggang*, which warms the stomach without clouding the mind and is therefore permitted!). In traditional monastic life, eating is never separated from spiritual practice; it is performed through the silent, choreographed ritual of **Baru Gongyang** (바루공양, 'Monastic Four-Bowl Meal'). Each monk or nun unfolds a cloth wrapping to arrange four nested wooden lacquer bowls (*baru*, carved from zelkova wood and coated in antibacterial *ottchil* lacquer): the largest bowl for rice, the second for soup (*guk*), the third for side dishes (*banchan*), and the fourth for pure water (*cheongsu*). Before lifting chopsticks, the assembly chants the **Five Contemplations** (*O-gwan-gye*, 오관게): 'First, I reflect on the countless labors and natural forces that brought this food before me; second, I examine whether my own virtue and practice deserve this offering; third, I guard my mind against greed and attachment; fourth, I receive this food as medicine to prevent the wasting of the body; fifth, I accept this nourishment solely to accomplish the Way of Enlightenment.' Today at Baekyangsa Temple in Jangseong, a new generation of practitioners and cultural stewards continues to breathe fresh vitality into Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식 Korean Buddhist Temple Cuisine). Under the strict zero-waste rule of *Baru Gongyang*, every single grain of rice and drop of seasoning must be consumed. At the conclusion of the meal, warm water (*sungnyung*) is poured into the largest bowl, swished from bowl to bowl with a single leftover slice of pickled radish (*danmuji*) to wipe every lacquer surface spotless, and drunk down—followed by rinsing with clear cold water that is inspected for a single floating speck of chili oil before being returned to the soil. 1. Neuro-Gastronomy & Umami Synergy: Replacing Alliums with GMP and Glutamate

From a food chemistry standpoint, Korean temple cuisine achieves remarkable savory depth without animal protein or the sulfur compounds of garlic and onions (allicin) by pairing two synergistic plant molecules: free L-glutamate (abundant in multi-year fermented soybean ganjang, doenjang, and kelp/dasima) and 5'-guanylate (5'-GMP, concentrated in sun-dried oak shiitake mushrooms and lotus roots). When glutamate and guanylate bind simultaneously to the human T1R1/T1R3 umami taste receptor, the neurological flavor signal increases up to eightfold. 2. Zero-Waste Ecology: The Ethics of 'Baru Gongyang' and the Five Contemplations

For over a millennium, Korean Seon monasteries have practiced absolute zero-food-waste dining through Baru Gongyang (바루공양). By taking only what one's body requires as 'medicine to sustain practice' and wiping all four nested ottchil-lacquered wooden bowls clean with a single slice of radish and warm water that is then drunk, the monastic community honors the sun, rain, soil microbes, and farmer labor embedded in every grain of rice. Remember three foundational terms: first, Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식), Korean Buddhist temple cuisine designated a National Intangible Cultural Heritage item; second, Oshinchae (오신채), the five pungent roots (garlic, onion, scallion, chive, wild leek) excluded to maintain mental clarity during meditation; and third, Bugak (부각), the temple preservation method of coating seasonal leaves and kelp in glutinous rice paste and sun-drying them.
