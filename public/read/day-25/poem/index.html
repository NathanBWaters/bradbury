Easter, 1916

W. B. Yeats (1916) • 6 min read

Shocked by the British execution of the leaders of the Dublin Easter Rising—people he had greeted casually on the street—Yeats confronts the birth of a terrible beauty.

Key Context & Why It Matters

On Easter Monday, April 24, 1916, a small group of Irish rebels seized buildings in Dublin and declared an Irish republic. The Rising lasted about six days and was crushed by British troops. At first many Dubliners were angry at the rebels. Then the British shot the leaders by firing squad over the following weeks, and public feeling turned sharply. W. B. Yeats, already Ireland's most famous poet, knew several of these men personally. He was in England when the Rising began, and he finished this poem that September. It is his attempt to work out how people he had dismissed as fools and dreamers could become something he could not name. The poem is important because it is a great political poem that refuses easy politics. It does not cheer and it does not condemn. Its meaning is carried by one refrain, that everything has changed, and that what has been born is both dreadful and beautiful.

I have met them at close of day

Coming with vivid faces

From counter or desk among grey

Eighteenth-century houses.

I have passed with a nod of the head

Or polite meaningless words,

Or have lingered awhile and said

Polite meaningless words,

And thought before I had done

Of a mocking tale or a gibe

To please a companion

Around the fire at the club,

Being certain that they and I

But lived where motley is worn:

All changed, changed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

That woman’s days were spent

In ignorant good will,

Her nights in argument

Until her voice grew shrill.

What voice more sweet than hers

When young and beautiful,

She rode to harriers?

This man had kept a school

And rode our winged horse;

This other his helper and friend

Was coming into his force;

He might have won fame in the end,

So sensitive his nature seemed,

So daring and sweet his thought.

This other man I had dreamed

A drunken, vain-glorious lout.

He had done most bitter wrong

To some who are near my heart,

Yet I number him in the song;

He, too, has resigned his part

In the casual comedy;

He, too, has been changed in his turn,

Transformed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

Hearts with one purpose alone

Through summer and winter seem

Enchanted to a stone

To trouble the living stream.

The horse that comes from the road,

The rider, the birds that range

From cloud to tumbling cloud,

Minute by minute they change;

A shadow of cloud on the stream

Changes minute by minute;

A horse-hoof slides on the brim,

And a horse plashes within it

Where long-legged moor-hens dive,

And hens to moor-cocks call.

Minute by minute they live:

The stone’s in the midst of all.

Too long a sacrifice

Can make a stone of the heart.

O when may it suffice?

That is heaven’s part, our part

To murmur name upon name,

As a mother names her child

When sleep at last has come

On limbs that had run wild.

What is it but nightfall?

No, no, not night but death;

Was it needless death after all?

For England may keep faith

For all that is done and said.

We know their dream; enough

To know they dreamed and are dead;

And what if excess of love

Bewildered them till they died?

I write it out in a verse—

MacDonagh and MacBride

And Connolly and Pearse

Now and in time to be,

Wherever green is worn,

Are changed, changed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem tells a story of a poet's mistaken judgment. Yeats begins by remembering how he used to meet these people on the streets of Dublin at the end of the day, "coming with vivid faces from counter or desk among grey eighteenth-century houses." They were clerks, teachers, shopkeepers. He would nod, exchange "polite meaningless words," and then, he admits, think of a clever joke about them to tell friends at his club. He confesses to a kind of snobbery. He was "certain that they and I but lived where motley is worn," meaning that everyone, himself included, was a costumed clown in a trivial comedy.

Then the refrain breaks in: "All changed, changed utterly: / A terrible beauty is born." The phrase "terrible beauty" is the heart of the poem. Terrible here carries two meanings at once. It means causing terror, and it means awe-inspiring. The Rising was both horrifying, because of the violence and the killing, and magnificent, because ordinary people gave up everything for an idea.

In the second stanza, Yeats paints brief portraits of individuals, though he does not name most of them. "That woman" whose days were spent "in ignorant good will, her nights in argument until her voice grew shrill" is Constance Markievicz, who had been a beautiful young woman riding with hounds near Sligo. "This man had kept a school and rode our winged horse" is Patrick Pearse, a teacher and poet, the winged horse being Pegasus, the horse of poetry. "This other, his helper and friend" is Thomas MacDonagh, whose gifts, Yeats says, might have won him fame. And then there is the surprise: "This other man I had dreamed a drunken, vain-glorious lout." That is John MacBride, who had done "most bitter wrong to some who are near my heart," a reference to his failed marriage with Maud Gonne, the woman Yeats loved for decades. Yet Yeats numbers him in the song. Even a man he despised has been "transformed utterly."

The third stanza shifts to imagery. Hearts devoted to "one purpose alone" are "enchanted to a stone / To trouble the living stream." The stone is fixed and unchanging, while everything else in the stream, the horse, the rider, the birds, the clouds, "minute by minute they change." So the rebels have become something hard and permanent in a world of flux. That is both their greatness and their danger.

The fourth stanza turns to a kind of gentle, uneasy prayer. "Too long a sacrifice can make a stone of the heart." Yeats asks, "O when may it suffice?" and leaves the answer to heaven. His own job is to "murmur name upon name, as a mother names her child." Then he asks the hardest question: "Was it needless death after all?" Britain, he notes, "may keep faith" and promise Irish self-government. But he stops himself. "We know their dream; enough to know they dreamed and are dead." Finally, he names four of them, MacDonagh, MacBride, Connolly, and Pearse, and the refrain rings one last time. The poem does not decide whether the sacrifice was right. It insists only that something irreversible has happened.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As a public poem, this is as good as the form gets, and the first thing to admire is its control of tone. Writing about a recent tragedy, in which real people had just been shot, a poet faces enormous temptations: to bluster, to grieve theatrically, to take sides. Yeats resists all three. The poem moves from self-mockery to wonder to doubt, and it keeps its dignity by being honest about its own uncertainty. The question "Was it needless death after all?" is placed right in the middle of a poem of commemoration, which is a daring thing to do.

The refrain is the poem's masterstroke. It appears at the end of each of the first, second, and fourth stanzas, with small variations, and each time it means something slightly different. First it announces the change. Then it names the individuals who have changed. Finally it extends the change to every place where green is worn, a reference to the Irish national color. The repetition gives the poem the ritual force of an incantation. The oxymoron, terrible beauty, is one of the most memorable phrases in the language, because it captures a feeling most people have had but few can name, the sense that something can be dreadful and magnificent at once.

The third stanza, with its stone and stream, is a different kind of achievement. It moves away from people and into a quick, shimmering landscape of birds, a horse, a shadow of cloud, moor-hens diving. The short lines, the rapid repetition of "minute by minute," enact motion and change, and the stone in the middle of it is fixed and silent. It is an extraordinary stretch of verse, partly because it is not obviously about politics at all. It is a natural picture that quietly stands in for a political idea.

The short lines of the whole poem, mostly four beats or fewer, give it a plain, speech-like clarity. Yeats had moved far from his early dreamy manner, and here the language is direct and conversational. "I have met them at close of day" could be the start of a story told to a friend.

But there are weaknesses. The second stanza's portraits are uneven. The sketch of Markievicz, with its undertone of mild reproach, can feel a bit condescending, since Yeats is rather hard on a woman who had given up a privileged life. The poem's moral stance is sometimes unclear, which is deliberate but occasionally frustrating. Critics have also felt that the fourth stanza, with its mother naming a sleeping child, is a touch sentimental, sweet in a way the harder stanzas are not.

More fundamentally, the poem keeps the reader at a distance from the actual violence. We never see the firing squad or the burning streets. The Rising is mostly reflected through Yeats's own changing opinion. That is a legitimate choice, since it is a poem about perception, but it makes the poem as much about the poet as about the dead.

III. The Impact It Had

The poem's impact on Ireland, and on how Irish history has been remembered, has been enormous. It was written in September 1916 and circulated privately, printed in a small private edition of twenty-five copies, and it was not widely published until 1920, when it appeared in the New Statesman and then in The Dial. Even so, its phrases entered the national memory. "A terrible beauty is born" is one of those lines, like a proverb, that people use without knowing where it comes from.

It also shaped how the Easter Rising has been understood. For a century, Irish and international writers have drawn on the poem to describe the Rising and the transformation of Irish opinion after the executions. Historians have often noted that the executions of the leaders turned a failed, unpopular rebellion into the foundation of a movement that led to the Irish War of Independence and, in 1922, the Irish Free State. Yeats's poem captures that transformation almost in real time. Whenever the Rising has been commemorated, as in the centenary in 2016, the poem has been read aloud.

For Yeats's career, it was a pivotal moment. It showed he could write public poetry of great seriousness, and it led to a series of political poems that include Sixteen Dead Men, The Rose Tree, Meditations in Time of Civil War, and Nineteen Hundred and Nineteen. These poems made him not only the laureate of an Irish dream but also its tough-minded critic. The Nobel Prize he received in 1923 recognized this widening of scope.

Its influence on other poets has been deep. Poets writing about political violence have taken it as a model for how to be committed and honest at once. Seamus Heaney, writing in the shadow of the Troubles in Northern Ireland, and many other poets of that conflict, faced the same problem Yeats did: how to respond to violence done in the name of a cause. The poem has been called the template for the modern political poem, in which doubt is part of the truth. W. H. Auden's famous remark that poetry makes nothing happen, from his elegy for Yeats, can be read partly as a response to the question this poem raises.

The poem is also taught widely, in Irish schools and in universities, where it is an essential text. It is frequently set in examinations in Ireland, and it is one of the best-known poems in the national canon.

Finally, the phrase "terrible beauty" now describes wars, storms, and revolutions far beyond the poem.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this poem, above all, for its honesty about changing one's mind. Yeats does not pretend that he understood these people. He admits he mocked them, and he shows himself being forced to look again. There is something very appealing in a great poet confessing that he got people wrong. It makes the poem's final reverence feel earned rather than automatic.

They love its refusal to simplify. The poem does not tell you the Rising was right, or wrong. It holds both thoughts. Readers who have lived through political turmoil, where people are often expected to pick a side and cheer, find a rare relief in a poem that allows complexity. Many Irish readers have said that the poem expressed their own mixed feelings, pride and sorrow together, better than any speech.

They love the music of the refrain. Even readers who have never studied the Rising can feel the force of those last two lines of the first stanza. The sound of "changed, changed utterly" followed by the quiet, awed "a terrible beauty is born" is deeply memorable, and many people can recite it. It works especially well read aloud, where the repetition becomes a kind of chant.

They love the humanity of the portraits. By describing the rebels as clerks and teachers who had once seemed trivial, Yeats makes them real. They are not statues or saints. They have faults, shrill voices, and drunken habits, and he includes them anyway. The inclusion of MacBride, whom he disliked, is often singled out as an act of generosity and honesty at once. It is one of the moments readers most often point to as proof of the poem's greatness.

They love the third stanza, with its stone and living stream. Many people who find political poetry dry are moved by this passage, which is simply beautiful, an image of change in nature that doubles as a way of thinking about commitment. It is often cited as one of Yeats's finest pieces of writing.

Critics and fellow writers have been warm. The poem is regularly described as one of the greatest political poems in English, and is routinely placed among Yeats's best work. Irish readers, in particular, treat it almost as a national possession. Its closing roll call of names, spoken like a litany, is what moves many people most, since it turns grief into ceremony. For a poem about a violent event, it has an unusual power to console.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The poem has attracted a great deal of argument, much of it about politics. It helps to remember that the argument began with Yeats himself, who was uneasy about the Rising even as he honored it.

The earliest and most obvious objection is that the poem is ambivalent, and that ambivalence can be read as either wisdom or evasion. Some nationalists felt that Yeats, an Anglo-Irish Protestant writer who lived partly in England, was holding the rebels at arm's length and judging them from above. The opening stanza, in which he jokes about them at his club, shows the social distance. Meanwhile, some on the other side felt the poem gave too much glory to people who had used violence. There is a long-running argument about whether it romanticizes a bloody uprising.

That second objection grew louder during the Troubles, the decades of violence in Northern Ireland from the late 1960s. Some critics, including the Irish writer and politician Conor Cruise O'Brien, argued that Yeats's poetry of sacrifice and blood helped feed a dangerous tradition of glorifying martyrdom for the nation. Others, in turn, defended Yeats, noting that the poem itself asks, "Was it needless death after all?" and warns that a long sacrifice can turn the heart to stone. So the poem has been used in debates about whether literature can inspire violence. It seems fair to say the poem contains the evidence for both sides.

There is also a gender critique. The portrait of Constance Markievicz is slightly mocking, with its "ignorant good will" and "her voice grew shrill," and some readers feel that Yeats treats a woman who risked her life with a patronizing tone that he does not apply to the men. Because her death sentence was commuted, she is not in the closing list of names, and her absence has sometimes been noted. The poem also says little about the many women who took part in the Rising.

Another complaint is that the poem is oddly detached from the actual events. Yeats was in England when the Rising began, and the poem focuses on a few individuals rather than on the fighting, the civilians killed, or the wreckage of the city. Critics have pointed out that it cleans up the reality of the violence into a symbol.

Finally, readers have disagreed about the ending. The names are given in the poem as a kind of honor roll, but the doubt expressed earlier, "What is it but nightfall?," is never fully resolved. Some find that honest, and some find it unsatisfying. The poem asks the question whether the sacrifice was worth it, and then leaves it hanging, which for some readers is a failure of nerve and for others its greatest strength.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Easter, 1916 belongs to two traditions at once: modernist poetry and the long tradition of political and occasional verse. An occasional poem is one written for a specific event, such as a coronation, a battle, or a death, and for centuries these were mostly flattering and forgettable. What Yeats does is to take that traditional genre and make it truthful and hard. He turns a poem about a public event into a drama of private conscience.

Among its ancestors are the great public poems of earlier centuries, including Andrew Marvell's Horatian Ode upon Cromwell's Return from Ireland, which also praises and doubts a violent political actor at the same time. Marvell's ode, with its famous admiration for Charles I's dignity at the scaffold, is a close relative in its refusal to take a simple side. Wordsworth's sonnets on political themes and Shelley's poems on Peterloo are other forebears, though they are much more openly partisan.

Looking forward, its descendants are many. Poets writing in the shadow of war and conflict have turned to it as a model. Auden's elegy for Yeats, and Heaney's work on the Troubles, belong in its line. So does the tradition of what has been called poetry of witness, in which a writer attempts to record and judge a historical atrocity without simple propaganda. Poets from Eastern Europe, such as Czeslaw Milosz, who wrote about political violence in the twentieth century, faced the same difficulty of honesty in an age of causes.

Within Yeats's own work, it serves as a hinge. After it, the dreamy Celtic twilight poet of the 1890s was gone, and the author of the great late poems was in view. Compare it with The Lake Isle of Innisfree, written when Yeats was young, and you see how far he traveled: from a private longing for peace to a public confrontation with history. Compare it also with Sailing to Byzantium, where the speaker wants to leave history behind for the timelessness of art, and you see that the poem's refusal to escape is one of its distinctions.

Why does it still matter? Because we are still living in an age of political violence and passionate causes, and we still need to know how to think about them. This poem models a rare habit of mind. It admires without surrendering its judgment, mourns without turning the dead into slogans, and admits it does not have the final answer.

Read it, finally, as a lesson in being surprised. Yeats set out to remember people he had written off, and he discovered that he had been wrong. A poem that begins with a nod in the street and ends with a roll call of the dead is one of the best arguments in literature for taking ordinary people seriously.
