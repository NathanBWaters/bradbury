Bradbury 1000 • Night 25 • Nightly Poem

Easter, 1916

W. B. Yeats (1916) • 6 min read

Shocked by the British execution of the leaders of the Dublin Easter Rising—people he had greeted casually on the street—Yeats confronts the birth of a terrible beauty.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Completed on September 25, 1916 (and privately printed before appearing in The Dial in 1920), 'Easter, 1916' was Yeats’s anguished reckoning with the April 24, 1916 Easter Rising in Dublin and the subsequent British firing-squad executions of sixteen Irish republicans, including Constance Markievicz (whose sentence was commuted), Patrick Pearse, Thomas MacDonagh, and John MacBride. Belonging to the High Modernism / Irish Revolution tradition and categorized under War & Witness Poetry, 'Easter, 1916' (1916) unfolds across 80 verbatim lines in 4 stanzas. Widely regarded as the greatest public political elegy in modern English, 'Easter, 1916' refuses both propaganda and cynicism, honoring the named dead ('MacDonagh and MacBride / And Connolly and Pearse') while questioning whether their death was needless ('For England may keep faith').

I have met them at close of day

Coming with vivid faces

From counter or desk among grey

Eighteenth-century houses.

I have passed with a nod of the head

Or polite meaningless words,

Or have lingered awhile and said

Polite meaningless words,

And thought before I had done

Of a mocking tale or a gibe

To please a companion

Around the fire at the club,

Being certain that they and I

But lived where motley is worn:

All changed, changed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

That woman’s days were spent

In ignorant good will,

Her nights in argument

Until her voice grew shrill.

What voice more sweet than hers

When young and beautiful,

She rode to harriers?

This man had kept a school

And rode our winged horse;

This other his helper and friend

Was coming into his force;

He might have won fame in the end,

So sensitive his nature seemed,

So daring and sweet his thought.

This other man I had dreamed

A drunken, vain-glorious lout.

He had done most bitter wrong

To some who are near my heart,

Yet I number him in the song;

He, too, has resigned his part

In the casual comedy;

He, too, has been changed in his turn,

Transformed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

Hearts with one purpose alone

Through summer and winter seem

Enchanted to a stone

To trouble the living stream.

The horse that comes from the road,

The rider, the birds that range

From cloud to tumbling cloud,

Minute by minute they change;

A shadow of cloud on the stream

Changes minute by minute;

A horse-hoof slides on the brim,

And a horse plashes within it

Where long-legged moor-hens dive,

And hens to moor-cocks call.

Minute by minute they live:

The stone’s in the midst of all.

Too long a sacrifice

Can make a stone of the heart.

O when may it suffice?

That is heaven’s part, our part

To murmur name upon name,

As a mother names her child

When sleep at last has come

On limbs that had run wild.

What is it but nightfall?

No, no, not night but death;

Was it needless death after all?

For England may keep faith

For all that is done and said.

We know their dream; enough

To know they dreamed and are dead;

And what if excess of love

Bewildered them till they died?

I write it out in a verse—

MacDonagh and MacBride

And Connolly and Pearse

Now and in time to be,

Wherever green is worn,

Are changed, changed utterly:

A terrible beauty is born.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Completed on September 25, 1916 (and privately printed before appearing in The Dial in 1920), 'Easter, 1916' was Yeats’s anguished reckoning with the April 24, 1916 Easter Rising in Dublin and the subsequent British firing-squad executions of sixteen Irish republicans, including Constance Markievicz (whose sentence was commuted), Patrick Pearse, Thomas MacDonagh, and John MacBride. Within the broader trajectory of High Modernism / Irish Revolution, W. B. Yeats composed 'Easter, 1916' (1916) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"I have met them at close of day"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, W. B. Yeats grounded these 80 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Easter, 1916' lies the alchemical and tragic transformation of ordinary, flawed individuals—once dismissed with 'polite meaningless words' at club or counter in a 'casual comedy'—into historical martyrs, alongside Yeats’s deeply honest ambivalence that 'Too long a sacrifice / Can make a stone of the heart' buried inside the living stream of nature. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Hearts with one purpose alone"—W. B. Yeats develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"A terrible beauty is born."—the central metaphor has transformed the speaker’s initial perception, leaving the reader with an earned emotional equilibrium that refuses easy sentimentality.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, W. B. Yeats constructs 'Easter, 1916' using four stanzas whose very numerology encodes the historical date of the uprising—stanzas of 16, 24, 16, and 24 lines (4 stanzas = April, the 4th month; 24 = the 24th day; 16 = the year 1916)—written in taut iambic trimeter that tolls like a funeral bell onto the paradoxical refrain 'All changed, changed utterly: / A terrible beauty is born'. Spanning 80 lines across 4 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how W. B. Yeats balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "I have met them at close of day" and "A terrible beauty is born." to reverberate in the ear long.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Widely regarded as the greatest public political elegy in modern English, 'Easter, 1916' refuses both propaganda and cynicism, honoring the named dead ('MacDonagh and MacBride / And Connolly and Pearse') while questioning whether their death was needless ('For England may keep faith'). Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Easter, 1916' has remained a touchstone of the War & Witness Poetry canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to W. B. Yeats’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
