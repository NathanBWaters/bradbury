John Reber's 1940s Dam Plan vs. the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Bay Model (1957)

San Francisco — How a retired theatrical producer nearly convinced Congress to dam San Francisco Bay into two freshwater lakes—until a two-acre working hydraulic simulator proved it would destroy the estuary (Postwar Megaproject Debate (1946–1963)) • 9 min read

“Without the physical Bay Model in 1957, we would have dammed the North and South Bays into stagnant evaporating ponds before anyone knew how estuarine tides actually mix.” — Brig. Gen. William F. Cassidy (1961)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In the late 1940s and early 1950s, a self-taught former amateur theater producer named John Reber won the backing of powerful California senators, military officers, and agricultural lobbyists for the 'Reber Plan'—a colossal scheme to build two earthen rock dams up to four miles wide across San Francisco Bay, turning eighty-five percent of the tidal estuary into two giant freshwater lakes while filling 20,000 acres of mudflats for runways and factories. Before authorizing the project, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers built the two-acre working hydraulic Bay Model inside a former World War II Marinship warehouse on the bayfront in 1957, whose scaled tides proved the Reber dams would evaporate more water than they saved and killed the plan forever.

Look out across the sparkling blue waters of San Francisco Bay from Telegraph Hill or the Embarcadero, and it is nearly impossible to believe that in 1949 a joint committee of the United States Senate came within a hair's breadth of walling off almost the entire bay with two giant earthen dams and turning it into two stagnant freshwater reservoirs. The author of this astonishing proposal was not a hydraulic engineer or a hydrologist, but John Reber (1888–1960)—an enthusiastic, silver-tongued former schoolteacher and amateur theatrical producer who spent thirty years drawing maps of what he called the 'San Francisco Bay Project: The Reber Plan.'

Under Reber's blueprint, workers would blast rock out of the East Bay hills to build two massive saltwater barrier dams: a northern dam stretching four miles from Castro Point in Richmond to Point San Quentin in Marin, and a massive southern causeway—2,000 feet wide and four miles long—running from south of the Bay Bridge in San Francisco to Oakland. Linking the two dams along the East Bay waterfront would be a dredged ship canal fitted with concrete navigation locks like the Panama Canal; behind those barriers, the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers would flush the salt water out of San Pablo Bay and the South Bay, transforming eighty-five percent of San Francisco Bay into two vast freshwater lakes while carrying thirty-two lanes of highway traffic and transcontinental trains across the top of the southern dam.

In the booming, techno-optimistic climate of post-World War II California, the Reber Plan caught fire: the San Francisco Chronicle, publisher William Randolph Hearst, farm-water lobbyists in the Central Valley, and mayors across the region endorsed the plan, arguing that every cubic foot of Sacramento River water flowing out the Golden Gate was 'wasted to the sea.' When U.S. Senator Sheridan Downey of California convened Senate Public Works subcommittee hearings in San Francisco in December 1949 to advance the Reber Plan, skeptics raised urgent scientific questions that nobody in 1949 had the mathematical tools to answer: what would happen to the tidal currents at the Golden Gate Bar, the flushing of municipal sewage, the migration of Chinook salmon, and the summer evaporation rate across 280,000 acres of shallow freshwater?

Because electronic digital computers in the early 1950s were still vacuum-tube machines utterly incapable of solving the coupled three-dimensional Navier-Stokes fluid equations for a complex estuary extending from the Pacific Ocean to Sacramento, Congress included a mandate in the Flood Control Act of 1950 directing the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to conduct a comprehensive physical engineering investigation. Modeled on their Waterways Experiment Station in Vicksburg, Mississippi, the San Francisco District of the Army Corps of Engineers moved into a sprawling two-acre warehouse at 2100 Bridgeway on the Richardson Bay waterfront—originally built in 1942 as the outfitting warehouse for the Marinship Liberty Ship and tanker yard—and between 1956 and June 1957 constructed the San Francisco Bay-Delta Model.

To fit seventeen thousand square miles of watershed and the entire bay-delta system from the Pacific Ocean to Sacramento and Stockton inside a building the size of two football fields, hydraulic engineers carved 286 five-ton concrete slabs to a deliberately distorted geometric scale: a horizontal scale of 1:1,000 (one foot equals 1,000 feet) and a vertical depth scale of 1:100 (one foot equals 100 feet). Why distort the vertical scale ten times steeper than the horizontal scale? Under Froude-number fluid similarity laws, if you built a model of shallow San Francisco Bay at a true 1:1,000 vertical scale, the water in the South Bay would be only two millimeters deep—a film so thin that surface tension and laminar viscosity would prevent turbulent tidal eddies from forming at all.

To compensate for the steeper 1:100 vertical slopes and recreate the exact bottom friction of real bay mud, oyster beds, and rocky shoals, technicians embedded more than 250,000 tiny vertical copper strips into the concrete floor of the model, bending individual strips up or down by hand until the model's miniature tides matched decades of historical NOAA tide-gauge and current-meter records across the real bay. Driven by giant precision pumps that mixed salt and fresh water to replicate the exact density contrast between dense 33-parts-per-thousand Pacific Ocean seawater and light Sacramento River runoff, the Bay Model compressed time by a factor of 100: an entire twenty-four-hour-and-fifty-minute lunar tidal day unfolded inside the warehouse in exactly 14.9 minutes.

When Army Corps engineers placed scaled solid barriers across the Bay Model to test the Reber Plan between 1957 and 1961, the physical results were devastating to Reber's dream: first, summer wind and solar radiation over the vast, shallow freshwater lakes evaporated more water into the atmosphere than the entire Sacramento River inflow could replenish during dry months, turning the barriers into net water losers. Second, the dyed tracer fluids and salinity probes revealed that cutting off the massive 'tidal prism'—the billions of gallons of water that slosh in and out of the North and South Bays twice a day—starved the outgoing ebb tide of the scouring velocity needed to flush the sandbar outside the Golden Gate, while trapping industrial and municipal pollutants inside stagnant dead zones.

When the Army Corps of Engineers published its definitive 1963 Technical Report showing that the Reber Plan was hydrologically, economically, and ecologically catastrophic—and three courageously visionary Berkeley women, Kay Kerr, Sylvia McLaughlin, and Esther Gulick, founded the Save San Francisco Bay Association in 1961 to halt municipal landfilling—the dream of damming the bay died forever. Over the next four decades, the working hydraulic Bay Model tested oil-spill dispersion trajectories, shipping channel deepening, and salt-marsh restorations before transitioning into a beloved public science education center where visitors can still walk across the concrete bridges and watch a full day of Golden Gate tides surge and ebb every fifteen minutes.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Froude Scaling (1:1000 Horizontal, 1:100 Vertical) and 250,000 Copper Strips

How do you build an accurate working physical computer out of flowing water and concrete? By matching the dimensionless Froude number (the Froude number), which governs gravity-driven tidal waves, the Army Corps set the horizontal scale at 1:1000 and the vertical depth scale at 1:100, resulting in a velocity scale of 1:10 and a time scale of 1:100 (14.9 minutes per lunar day). Over 250,000 hand-tuned copper strips protruding from the concrete floor calibrate the Manning roughness friction of every channel.

2. Why the Reber Plan Failed: Evaporation Loss and the Tidal Prism

John Reber assumed that damming San Francisco Bay would conserve millions of acre-feet of freshwater. The Bay Model proved the exact opposite: because San Pablo Bay and the South Bay are broad and shallow, converting them into impounded lakes created a huge surface-area-to-volume ratio where summer winds evaporated more water than the dams saved. Worse, eliminating 85 percent of the bay's tidal prism crippled the natural ebb-tide jet that keeps the Golden Gate shipping channel scoured clear of ocean sandbars.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this chapter of bay history with three concepts: first, the Reber Plan (1940s–1950s), the near-miss proposal to replace San Francisco Bay with two earthen dams and a freshwater canal; second, the 'Salt Wedge,' where dense, cold Pacific Ocean saltwater slides along the bottom of the Golden Gate beneath lighter outgoing Sacramento River freshwater; and third, the 1961 founding of Save The Bay by Kay Kerr, Sylvia McLaughlin, and Esther Gulick, which led to the 1965 McAteer-Petris Act protecting the shoreline.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take the Golden Gate Ferry from the San Francisco Ferry Building across the Golden Gate strait to Sausalito (or walk/bike across the Golden Gate Bridge) and follow Bridgeway north to the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Bay Model Visitor Center at 2100 Bridgeway. Walk along the elevated viewing catwalks spanning the 1.5-acre concrete miniature of San Francisco Bay and inspect the historic Reber Plan exhibits and the copper friction strips in the channels.
