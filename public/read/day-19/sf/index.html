A. Page Brown's 1898 Ferry Building Clock Tower and the Tearing Down of the Embarcadero Freeway

San Francisco — How a 245-foot Beaux-Arts campanile built on five thousand Oregon pine piles survived 1906, endured thirty-two years behind a double-deck concrete freeway, and reclaimed the bay (Golden Age of Ferries (1898–1937) to Post-1989 Waterfront Renaissance) • 8 min read

“Until the bridges opened in 1936 and 1937, fifty million passengers a year stepped off the ferryboats beneath A. Page Brown’s clock tower; it was the front door of the American West.” — Kevin Starr (2002)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Standing at the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero, the 660-foot-long San Francisco Ferry Building and its 245-foot Clock Tower—modeled by twenty-nine-year-old Beaux-Arts architect A. Page Brown after the twelfth-century Giralda bell tower in Seville—opened in July 1898 as the second-busiest transit terminal on earth after London's Charing Cross Station. Anchored into deep bay mud by 5,000 seventy-five-foot Oregon pine pilings encapsulated in concrete arches, the Colusa sandstone terminal survived the 1906 earthquake intact, only to be cut off from the city in 1959 when highway engineers erected the double-deck concrete Embarcadero Freeway directly across its face—a blight finally removed after the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake triggered a triumphant waterfront renaissance.

Before the opening of the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge in November 1936 and the Golden Gate Bridge in May 1937, the only way to enter San Francisco from the transcontinental railroad terminus in Oakland or the valleys of Marin was by water, funneling up to fifty million passengers a year—and 250,000 daily commuters—through the slips at the foot of Market Street. In 1892, when California voters approved a $600,000 state bond to replace the cramped 1875 wooden ferry shed, the Board of State Harbor Commissioners awarded the commission to twenty-nine-year-old New York architect A. Page Brown—a protégé of McKim, Mead & White who had recently moved to San Francisco and hired young Bernard Maybeck and Willis Polk in his drafting room.

The first challenge confronting Harbor Commission Chief Engineer Howard C. Holmes was geotechnical: the foot of Market Street lay 1,800 feet east of the original 1849 shoreline of Yerba Buena Cove, floating over ninety feet of fluid, unconsolidated 'Young Bay Mud' that trembled whenever a steam ferry bumped a dolphin piling. Between 1893 and 1895, Holmes engineered the largest sub-aqueous foundation ever attempted on the Pacific Coast: pile-driving barges hammered more than 5,000 whole Oregon Douglas fir tree trunks—each eighty feet long—in clusters into the stiff Pleistocene clay layer deep below the mudline, capping them below low-tide level with an eleven-foot-thick reinforced-concrete groin-vaulted raft.

Atop this submerged forest of timber and concrete, A. Page Brown erected a 660-foot-long, two-story Beaux-Arts arcade clad in warm gray-buff sandstone quarried from the Sites beds of Colusa County, California, spanned inside by a soaring 45-foot-high sunlit Grand Nave inspired by the nineteenth-century glass-and-iron train sheds of Paris and Milan. Rising from the exact center of the terminal aligned with the diagonal axis of Market Street stood a 245-foot square clock tower modeled directly on the twelfth-century Moorish-Renaissance Giralda campanile of Seville Cathedral, housing a massive mechanical Seth Thomas clock whose four twenty-two-foot dials featured three-foot-tall Roman numerals illuminated at night.

Tragically, A. Page Brown never saw his masterpiece completed: in October 1895, while driving a runaway horse carriage in Burlingame, he broke his spine and died in January 1896 at age thirty-six, leaving young architect Edward R. Swain to oversee the terminal's grand opening on July 13, 1898. Even as the three-day firestorm consumed every block of Market Street up to the west facade of the terminal, Navy fireboats and harbor tugs pumped bay water onto the Ferry Building's Colusa sandstone arches, keeping the slips open so that over 300,000 burned-out refugees could escape by ferry across the bay to Oakland and Alameda.

During the 1920s peak of bay ferry transit, when 170 paddlewheel and screw-propeller ferries of the Southern Pacific, Key System, and Northwestern Pacific docked every ten minutes beneath the Grand Nave, the upstairs gallery displayed a 600-foot-long relief map of California before the 1936–1937 bridges slashed annual ferry ridership by ninety percent almost overnight. In the 1950s, at the zenith of mid-century auto-centric planning, the California Division of Highways converted the grand 660-foot second-floor nave into three cramped floors of acoustic-tile state offices with fluorescent drop ceilings and—in February 1959—opened the double-deck concrete Embarcadero Freeway (Interstate 480) directly in front of the Ferry Building.

For thirty-two years, the seventy-foot-tall concrete viaduct of the Embarcadero Freeway cast a dark, noisy shadow over the foot of Market Street, slicing off the Clock Tower's lower arches from the city skyline and turning the waterfront below into a grim parking lot under concrete pillars. Then, at 5:04 p.m. on Tuesday, October 17, 1989, during Game 3 of the Bay Bridge World Series at Candlestick Park, the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta earthquake struck sixty miles south in the Santa Cruz Mountains, sending shear waves that severely cracked the reinforced-concrete support columns of the Embarcadero Freeway on its soft bay-mud foundations.

Seizing the historic moment over fierce opposition from motorists and some merchants who feared losing direct freeway ramps, Mayor Art Agnos and waterfront urbanists convinced the Board of Supervisors in 1990 to tear down the crippled Embarcadero Freeway permanently rather than spend $70 million retrofitting an eyesore. When demolition crews brought down the final concrete spans of the freeway in February 1991, sunlight flooded the foot of Market Street for the first time since 1959, revealing A. Page Brown's Colusa sandstone arches and sparking the conversion of the waterfront into a palm-lined boulevard served by vintage historic streetcars on the F-Market & Wharves line.

Following a $100 million historic restoration completed in March 2003 by SMWM, Baldauf Catton Von Eckartsberg, and Page & Turnbull—which stripped out the 1950s office floors to reopen the 660-foot skylights of the Grand Nave, restored the nineteenth-century mosaic tile floor depicting the Great Seal of California, and created a world-renowned artisan food hall and Saturday farmers market—the Ferry Building once again reigns as the civic hearth of San Francisco Bay.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Five Thousand Pine Trees in Anaerobic Mud: The 1895 Foundation Engineering

How has a massive stone-and-steel building stood at the edge of soft bay fill through both the 1906 and 1989 earthquakes? Chief Engineer Howard C. Holmes understood two principles of marine geotechnics: first, Oregon Douglas fir tree trunks driven completely below the permanent water table into oxygen-free bay mud never rot because wood-eating fungi cannot survive without oxygen; second, by clustering 5,000 eighty-foot piles beneath an eleven-foot-thick grid of concrete arches, the Ferry Building acts as a rigid floating pier tied into deep Pleistocene clays.

2. The 1989 Loma Prieta Quake and the Death of the Urban Freeway

Opened in 1959 before San Francisco's citizens halted further highway construction in the 1966 Freeway Revolt, the double-deck Embarcadero Freeway (I-480) severed downtown from its bayfront for three decades. When the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake damaged the viaduct's concrete joints and Mayor Art Agnos championed its 1991 demolition in favor of a surface boulevard with historic streetcars and a 25-foot bayside promenade, San Francisco created the global model for urban freeway removal later emulated in Seoul's Cheonggyecheon and Seattle's Alaskan Way.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this story with three architectural details: first, the Seville Giralda inspiration of A. Page Brown's 245-foot Clock Tower and its mechanical Seth Thomas clock (whose four 22-foot dials stopped at 5:16 a.m. on April 18, 1906); second, Colusa County sandstone, the warm buff sedimentary stone from Sites, California, that clads the 660-foot facade; and third, the restored 45-foot-high second-floor Grand Nave with its steel Fink trusses and skylight monitor.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Stand at the intersection of Market Street and the Embarcadero in Harry Bridges Plaza on a Saturday morning during the Ferry Plaza Farmers Market to take in the full 660-foot Colusa sandstone arcade unhindered by the vanished freeway. Step inside the central portal, look up through the open mezzanine to the steel roof trusses of the Grand Nave and the tiled Great Seal of California on the floor, and walk out the eastern bayside doors to watch commuter ferries arriving from Sausalito, Larkspur, and Oakland.
