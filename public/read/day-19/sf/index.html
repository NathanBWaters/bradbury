A. Page Brown's 1898 Ferry Building Clock Tower and the Tearing Down of the Embarcadero Freeway

San Francisco — How a 245-foot Beaux-Arts campanile built on five thousand Oregon pine piles survived 1906, endured thirty-two years behind a double-deck concrete freeway, and reclaimed the bay (Golden Age of Ferries (1898–1937) to Post-1989 Waterfront Renaissance) • 20 min read

“Until the bridges opened in 1936 and 1937, fifty million passengers a year stepped off the ferryboats beneath A. Page Brown’s clock tower; it was the front door of the American West.” — Kevin Starr (2002)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Standing at the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero (37.7955° N, 122.3937° W), the 660-foot-long San Francisco Ferry Building and its 245-foot Clock Tower—modeled by twenty-nine-year-old Beaux-Arts architect A. Page Brown after the twelfth-century Giralda bell tower in Seville—opened in July 1898 as the second-busiest transit terminal on earth after London's Charing Cross Station. Anchored into deep bay mud by 5,000 seventy-five-foot Oregon pine pilings encapsulated in concrete arches, the Colusa sandstone terminal survived the 1906 earthquake intact, only to be cut off from the city in 1959 when highway engineers erected the double-deck concrete Embarcadero Freeway directly across its face—a blight finally removed after the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake triggered a triumphant waterfront renaissance.

Standing today at the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower in the heart of the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East (37.7955° N, 122.3937° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Golden Age of Ferries (1898–1937) to Post-1989 Waterfront Renaissance. Before the opening of the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge in November 1936 and the Golden Gate Bridge in May 1937, the only way to enter San Francisco from the transcontinental railroad terminus in Oakland or the valleys of Marin was by water, funneling up to fifty million passengers a year—and 250,000 daily commuters—through the slips at the foot of Market Street. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how architect A. Page Brown, chief wharf engineer Howard C. Holmes, and Mayor Art Agnos and a generation of San Franciscans confronted anchoring a 660-foot stone transit palace atop soft tidal bay mud in 1898 and liberating the waterfront from a double-deck concrete freeway after 1989, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East. In 1892, when California voters approved a $600,000 state bond to replace the cramped 1875 wooden ferry shed, the Board of State Harbor Commissioners awarded the commission to twenty-nine-year-old New York architect A. Page Brown—a protégé of McKim, Mead & White who had recently moved to San Francisco and hired young Bernard Maybeck and Willis Polk in his drafting room. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during July 13, 1898 (opening), April 18, 1906 (quake survival), and October 17, 1989–1991 (Embarcadero Freeway demolition). The first challenge confronting Harbor Commission Chief Engineer Howard C. Holmes was geotechnical: the foot of Market Street lay 1,800 feet east of the original 1849 shoreline of Yerba Buena Cove, floating over ninety feet of fluid, unconsolidated 'Young Bay Mud' that trembled whenever a steam ferry bumped a dolphin piling. Contemporary observers who walked past the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood architect A. Page Brown, chief wharf engineer Howard C. Holmes, and Mayor Art Agnos, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Between 1893 and 1895, Holmes engineered the largest sub-aqueous foundation ever attempted on the Pacific Coast: pile-driving barges hammered more than 5,000 whole Oregon Douglas fir tree trunks—each eighty feet long—in clusters into the stiff Pleistocene clay layer deep below the mudline, capping them below low-tide level with an eleven-foot-thick reinforced-concrete groin-vaulted raft. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave. Atop this submerged forest of timber and concrete, A. Page Brown erected a 660-foot-long, two-story Beaux-Arts arcade clad in warm gray-buff sandstone quarried from the Sites beds of Colusa County, California, spanned inside by a soaring 45-foot-high sunlit Grand Nave inspired by the nineteenth-century glass-and-iron train sheds of Paris and Milan. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through July 13, 1898 (opening), April 18, 1906 (quake survival), and October 17, 1989–1991 (Embarcadero Freeway demolition), the surrounding streets of the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. Rising from the exact center of the terminal aligned with the diagonal axis of Market Street stood a 245-foot square clock tower modeled directly on the twelfth-century Moorish-Renaissance Giralda campanile of Seville Cathedral, housing a massive mechanical Seth Thomas clock whose four twenty-two-foot dials featured three-foot-tall Roman numerals illuminated at night. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Tragically, A. Page Brown never saw his masterpiece completed: in October 1895, while driving a runaway horse carriage in Burlingame, he broke his spine and died in January 1896 at age thirty-six, leaving young architect Edward R. Swain to oversee the terminal's grand opening on July 13, 1898. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave toward private speculation—forcing architect A. Page Brown, chief wharf engineer Howard C. Holmes, and Mayor Art Agnos and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower. Less than eight years later, at 5:12 a.m. on April 18, 1906, the Great Earthquake violently liquefied the filled streets immediately west of the waterfront, buckling cable-car tracks along East Street (the Embarcadero) by six feet; yet Holmes's 5,000-piling concrete foundation and riveted steel frame held the Ferry Building intact while the hands of the Seth Thomas clock froze at 5:16 a.m. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave, the day-to-day craft of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East. Even as the three-day firestorm consumed every block of Market Street up to the west facade of the terminal, Navy fireboats and harbor tugs pumped bay water onto the Ferry Building's Colusa sandstone arches, keeping the slips open so that over 300,000 burned-out refugees could escape by ferry across the bay to Oakland and Alameda. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower. During the 1920s peak of bay ferry transit, when 170 paddlewheel and screw-propeller ferries of the Southern Pacific, Key System, and Northwestern Pacific docked every ten minutes beneath the Grand Nave, the upstairs gallery displayed a 600-foot-long relief map of California before the 1936–1937 bridges slashed annual ferry ridership by ninety percent almost overnight. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in July 13, 1898 (opening), April 18, 1906 (quake survival), and October 17, 1989–1991 (Embarcadero Freeway demolition), the entire city turned its eyes toward the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave in the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East. In the 1950s, at the zenith of mid-century auto-centric planning, the California Division of Highways converted the grand 660-foot second-floor nave into three cramped floors of acoustic-tile state offices with fluorescent drop ceilings and—in February 1959—opened the double-deck concrete Embarcadero Freeway (Interstate 480) directly in front of the Ferry Building. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed July 13, 1898 (opening), April 18, 1906 (quake survival), and October 17, 1989–1991 (Embarcadero Freeway demolition), shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave. For thirty-two years, the seventy-foot-tall concrete viaduct of the Embarcadero Freeway cast a dark, noisy shadow over the foot of Market Street, slicing off the Clock Tower's lower arches from the city skyline and turning the waterfront below into a grim parking lot under concrete pillars. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation. Then, at 5:04 p.m. on Tuesday, October 17, 1989, during Game 3 of the Bay Bridge World Series at Candlestick Park, the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta earthquake struck sixty miles south in the Santa Cruz Mountains, sending shear waves that severely cracked the reinforced-concrete support columns of the Embarcadero Freeway on its soft bay-mud foundations. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of architect A. Page Brown, chief wharf engineer Howard C. Holmes, and Mayor Art Agnos, discovering anew how deeply its design was tuned to the unique rhythms of the San Francisco peninsula.

By the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, a new generation of preservationists, scientists, and community organizers recognized that the 1898 San Francisco Ferry Building and Grand Nave was more than a relic of Golden Age of Ferries (1898–1937) to Post-1989 Waterfront Renaissance; it was a living ecological and cultural keystone. Seizing the historic moment over fierce opposition from motorists and some merchants who feared losing direct freeway ramps, Mayor Art Agnos and waterfront urbanists convinced the Board of Supervisors in 1990 to tear down the crippled Embarcadero Freeway permanently rather than spend $70 million retrofitting an eyesore. Through painstaking archival recovery, structural stabilization, and public stewardship, the site at the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower was safeguarded so that future generations could experience its lessons firsthand.

From the weathered textures of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation to the sightlines stretching toward the bay or the surrounding hills, the physical landscape reads like an open manuscript in which every chapter of Golden Age of Ferries (1898–1937) to Post-1989 Waterfront Renaissance remains legible.

Ultimately, the story of A. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Five Thousand Pine Trees in Anaerobic Mud: The 1895 Foundation Engineering

How has a massive stone-and-steel building stood at the edge of soft bay fill through both the 1906 and 1989 earthquakes? Chief Engineer Howard C. Holmes understood two principles of marine geotechnics: first, Oregon Douglas fir tree trunks driven completely below the permanent water table into oxygen-free bay mud never rot because wood-eating fungi cannot survive without oxygen; second, by clustering 5,000 eighty-foot piles beneath an eleven-foot-thick grid of concrete arches, the Ferry Building acts as a rigid floating pier tied into deep Pleistocene clays. At the foot of Market Street along the Embarcadero beneath the 245-foot Ferry Building Clock Tower in the Embarcadero Waterfront and Financial District East, this synthesis of sub-aqueous timber-pile cluster foundations, Colusa sandstone steel framing, and post-freeway boulevard reclamation and civic memory remains visible at every turn. 2. The 1989 Loma Prieta Quake and the Death of the Urban Freeway

Opened in 1959 before San Francisco's citizens halted further highway construction in the 1966 Freeway Revolt, the double-deck Embarcadero Freeway (I-480) severed downtown from its bayfront for three decades. When the October 17, 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake damaged the viaduct's concrete joints and Mayor Art Agnos championed its 1991 demolition in favor of a surface boulevard with historic streetcars and a 25-foot bayside promenade, San Francisco created the global model for urban freeway removal later emulated in Seoul's Cheonggyecheon and Seattle's Alaskan Way.
