On the Negative Spirit

G. K. Chesterton (1905) • 15 min read

The human brain is a machine for coming to conclusions; if it cannot come to conclusions it is rusty.

Key Context & Why It Matters

In 1905 a thirty-one-year-old journalist named G. K. Chesterton published Heretics, a book that took on the leading thinkers of his day one at a time and argued with them like a friend arguing across a dinner table. This is the second chapter, and it is where he states his main complaint. Chesterton thought his age was wonderful at saying what is wrong and hopeless at saying what is right. Reformers talked about liberty, progress and education, but never said what people should be free for, progress toward, or educated into. To make the point, he compares a medieval monk, who at least dreams of perfect health, with the modern moralist, who only knows how to point at disease. His main example is the Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen, and he also takes a fond swipe at his friend H. G. Wells. The essay is funny, but it has a serious edge. It asks a question that still stings: what do you actually believe is good, and why?

Much has been said, and said truly, of the monkish morbidity, of the hysteria which has often gone with the visions of hermits or nuns. But let us never forget that this visionary religion is, in one sense, necessarily more wholesome than our modern and reasonable morality. It is more wholesome for this reason, that it can contemplate the idea of success or triumph in the hopeless fight towards the ethical ideal, in what Stevenson called, with his usual startling felicity, “the lost fight of virtue.” A modern morality, on the other hand, can only point with absolute conviction to the horrors that follow breaches of law; its only certainty is a certainty of ill. It can only point to imperfection. It has no perfection to point to. But the monk meditating upon Christ or Buddha has in his mind an image of perfect health, a thing of clear colours and clean air. He may contemplate this ideal wholeness and happiness far more than he ought; he may contemplate it to the neglect of exclusion of essential things; he may contemplate it until he has become a dreamer or a driveller; but still it is wholeness and happiness that he is contemplating. He may even go mad; but he is going mad for the love of sanity. But the modern student of ethics, even if he remains sane, remains sane from an insane dread of insanity.

The anchorite rolling on the stones in a frenzy of submission is a healthier person fundamentally than many a sober man in a silk hat who is walking down Cheapside. For many such are good only through a withering knowledge of evil. I am not at this moment claiming for the devotee anything more than this primary advantage, that though he may be making himself personally weak and miserable, he is still fixing his thoughts largely on gigantic strength and happiness, on a strength that has no limits, and a happiness that has no end. Doubtless there are other objections which can be urged without unreason against the influence of gods and visions in morality, whether in the cell or street. But this advantage the mystic morality must always have⁠—it is always jollier. A young man may keep himself from vice by continually thinking of disease. He may keep himself from it also by continually thinking of the Virgin Mary. There may be question about which method is the more reasonable, or even about which is the more efficient. But surely there can be no question about which is the more wholesome.

I remember a pamphlet by that able and sincere secularist, Mr. G. W. Foote, which contained a phrase sharply symbolizing and dividing these two methods. The pamphlet was called Beer and Bible, those two very noble things, all the nobler for a conjunction which Mr. Foote, in his stern old Puritan way, seemed to think sardonic, but which I confess to thinking appropriate and charming. I have not the work by me, but I remember that Mr. Foote dismissed very contemptuously any attempts to deal with the problem of strong drink by religious offices or intercessions, and said that a picture of a drunkard’s liver would be more efficacious in the matter of temperance than any prayer or praise. In that picturesque expression, it seems to me, is perfectly embodied the incurable morbidity of modern ethics. In that temple the lights are low, the crowds kneel, the solemn anthems are uplifted. But that upon the altar to which all men kneel is no longer the perfect flesh, the body and substance of the perfect man; it is still flesh, but it is diseased. It is the drunkard’s liver of the New Testament that is marred for us, which we take in remembrance of him.

Now, it is this great gap in modern ethics, the absence of vivid pictures of purity and spiritual triumph, which lies at the back of the real objection felt by so many sane men to the realistic literature of the nineteenth century. If any ordinary man ever said that he was horrified by the subjects discussed in Ibsen or Maupassant, or by the plain language in which they are spoken of, that ordinary man was lying. The average conversation of average men throughout the whole of modern civilization in every class or trade is such as Zola would never dream of printing. Nor is the habit of writing thus of these things a new habit. On the contrary, it is the Victorian prudery and silence which is new still, though it is already dying. The tradition of calling a spade a spade starts very early in our literature and comes down very late. But the truth is that the ordinary honest man, whatever vague account he may have given of his feelings, was not either disgusted or even annoyed at the candour of the moderns. What disgusted him, and very justly, was not the presence of a clear realism, but the absence of a clear idealism. Strong and genuine religious sentiment has never had any objection to realism; on the contrary, religion was the realistic thing, the brutal thing, the thing that called names. This is the great difference between some recent developments of Nonconformity and the great Puritanism of the seventeenth century. It was the whole point of the Puritans that they cared nothing for decency. Modern Nonconformist newspapers distinguish themselves by suppressing precisely those nouns and adjectives which the founders of Nonconformity distinguished themselves by flinging at kings and queens. But if it was a chief claim of religion that it spoke plainly about evil, it was the chief claim of all that it spoke plainly about good. The thing which is resented, and, as I think, rightly resented, in that great modern literature of which Ibsen is typical, is that while the eye that can perceive what are the wrong things increases in an uncanny and devouring clarity, the eye which sees what things are right is growing mistier and mistier every moment, till it goes almost blind with doubt. If we compare, let us say, the morality of the Divine Comedy with the morality of Ibsen’s Ghosts, we shall see all that modern ethics have really done. No one, I imagine, will accuse the author of the Inferno of an Early Victorian prudishness or a Podsnapian optimism. But Dante describes three moral instruments⁠—Heaven, Purgatory, and Hell, the vision of perfection, the vision of improvement, and the vision of failure. Ibsen has only one⁠—Hell. It is often said, and with perfect truth, that no one could read a play like Ghosts and remain indifferent to the necessity of an ethical self-command. That is quite true, and the same is to be said of the most monstrous and material descriptions of the eternal fire. It is quite certain the realists like Zola do in one sense promote morality⁠—they promote it in the sense in which the hangman promotes it, in the sense in which the devil promotes it. But they only affect that small minority which will accept any virtue of courage. Most healthy people dismiss these moral dangers as they dismiss the possibility of bombs or microbes. Modern realists are indeed Terrorists, like the dynamiters; and they fail just as much in their effort to create a thrill. Both realists and dynamiters are well-meaning people engaged in the task, so obviously ultimately hopeless, of using science to promote morality.

I do not wish the reader to confuse me for a moment with those vague persons who imagine that Ibsen is what they call a pessimist. There are plenty of wholesome people in Ibsen, plenty of good people, plenty of happy people, plenty of examples of men acting wisely and things ending well. That is not my meaning. My meaning is that Ibsen has throughout, and does not disguise, a certain vagueness and a changing attitude as well as a doubting attitude towards what is really wisdom and virtue in this life⁠—a vagueness which contrasts very remarkably with the decisiveness with which he pounces on something which he perceives to be a root of evil, some convention, some deception, some ignorance. We know that the hero of Ghosts is mad, and we know why he is mad. We do also know that Dr. Stockman is sane; but we do not know why he is sane. Ibsen does not profess to know how virtue and happiness are brought about, in the sense that he professes to know how our modern sexual tragedies are brought about. Falsehood works ruin in The Pillars of Society, but truth works equal ruin in The Wild Duck. There are no cardinal virtues of Ibsenism. There is no ideal man of Ibsen. All this is not only admitted, but vaunted in the most valuable and thoughtful of all the eulogies upon Ibsen, Mr. Bernard Shaw’s “Quintessence of Ibsenism.” Mr. Shaw sums up Ibsen’s teaching in the phrase, “The golden rule is that there is no golden rule.” In his eyes this absence of an enduring and positive ideal, this absence of a permanent key to virtue, is the one great Ibsen merit. I am not discussing now with any fullness whether this is so or not. All I venture to point out, with an increased firmness, is that this omission, good or bad, does leave us face to face with the problem of a human consciousness filled with very definite images of evil, and with no definite image of good. To us light must be henceforward the dark thing⁠—the thing of which we cannot speak. To us, as to Milton’s devils in Pandemonium, it is darkness that is visible. The human race, according to religion, fell once, and in falling gained knowledge of good and of evil. Now we have fallen a second time, and only the knowledge of evil remains to us.

A great silent collapse, an enormous unspoken disappointment, has in our time fallen on our Northern civilization. All previous ages have sweated and been crucified in an attempt to realize what is really the right life, what was really the good man. A definite part of the modern world has come beyond question to the conclusion that there is no answer to these questions, that the most that we can do is to set up a few notice-boards at places of obvious danger, to warn men, for instance, against drinking themselves to death, or ignoring the mere existence of their neighbours. Ibsen is the first to return from the baffled hunt to bring us the tidings of great failure.

Every one of the popular modern phrases and ideals is a dodge in order to shirk the problem of what is good. We are fond of talking about “liberty”; that, as we talk of it, is a dodge to avoid discussing what is good. We are fond of talking about “progress”; that is a dodge to avoid discussing what is good. We are fond of talking about “education”; that is a dodge to avoid discussing what is good. The modern man says, “Let us leave all these arbitrary standards and embrace liberty.” This is, logically rendered, “Let us not decide what is good, but let it be considered good not to decide it.” He says, “Away with your old moral formulae; I am for progress.” This, logically stated, means, “Let us not settle what is good; but let us settle whether we are getting more of it.” He says, “Neither in religion nor morality, my friend, lie the hopes of the race, but in education.” This, clearly expressed, means, “We cannot decide what is good, but let us give it to our children.”

Mr. H. G. Wells, that exceedingly clear-sighted man, has pointed out in a recent work that this has happened in connection with economic questions. The old economists, he says, made generalizations, and they were (in Mr. Wells’s view) mostly wrong. But the new economists, he says, seem to have lost the power of making any generalizations at all. And they cover this incapacity with a general claim to be, in specific cases, regarded as “experts,” a claim “proper enough in a hairdresser or a fashionable physician, but indecent in a philosopher or a man of science.” But in spite of the refreshing rationality with which Mr. Wells has indicated this, it must also be said that he himself has fallen into the same enormous modern error. In the opening pages of that excellent book Mankind in the Making, he dismisses the ideals of art, religion, abstract morality, and the rest, and says that he is going to consider men in their chief function, the function of parenthood. He is going to discuss life as a “tissue of births.” He is not going to ask what will produce satisfactory saints or satisfactory heroes, but what will produce satisfactory fathers and mothers. The whole is set forward so sensibly that it is a few moments at least before the reader realises that it is another example of unconscious shirking. What is the good of begetting a man until we have settled what is the good of being a man? You are merely handing on to him a problem you dare not settle yourself. It is as if a man were asked, “What is the use of a hammer?” and answered, “To make hammers”; and when asked, “And of those hammers, what is the use?” answered, “To make hammers again.” Just as such a man would be perpetually putting off the question of the ultimate use of carpentry, so Mr. Wells and all the rest of us are by these phrases successfully putting off the question of the ultimate value of the human life.

The case of the general talk of “progress” is, indeed, an extreme one. As enunciated today, “progress” is simply a comparative of which we have not settled the superlative. We meet every ideal of religion, patriotism, beauty, or brute pleasure with the alternative ideal of progress⁠—that is to say, we meet every proposal of getting something that we know about, with an alternative proposal of getting a great deal more of nobody knows what. Progress, properly understood, has, indeed, a most dignified and legitimate meaning. But as used in opposition to precise moral ideals, it is ludicrous. So far from it being the truth that the ideal of progress is to be set against that of ethical or religious finality, the reverse is the truth. Nobody has any business to use the word “progress” unless he has a definite creed and a cast-iron code of morals. Nobody can be progressive without being doctrinal; I might almost say that nobody can be progressive without being infallible⁠—at any rate, without believing in some infallibility. For progress by its very name indicates a direction; and the moment we are in the least doubtful about the direction, we become in the same degree doubtful about the progress. Never perhaps since the beginning of the world has there been an age that had less right to use the word “progress” than we. In the Catholic twelfth century, in the philosophic eighteenth century, the direction may have been a good or a bad one, men may have differed more or less about how far they went, and in what direction, but about the direction they did in the main agree, and consequently they had the genuine sensation of progress. But it is precisely about the direction that we disagree. Whether the future excellence lies in more law or less law, in more liberty or less liberty; whether property will be finally concentrated or finally cut up; whether sexual passion will reach its sanest in an almost virgin intellectualism or in a full animal freedom; whether we should love everybody with Tolstoy, or spare nobody with Nietzsche;⁠—these are the things about which we are actually fighting most. It is not merely true that the age which has settled least what is progress is this “progressive” age. It is, moreover, true that the people who have settled least what is progress are the most “progressive” people in it. The ordinary mass, the men who have never troubled about progress, might be trusted perhaps to progress. The particular individuals who talk about progress would certainly fly to the four winds of heaven when the pistol-shot started the race. I do not, therefore, say that the word “progress” is unmeaning; I say it is unmeaning without the previous definition of a moral doctrine, and that it can only be applied to groups of persons who hold that doctrine in common. Progress is not an illegitimate word, but it is logically evident that it is illegitimate for us. It is a sacred word, a word which could only rightly be used by rigid believers and in the ages of faith.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Chesterton opens with a deliberately shocking claim. Everyone has heard that the visions of hermits and nuns can tip into hysteria, and he grants it. But he says this visionary religion is, in one sense, healthier than our modern, sensible morality. His reason is about what the mind is looking at. The monk meditating on Christ or Buddha has in his head a picture of perfect health, "a thing of clear colours and clean air." He may stare at it too long and become a dreamer or even a driveller, but what he is staring at is wholeness and happiness. As Chesterton puts it, he may go mad, but he is "going mad for the love of sanity." The modern student of ethics, by contrast, may stay sane, but he does so "from an insane dread of insanity." The point is not that monks are right. It is that a person trying to be good by gazing at an ideal is in a different mental place from a person trying to be good by gazing at the wreckage of badness.

He then gives that idea a grotesque and memorable image. A secularist pamphleteer named G. W. Foote had written a pamphlet called Beer and Bible, and argued that a picture of a drunkard's liver would do more for temperance than any prayer. Chesterton says this perfectly captures the incurable morbidity of modern ethics. The altar of the modern temple no longer holds the perfect man, but a diseased body. It is, he says, "the drunkard's liver of the New Testament that is marred for us." It is a joke, a blasphemy and an argument all at once, which is the Chesterton specialty.

From there he turns to literature, and this is where the essay really gets going. Why did so many sane people dislike the realistic writers of the nineteenth century, such as Ibsen and Zola? Chesterton says it was not the frankness. Ordinary men, he says, talk in ways Zola would never print. What bothered them was not the presence of a clear realism but the absence of a clear idealism. Compare Dante, who gives us three moral instruments, Heaven, Purgatory and Hell, with Ibsen, who has only one. In Ibsen's Ghosts we know exactly why the hero is mad. In An Enemy of the People we know Dr. Stockman is sane, but not why. Evil is drawn in uncanny detail while goodness grows "mistier and mistier."

Finally Chesterton widens the lens. Modern slogans such as liberty, progress and education are, he says, dodges to avoid the question of what is good. He translates each one into plain English, and the translations are the heart of the essay. Even his friend Wells, in Mankind in the Making, proposes to study life as a tissue of births, but, Chesterton asks, what is the good of begetting a man until we have settled what is the good of being a man? Hence the title. The negative spirit is a mind that defines itself by what it refuses, and so never has to say what it wants.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The great strength of this essay is the way Chesterton turns an abstract complaint into pictures you cannot forget. The drunkard's liver on the altar is one. The man answering "To make hammers" when asked the use of a hammer is another. His translations of the modern slogans are a small masterclass in comic logic. "Liberty" becomes "Let us not decide what is good, but let it be considered good not to decide it." That sentence does more work than a page of academic argument, and it is hard to read the word liberty in a political speech afterward without hearing it. His treatment of progress is similarly sharp. Progress, he says, is "a comparative of which we have not settled the superlative." A comparative is a word like "better." You cannot say something is getting better until you know what best would be. It is a real philosophical point delivered as a joke, and it holds up.

The prose also has a kind of generosity that makes the argument harder to resist. He calls Wells "that exceedingly clear-sighted man," credits Shaw with the best defense of Ibsen, and does not pretend that monastic hysteria never happened. He loses very little by conceding things.

But it is fair to say the essay is also uneven and, in places, unfair. The portrait of Ibsen is a caricature. Ibsen did not merely prosecute; plays like A Doll's House end with a person walking out of a door toward a self she has not yet met, and Brand is a demanding idealist if ever there was one. To say Ibsen has only Hell is clever, but it flattens him. Chesterton half admits as much when he insists he does not think Ibsen a pessimist, yet then lets the Hell-only line stand as the headline.

The central argument also contains a slippery step. Chesterton says the monk's morality is more wholesome, and he admits there may be a question about which method is more reasonable or more effective. But wholesome is not the same as true. A skeptic could say that a cheerful picture of perfection is comforting precisely because it may be false, and that a drunkard's liver is at least real. Chesterton does not really answer that here. He is arguing about psychology, not truth, and the essay sometimes lets you forget the difference.

There is also a certain amount of sweeping assertion, such as the claim that the twelfth century agreed about the direction of progress, which no medievalist would accept, and the comparison of realists to bomb-throwing anarchists, which is more a flourish than a point. And the essay, by design, never says what the good life actually is. It diagnoses a gap and walks away. That is satisfying as a provocation but incomplete as an argument.

III. The Impact It Had

Taken on its own, "On the Negative Spirit" is not one of the famous pieces in English literature, and it is honest to say that its impact is mostly the impact of the book it sits in. Heretics was a notable success, and it cemented Chesterton's reputation as a public intellectual who could take on Shaw, Wells and Kipling in print without losing their friendship. It also put him on a clear path. A critic had remarked that it was all very well to attack other people's philosophies, but what was Chesterton's own? He answered with Orthodoxy in 1908, which is the positive half of the argument this chapter sets up. The famous story is that Orthodoxy exists because Heretics left a challenge hanging, and this chapter, with its relentless demand that people state what is good, is where the challenge is most pointed. Chesterton went on to become the leading lay Christian apologist in English, and he joined the Roman Catholic Church in 1922.

Beyond Chesterton's own career, the specific idea in this chapter, that talk of progress or freedom is empty without an account of the good, has a long afterlife. It echoes through later critics of modern moral thinking. C. S. Lewis, who credited Chesterton with helping to change his mind about Christianity, argued in The Abolition of Man that a society can lose the ability to speak about real goodness while still demanding it. The philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre, in After Virtue, made a much more technical case that modern moral language is a set of fragments from a lost shared purpose. These are different writers with different methods, and it would be wrong to say Chesterton caused them, but the family resemblance is real.

In ordinary culture the legacy is less a particular quotation than a habit of mind. When someone asks a politician what, exactly, they mean by "progress," or points out that "freedom" is being used without saying freedom to do what, they are doing a very Chestertonian thing, whether or not they know it. The chapter is also a favourite among readers of Chesterton who come to him through his detective stories or his Christian writing, and it is read in societies devoted to his work.

It is not a standard classroom text. It is more often encountered as a chapter in Heretics or in anthologies of Chesterton's essays than as a set piece in literature courses, and there is little in the way of famous adaptation. Its influence is quiet and intellectual rather than loud.

IV. What Readers Loved

People have enjoyed this essay first of all because it is fun. Chesterton wrote with a kind of exuberance that does not wear off. He can describe a man answering "To make hammers" and make you laugh and wince in the same second. Readers who disagree with him completely often say that he is the most enjoyable person to disagree with, because he plays fair about his opponents' virtues and has no malice in him. That tone, argument as a game between friends, is a large part of why he has stayed in print.

Readers have also loved the sharpness of the central diagnosis. Many people have felt, without being able to say it, that public conversation is very good at denouncing and very poor at proposing. Chesterton gave that feeling a shape and a name more than a century ago. The line about the modern mind having "a great silent collapse, an enormous unspoken disappointment" resonates with readers who feel a similar weariness, whatever their politics. The essay is not a complaint that moderns are wicked. It is a charge that they are vague about the very thing they claim to care about most, and readers recognise that charge in themselves.

Writers have admired him too. Jorge Luis Borges was a lifelong enthusiast of Chesterton's essays and stories, and W. H. Auden praised him. C. S. Lewis, as noted, said the book that most helped him was Chesterton's The Everlasting Man. Admirers often say that what they love is how he makes the commonplace look strange: a hammer, a drunkard's liver, a hairdresser.

For religious readers the essay is a joy because it defends the beauty of belief without sneering at doubt. For nonbelieving readers it offers something rarer, which is a challenge made with enough charm that you cannot dismiss it. Even critics who think he is wrong tend to concede that he asks the right question: what are you for? It is a question that applies equally to a monk, a reformer, a scientist or a social media user, and that breadth is part of why people return to the chapter.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The most common criticism is that Chesterton mistakes style for proof. His method is paradox: take a familiar idea, turn it upside down, and let the surprise do the persuading. That works wonderfully on the page, but critics have long said that an epigram is not an argument. The statement that the monk is sane in his madness while the modern moralist is mad in his sanity is delightful, but it is not demonstrated. Readers who admire him sometimes admit that his best lines persuade them before they have checked whether they are true.

A second objection concerns his picture of the past. Chesterton often paints the Middle Ages, or the age of faith, as a time of shared direction and clear ideals, and sets it against a confused modern world. Historians would say that medieval Europe was full of heresy, war and argument about what was good, and that real moral progress, such as the end of slavery or the expansion of voting rights, has happened in modern societies without a single agreed creed. Critics have also noted that his demand for a clear doctrine could be used to justify dogmatism: if only the certain can be progressive, what happens to those who disagree?

A third complaint is aimed at his treatment of his targets. Ibsen's defenders have argued that Chesterton reads him unfairly, as we have seen. Secularists and rationalists of his own day, such as those who published and read Foote, would have said that preferring a picture of a diseased liver to a prayer is not morbid but honest. They would add that a morality based on evidence has no need to apologise for pointing at consequences.

There is also a moral and political shadow over Chesterton's reputation that readers should be aware of. His later writings on Jews, in particular, have been criticised as antisemitic, and scholars and admirers still argue about how serious the problem is. This chapter is not where that appears, but anyone encountering Chesterton should know that his reputation is complicated.

Finally, some readers simply find him exhausting. The constant stream of reversals and flourishes can feel like being talked at by a brilliant person who will not let you finish a sentence. Even sympathetic readers admit that over a whole book the technique can grow wearing, and that the chapter offers more diagnosis than cure.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Chesterton belongs to a particular moment, the Edwardian decade, when English journalism was full of brilliant men arguing in public about everything. Shaw, Wells, Belloc, Kipling and Chesterton himself were all, in different ways, essayists and prophets, and Heretics is a portrait gallery of that debate drawn by one of its participants. Behind them stands a longer tradition of English moral critics, from Thomas Carlyle and John Ruskin to John Henry Newman and Matthew Arnold. Arnold's Culture and Anarchy, written in 1869, also worried that the modern world was full of machinery and short of aims. Chesterton's weapon, the paradox, comes from Oscar Wilde, and there is a nice irony in his using Wilde's own tool against the fashionable skepticism that Wilde's heirs represented.

What descends from it? Most directly, Chesterton's own Orthodoxy and the Christian apologetics of Lewis and others. More broadly, a strain of cultural criticism that suspects modern talk of freedom and progress of being hollow, running through writers as different as MacIntyre and many conservative columnists. It is interesting to set the chapter beside W. B. Yeats's famous lines from "The Second Coming," written in 1919, that "the best lack all conviction, while the worst are full of passionate intensity." That is almost the same diagnosis after the First World War, delivered with tragic force rather than comic energy. Chesterton saw the same thing in 1905 and treated it as a problem of philosophy and, a little, of manners.

It is also a useful companion to the nineteenth-century realists he attacks. Read Ibsen's Ghosts first, and then this chapter, and you can feel what Chesterton is reacting to and where he is being unfair. That is a good way to read it: as one half of a conversation.

Why read it now? Partly for the pleasure of watching an unusually joyful mind work. But also because the negative spirit has not gone away. It is easier than ever to say what is wrong, with a thousand voices to say it, and harder than ever to say what we are for. Whether you end up agreeing with Chesterton's answer or not, his question is one worth being asked, and the chapter asks it in about fifteen minutes of lively, funny, provocative reading.
