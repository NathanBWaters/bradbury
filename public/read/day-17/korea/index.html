Jongmyo Shrine & Jongmyo Jeryeak: The 101-Meter Hall of Silence and Stone Chimes

Korea — Why Frank Lloyd Wright and Kim Swoo-geun revered Seoul's stark horizontal ancestral hall where King Sejong's 1447 bronze bells and L-shaped jade chimes still ring (Joseon Dynasty (1395–Present / UNESCO Heritage, 1995 & 2001)) • 11 min read

“In our ancestors’ achievements there was both civil virtue (Mun) and martial valor (Mu); how could our ritual music praise foreign emperors while remaining silent on our own founders?” — King Sejong the Great, Sejong Sillok (1447)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Established in December 1394 by King Taejo when the Joseon capital moved to Hanyang and expanded across four centuries as successive kings and queens passed away, Jongmyo Shrine in central Seoul is the oldest and most authentic Confucian royal ancestral sanctuary surviving anywhere in East Asia. Its main hall, Jeongjeon, stretches an astonishing 101 meters across a two-tiered rough granite platform (wolda), making it one of the longest single-span traditional wooden buildings on Earth. Stripped of ornamental clutter to create an overwhelming horizontal plane of red pillars, gray slate tiles, and open stone courtyard sky, Jongmyo comes alive every May with Jongmyo Jeryeak—the five-hundred-year-old ritual music and 64-dancer Ilmu choreography composed by King Sejong in 1447 using bronze bells (pyeonjong) and L-shaped Namyang hornfels stone chimes (pyeongyeong).

Walk north from the bustling jewelry stalls and metalwork alleys of Jongno 3-ga in central Seoul, pass through the simple wooden outer gate of Jongmyo, and set your feet onto the central triple-pathed stone walkway (samdo). Immediately, your voice drops to a whisper. The raised central lane of rough-hewn granite slabs is not meant for living human feet—it is the Sin-ro (Spirit Path), reserved for the unseen spirits of deceased Joseon kings and queens, flanked on the right by the King’s Path (Eoro) and on the left by the Crown Prince’s Path (Sejaro). Follow that stone path through dense groves of oak and pine until the trees suddenly part at the southern gate of Jeongjeon (Main Hall, National Treasure No. 227). Even twenty-first-century architects accustomed to glass supertalls gasp at the sight. Stretching before you across a vast, empty courtyard of rough gray granite slabs (bakseok) is a single, unbroken horizontal timber colonnade 101 meters long, supported by forty-eight massive vermilion pine pillars and crowned by a sweeping expanse of dark gray clay roof tiles (giwa) that seems to press the sky and earth together.

Nothing on the facade of Jeongjeon seeks to entertain the eye. Unlike the throne halls of Gyeongbokgung or Changdeokgung Palaces—which dazzle with multi-colored dragon ceiling coffers, carved stone balustrades, and ornamental window lattices—Jongmyo is the architecture of death, memory, and Neo-Confucian solemnity. Its timber eaves use the stark, austere ikgong (wing-like bracket) system; its nineteen spirit chambers (gamshil) have no glass windows, only heavy vertical wooden plank shutters sealed in pitch darkness so the resting souls (hon) of forty-nine kings and queens enshrined on chestnut-wood spirit tablets (sinju) are never disturbed by daylight. When King Taejo completed the original Jeongjeon in October 1395 to the east of Gyeongbokgung Palace—obeying the ancient Zhouli ('Rites of Zhou') urban rule of Jwamyo Usa (: 'Ancestral Shrine on the Left/East of the Palace, Altar of Soil and Grain on the Right/West')—the hall had only seven spirit chambers. In Confucian tradition, only the founder and the four most recent generations of monarchs remained in the main hall, while earlier kings whose reigns had passed beyond four generations were moved to a separate hall (Yeongnyeongjeon, 'Hall of Eternal Peace,' built in 1421).

Joseon, however, introduced a uniquely Korean constitutional exception called Buryeok-jijong ('Ancestors of Unfading Virtue'): any king whose reign had achieved extraordinary moral, military, or scientific service to the nation would never be evicted from the Main Hall. Thus, as monarchs such as King Sejong, King Seongjong, King Seonjo, King Yeongjo, and King Jeongjo joined the pantheon—and after the entire shrine was rebuilt in 1608 following the 1592 Japanese invasion—architects simply extended the eastern and western ends of Jeongjeon bay by bay in 1667, 1726, and 1836 until nineteen contiguous chambers stood beneath a single 101-meter roof.

Notice the rough, uneven texture of the bakseok granite stones paving the 109-by-69-meter elevated courtyard terrace (wolda) in front of the hall. Had the masons polished those stones smooth, midday summer sun would have reflected a blinding glare into the eyes of ritual celebrants, winter ice would have made the terrace treacherous for elderly ministers carrying sacrificial vessels, and monsoon rainwater would have pooled instead of draining into the soil gaps between the stones. Yet architecture is only half of Jongmyo; the other half is acoustic time travel. For more than five hundred years, on the first Sunday of May (and historically five times a year at the seasonal solstices and equinoxes), the silent granite terrace of Jeongjeon transforms into the stage for the Jongmyo Daeje (Royal Ancestral Rite) and its musical score, Jongmyo Jeryeak —inscribed by UNESCO in 2001 on the very first global proclamation of Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

Before 1447, Joseon royal ancestral rites used Chinese ritual music (Aak) imported from the Song Dynasty, praising ancient Chinese sage-emperors rather than Korean history. King Sejong the Great found this absurd and spiritually dishonest. In a famous royal pronouncement recorded in the Sejong Sillok, the king observed: 'When our ancestors lived in the world, they listened to the native music of our own land (Hyangak); why, now that they are spirits, do we play foreign Chinese music they never heard in life?' Working with his brilliant court musicologist Park Yeon (1378–1458), Sejong composed two monumental eleven-piece orchestral suites in 1447: Botaepyeong ('Preserving the Great Peace,' praising the civil and cultural achievements of Joseon’s kings) and Jeongdaeeop ('Founding the Great Dynasty,' celebrating their military valor in defending the nation). In 1464, Sejong’s son King Sejo arranged these two suites specifically for the Jongmyo ancestral ritual and condensed the lyrics to praise each ritual offering of silk and triple-cupped rice wine.

The orchestra of Jongmyo Jeryeak is divided into two antiphonal ensembles representing Heaven (Yang) and Earth (Yin): the Deungga (Upper Terrace Orchestra) seated on the high stone verandah right outside the spirit chambers, and the Heonga (Lower Courtyard Orchestra) stationed on the lower wolda terrace. Leading both orchestras are two extraordinary percussion instruments tuned to the twelve chromatic pitches (sib-i-ryul) by Park Yeon during King Sejong’s reign: the Pyeonjong, a two-tiered wooden frame hung with sixteen cast-bronze bells of identical external dimensions whose wall thickness varies to produce sixteen exact pitches; and the Pyeongyeong, a frame of sixteen L-shaped stone chimes carved from dense, resonant calc-silicate hornfels rock discovered by Park Yeon at Namyang in Gyeonggi Province in 1425.

Why did fifteenth-century Korean acousticians prize stone chimes (gyeongseok) above all other instruments for tuning the royal orchestra? Bronze expands and drops in pitch on hot days, silk strings stretch and go flat in monsoon humidity, and bamboo pipes warp with moisture; but dense Namyang hornfels stone maintains an invariant vibrational frequency regardless of temperature or weather—serving as the immutable acoustic tuning fork of the Joseon state! Each musical movement begins with three booming strokes of the Jingo barrel drum and ends when a musician draws a split bamboo whisk across the twenty-seven wooden serrated teeth down the spine of a crouched wooden tiger called the Eo, producing a dry, rattling scrape that silences the court. Between those signals, the earthy, mournful cry of the Taepyeongso (conical double-reed shawm), the bamboo Daegeum flute, the Haegeum two-string fiddle, and the Ajaeng bowed zither weave native Korean pentatonic melodies into the solemn ritual.

Simultaneously, in the center of the granite terrace, sixty-four dancers arranged in an eight-by-eight square grid (Pal-il-mu) perform the Ilmu (Line Dance). During the first wine offering, when the orchestra plays Botaepyeong, the sixty-four dancers wear crimson robes and hold a bamboo flute (yak) and pheasant-feather plume (jeok) to embody civil wisdom (Munmu); during the second offering, when Jeongdaeeop sounds, they switch to wooden swords (geom) and spears (chang) to perform the martial dance (Mumu). While China’s imperial ancestral rituals and music died out after the fall of the Qing Dynasty in 1911, Korea’s Jeonju Yi Clan Royal Family Association and the musicians of the National Gugak Center (descending directly from the Joseon Jangakwon Royal Music Institute) kept both the ritual vessels (je-gi) and the fifteenth-century mensural musical scores (Jeongganbo, —the world’s oldest mensural notation system indicating exact pitch and rhythmic duration in grid squares, invented by King Sejong) unbroken across six centuries.

Stand on the rough bakseok granite stones of Jongmyo at twilight after the crowd has gone. As the 101-meter red colonnade of Jeongjeon glows in the last slant of western sun and a wind stirs the ancient pines, you can almost still hear the high, clear chime of Park Yeon’s Namyang stone ringing across five hundred years of silence.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Architectural Minimalism: Why Jeongjeon's 101-Meter Horizontal Line Stuns Modernists

Modern architects from Kim Swoo-geun and Seung H-Sang to international visitors have compared Jongmyo's Jeongjeon Hall to the Parthenon in Athens—yet with the opposite spatial geometry. Where the Parthenon rises vertically on an acropolis hill to command the horizon from afar, Jongmyo is hidden inside a low forest depression and stretches 101 meters horizontally across nineteen bays. By eliminating decorative paintwork and facing the south courtyard with an unbroken rhythm of vermilion columns and rough granite bakseok stones, the architecture induces immediate psychological stillness.

2. Acoustic Physics: Park Yeon's 1425 Namyang Stone Chimes & Jeongganbo Notation

Two scientific breakthroughs under King Sejong made the survival of Jongmyo Jeryeak possible. First, in 1425 court musicologist Park Yeon discovered native deposits of dense hornfels chime stone at Namyang, freeing Joseon from importing Chinese chimes and allowing him to calibrate sixteen L-shaped Pyeongyeong stones whose resonant frequencies remain unaffected by humidity. Second, Sejong invented Jeongganbo, a grid-box mensural musical notation that recorded both exact pitch and exact beat duration—allowing modern National Gugak Center musicians to play the 1447 score note-for-note.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Anchor this story with three essential terms: first, Samdo (Threefold Path), the granite walkway whose raised center lane is reserved exclusively for ancestral spirits; second, Botaepyeong and Jeongdaeeop, King Sejong's twin orchestral suites praising civil virtue (Mun) and martial valor (Mu); and third, Ilmu, the geometric 64-dancer ritual choreography performed on the wolda stone terrace.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Enter Jongmyo Shrine at Hunjeong-dong, Jongno-gu, walking beside the raised Sin-ro spirit path through the oak woods to both the 19-chamber Jeongjeon Main Hall and the 16-chamber Yeongnyeongjeon Hall. If you visit on the first Sunday of May, you can witness the full six-hour Jongmyo Daeje ritual live; year-round, you can see and hear the original Pyeonjong and Pyeongyeong instruments at the National Gugak Center in Seocho-gu.
