Ode on Melancholy

John Keats (1819) • 4 min read

Refusing the numbing poisons of oblivion, Keats argues that true melancholy lives inside the very temple of Delight, inseparable from fleeting beauty and joy.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Ode on Melancholy was written by John Keats in the spring of 1819, during the astonishing few months in which he produced most of his greatest poems. He was twenty-three. His younger brother Tom had died of tuberculosis the winter before, with Keats nursing him to the end, and Keats himself was beginning to show signs of the same illness. He was also newly in love with Fanny Brawne and unsure he would ever be able to marry her. Against that background, the poem does something startling. It tells a sad person not to drown their sadness in poison, forgetfulness or death imagery, and instead to feel it fully, among beautiful living things. Its boldest claim is that melancholy and joy are not opposites, because the sweetest things are sweet precisely because they will fade. It was published in 1820, a year before Keats died. As you listen, notice the abrupt opening, the list of dark symbols that he rejects, and the strange, almost religious final stanza.

No, no! go not to Lethe, neither twist

Wolf’s-bane, tight-rooted, for its poisonous wine;

Nor suffer thy pale forehead to be kiss’d

By nightshade, ruby grape of Proserpine;

Make not your rosary of yew-berries,

Nor let the beetle, or the death-moth be

Your mournful Psyche, nor the downy owl

A partner in your sorrow’s mysteries;

For shade to shade will come too drowsily,

And drown the wakeful anguish of the soul.

But when the melancholy fit shall fall

Sudden from heaven like a weeping cloud,

That fosters the droop-headed flowers all,

And hides the green hills in an April shroud;

Then glut thy sorrow on a morning rose,

Or on the rainbow of the salt-sand wave,

Or on the wealth of globed peonies;

Or if thy mistress some rich anger shows,

Emprison her soft hand, and let her rave,

And feed deep, deep upon her peerless eyes.

She dwells with Beauty—Beauty that must die;

And Joy, whose hand is ever at his lips

Bidding adieu; and aching Pleasure nigh,

Turning to poison while the bee-mouth sips:

Aye, in the very temple of Delight

Veil’d Melancholy has her sovran shrine,

Though seen of none save him whose strenuous tongue

Can burst Joy’s grape against his palate fine;

His soul shall taste the sadness of her might,

And be among her cloudy trophies hung.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a piece of advice, and the advice is surprising. Most of us, when we feel low, either try to escape the feeling or sink into it. Keats says, in effect, that both responses are wrong. He starts mid-argument with a shout: no, no, do not go to Lethe. Lethe is the river of forgetfulness in the Greek underworld, whose water wipes out memory. Do not twist wolfsbane, a deadly plant, into poisonous wine. Do not let nightshade kiss your forehead. He calls it the ruby grape of Proserpine, the Roman name for Persephone, queen of the dead. Do not make a rosary of yew berries, since the yew tree is associated with graveyards. Do not let a beetle or a death-moth be your Psyche, the Greek word that means both soul and butterfly. Do not let an owl keep you company.

All of these are the standard props of a gloomy, gothic sadness, the kind that treats grief as a performance or a way out. His reason is exact and strange. If you go that way, he says, shade will come to shade too drowsily, and drown the wakeful anguish of the soul. In other words, the danger of these dark rituals is that they numb you. They put out the very pain that is alive and awake and, in Keats's view, valuable.

The second stanza gives the alternative. When the melancholy fit falls suddenly, like a weeping cloud that waters the drooping flowers and hides the hills in an April shroud, then feed your sorrow on beautiful things: a morning rose, the rainbow on a wave, a bunch of round peonies. Pay attention to the verb. He says glut your sorrow. Fill up on it. The cure for sadness is not to flee it but to attach it to something alive and lovely, so that sorrow and beauty are held together. The lover image at the end of that stanza is more troubling, and we will come back to it.

The third stanza is the heart of the poem. Melancholy, it says, is not a stranger from outside. She lives with Beauty, beauty that must die, and with Joy, whose hand is always at his lips, saying goodbye, and with Pleasure that turns to poison while the bee-mouth sips. Her shrine is inside the temple of Delight itself. Only someone who can burst Joy's grape against his fine palate, who tastes pleasure so intensely and fully that he feels its ending in it, will see her. That person's soul will be hung among her cloudy trophies, like a captured banner.

What the poem is getting at, then, is a tough idea about intensity. The person who feels joy most keenly feels sorrow most keenly, and they are the same capacity. To avoid sadness, you would have to avoid feeling deeply at all. Keats chooses depth, with open eyes.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

There is a lot to admire in this ode, and a few things to question. Start with what is masterful. The opening is one of the boldest in English poetry. By beginning with a double no and a command, Keats throws us into the middle of an argument that seems to have been going on before the poem began. The effect is of overhearing a friend talk someone out of something dangerous. It is dramatic, immediate and unlike the stately openings of many odes. The poem then builds its three stanzas as a clear logical movement: what not to do, what to do instead, and why. That structure is precise without feeling mechanical, and the ten-line stanzas each form a complete thought.

The sensuous writing is extraordinary. Keats can put into a phrase the whole mood of a spring shower: a weeping cloud that fosters the droop-headed flowers and hides the green hills in an April shroud. That one image makes rain feel both sad and nourishing, which is exactly the poem's point, enacted rather than stated. The line about Pleasure turning to poison while the bee-mouth sips is also remarkable, because it shows Pleasure as a living creature drinking from a flower even as the sip becomes bitter.

Now to the weaker or more awkward parts. The first stanza is a catalogue of gloomy symbols, and some readers find it a bit overstuffed. Wolfsbane, nightshade, yew berries, beetle, moth, owl: the list can feel like an inventory of Gothic clichés, and the mixture of mythologies, with Greek Lethe, Roman Proserpine and Psyche, seems to pile one reference onto another. We should also note that this stanza went through revision. Keats's earlier draft had a darker, more elaborate opening with an image of a ship built from dead men's bones, and he cut it before publication. Most agree the cut improved the poem, but it shows how close it came to the very excess it warns against.

The second stanza has an even harder problem. The advice to take hold of a mistress's soft hand when she shows rich anger, let her rave, and feed deep, deep upon her peerless eyes sits uneasily with modern readers. It treats a woman's anger as something to be imprisoned and consumed for the poet's emotional benefit. Whether or not we think of it as ironic, it is more possessive than tender, and it breaks the otherwise gentle tone.

Finally, the last stanza risks a little over-ripeness. The images of grapes bursting against the palate and cloudy trophies are rich, but the personified figure of Melancholy, with her sovereign shrine and trophies of captured souls, is a more theatrical image than the others. The poem succeeds anyway because the central idea is so strong and the language so alive, but it is not perfect in the way To Autumn is, and its strength is its daring rather than its polish.

III. The Impact It Had

Ode on Melancholy appeared in Keats's 1820 volume, Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems, which is the book that finally secured his reputation, even though too late for him to enjoy it. He left for Italy in the autumn of 1820 and died the following February in Rome, aged twenty-five. For decades afterward, readers returned to the volume and especially to the odes, and Ode on Melancholy was one of the poems that made people see him not as a mere maker of pretty verses but as a serious thinker about pain.

Its largest influence lay in the Victorian age and beyond. The idea that melancholy and beauty are intertwined, that sadness is the shadow cast by loveliness, shaped the sensibility of the Pre-Raphaelite painters and poets, such as Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Christina Rossetti, and later writers associated with Aestheticism, who made a cult of beauty, such as Oscar Wilde. Wilde admired Keats enormously and wrote about him with real devotion. The notion in the third stanza that the true appreciator of joy tastes sadness in it echoes through fin-de-siecle writing, in which a languid, sweet sadness became a kind of fashion.

The poem also stands behind a long line of verse that treats grief as something to be inhabited and understood rather than cured. Poets who write about depression and loss in the modern age, with their willingness to look at pain while staying alive to beauty, are working in a territory Keats helped to map. You can see its shadow in the elegiac strand of English poetry running through Tennyson, Hardy and Edward Thomas.

In terms of culture, the poem has had a long life in the language of everyday feeling. The phrase about Joy's hand ever at his lips, bidding adieu, is one of those lines that readers carry to funerals and farewells. Titles and epigraphs have drawn on it. Composers have set parts of it. It is regularly taught in classes on the odes as a companion to the more famous poems on a nightingale and a Grecian urn.

It also influenced our understanding of Keats himself. Scholars use it to show how his ideas about suffering developed. In a letter written the year before, Keats had called the world a vale of Soul-making, meaning that pain is the process by which a soul becomes a self, and the ode can be read as a poem version of that thought. In this sense it changed his own career. After it, Keats's poems become both richer and more pained, moving toward To Autumn, the calm resolution of this struggle, and toward the unfinished Fall of Hyperion, where the question of whether a poet can bear to feel the world's suffering becomes the whole subject.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about Ode on Melancholy? First, many love its honesty about an experience that most of us have and few want to talk about. We are encouraged to cheer up, to move on, to fix our moods. Keats says that a blue mood has its own dignity and that there is something to learn in it. People who have gone through grief often say they find the poem consoling for exactly that reason. It does not tell them to stop feeling. It tells them their feeling is part of being alive to beauty.

Readers also love the imagery of the second stanza, perhaps the most beautiful passage in the poem. The weeping cloud, the morning rose, the rainbow on the salt-sand wave and the wealth of globed peonies are among the most vivid pictures Keats ever made, and they work because they are specific and tangible. Many people find that the poem's advice, to look at something beautiful when you are sad, works in real life. It is practical wisdom hidden inside lyric.

Another source of affection is the poem's paradox. The thought that melancholy dwells in the very temple of Delight is one of those ideas that, once heard, seems both strange and obvious. It captures a feeling we all know, the ache at the height of happiness when we realize it will end, at a wedding, on the last night of a holiday, or watching a child grow. Readers have often said that Keats named a feeling they had never managed to name.

Writers have been warm. Keats's friends, including Charles Brown and Richard Woodhouse, preserved and copied his work, and later poets from Tennyson onward praised his sensuous richness. The Victorian critic Matthew Arnold, though at times he found Keats unfinished, praised the intensity of his imaginative life, and in the twentieth century the critic Helen Vendler wrote a full book on the odes that treats this one with great respect as a poem of argument as well as feeling.

People also love how brief and dense it is. Thirty lines say what a long essay might try to prove. It also rewards rereading. On a first reading the dark list at the start can seem merely spooky, but on later readings the quiet turn of its logic becomes clearer, and the third stanza opens up. Many fans say they return to it at different times of life and find it saying something new: to a young reader a beautiful gloom, to an older one a hard truth about love and loss. That ability to change as we change may be what keeps it alive.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The criticism of Ode on Melancholy is quieter than the controversy around some of Keats's other work, partly because the poem has always been admired and partly because there is less documented contemporary reaction to it specifically. It was published in the 1820 volume, which was reviewed with mixed but increasingly favorable notices, and the reviews tended to discuss the long narrative poems more than the odes. So much of what we can say about objections is about later, retrospective debate.

One longstanding criticism, which we touched on, is the awkwardness of the opening stanza. Some readers feel that the catalogue of poisons and death symbols is overblown and self-indulgent, as though Keats were showing off his reading in Gothic literature while telling us not to do that. It has been pointed out that the poem partly depends on Robert Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy, a huge seventeenth-century book Keats read, and that it adopts its ornate, bookish manner, which can feel remote from real grief.

The second stanza's lover has drawn the most pointed moral objections. Modern critics, especially feminist critics, have noted that the poem treats a woman's anger as raw material for a man's sensation. The mistress is imprisoned, allowed to rave, and consumed through her eyes. It is a typical example of how Romantic poetry could make women into objects of the male gaze, and some say it reveals something uncomfortable about Keats's attitude, which is also visible in some of his letters. Others defend it as a dark joke, a deliberate, almost ironic extremity, or as being about the poet's capacity to find beauty even in conflict. The question remains open.

A different kind of critique concerns the poem's argument. Is it really true that you should feed on sorrow? Some have thought this a kind of aestheticizing of suffering, turning pain into material for the sensitive soul's enjoyment. The ending, in which the delicate-palated person is hung among Melancholy's trophies, can be read as a celebration of suffering as a mark of superior sensibility, a pose that later Romantics and Decadents adopted with less honesty. Critics of the cult of melancholy argue that the poem's glamor can be misleading for those in real depression, for whom pain is not beautiful and a morning rose is not a remedy.

Lastly, a few scholars have asked whether the poem's logic holds together, particularly the move from the second to the third stanza. The change from advice to a mythic personification is abrupt, and the personified Melancholy seems to belong to a different poem. Others see that jump as the whole point. Either way, what most critics now agree on is that these weaknesses are the weaknesses of a poem taking real risks, and that is a better fault than caution.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Ode on Melancholy belongs to the English Romantic movement and, more narrowly, to the group of odes Keats wrote in spring 1819, the other great ones being Ode to Psyche, Ode to a Nightingale, Ode on a Grecian Urn and Ode on Indolence. Together they form one of the supreme sequences in English poetry, each exploring a different way a person might try to escape or transform the pain of being mortal. In the Nightingale ode the poet longs to flee into song, in the Urn ode into timeless art, and here he insists that the way is not out of sadness but deeper into it.

The poem descends from several traditions. One is the old literary habit of writing about melancholy as a mood, an idea that goes back to Renaissance writers such as Robert Burton, whose Anatomy of Melancholy Keats knew well, and to Milton's paired poems Il Penseroso and L'Allegro, which put the two moods side by side. Another is the classical ode, with its formal address and its mythological names. A third is the Romantic cult of feeling, the belief that intense emotion, even painful emotion, is a source of insight.

What descends from it is broad. Its idea that sadness is allied with beauty runs into the Pre-Raphaelites, the Aesthetes and the Symbolists, and in French poetry the sensibility of Baudelaire, with his love of beauty touched by decay, shares something of its temper. In the twentieth century, writers who take grief seriously, such as Rilke in his Duino Elegies, make a similar claim that beauty is the beginning of a terror we can barely bear. The poem is also part of the background of modern writing about depression and mourning, where authors argue about whether suffering should be cured or inhabited.

A useful comparison is with Samuel Johnson's remarks on sorrow, or with Thomas Gray's Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College, both of which meditate on how happiness carries its own end. Gray is moralizing and resigned; Keats is sensuous and defiant. Compare, too, the many cheerful consolation poems of the age that tell the sufferer that all will be well. Keats refuses that kind of comfort. Next to Wordsworth's confident Immortality ode, where the loss of childhood vision is made up by a deeper wisdom, Keats's poem offers no compensation except intensity itself.

Why read it now? Because the idea that you must be cheerful at all costs is still strong, and the poem is a clear, beautiful answer to it. It does not say that sorrow is good. It says that sorrow is real and that a life without it would be a life without depth of love. In thirty lines, it gives you a way to stay awake to your own feelings. For a young poet who would be dead within two years, that is a remarkably brave thing to have written.
