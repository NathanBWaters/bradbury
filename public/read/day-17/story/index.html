The Pendulum

Ray Bradbury (1941) • 8 min read

“Up and down, back and forth, up and down.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Pendulum was published in November 1941 in Super Science Stories, and it holds a special place in Ray Bradbury's life. It was written with Henry Hasse, an older science fiction writer who had helped the young Bradbury, and it was the first story Bradbury ever sold to a professional magazine. He was twenty-one, working as a newspaper seller in Los Angeles, and writing at night. America was months away from entering the Second World War, and pulp magazines like this one were where a generation of future masters learned their craft. The story is a short, hard tale of revenge. An inventor builds a time machine, a terrible accident kills thirty great scientists, and the public punishes him with a cruel and ingenious sentence. It is rough in places, but it already shows the young writer's love of vivid images and rhythm. The overall meaning is a dark joke about time: you cannot escape it, and you may reach the future whether you want to or not.

Up and down, back and forth, up and down. First the quick flight skyward, gradually slowing, reaching the pinnacle of the curve, poising a moment, then flashing earthward again, faster and faster at a nauseating speed, reaching the bottom and hurtling aloft on the opposite side. Up and down. Back and forth. Up and down.

How long it had continued this way Layeville didn’t know. It might have been millions of years he’d spent sitting here in the massive glass pendulum watching the world tip one way and another, up and down, dizzily before his eyes until they ached. Since first they had locked him in the pendulum’s round glass head and set it swinging it had never stopped or changed. Continuous, monotonous movements over and above the ground. So huge was this pendulum that it shadowed one hundred feet or more with every majestic sweep of its gleaming shape, dangling from the metal intestines of the shining machine overhead. It took three or four seconds for it to traverse the one hundred feet one way, three or four seconds to come back.

The prisoner of time! That’s what they called him now! Now, fettered to the very machine he had planned and constructed. A pri⁠—son⁠—er⁠—of⁠—time! A⁠—pris⁠—on⁠—er⁠—of⁠—Time! With every swing of the pendulum it echoed in his thoughts. Forever like this until he went insane. He tried to focus his eyes on the arching hotness of the earth as it swept past beneath him.

They had laughed at him a few days before. Or was it a week? A month? A year? He didn’t know. This ceaseless pitching had filled him with an aching confusion. They had laughed at him when he said, some time before all this, he could bridge time gaps and travel into futurity. He had designed a huge machine to warp space, invited thirty of the world’s most gifted scientists to help him finish his colossal attempt to scratch the future wall of time.

The hour of the accident spun back to him now through misted memory. The display of the time machine to the public. The exact moment when he stood on the platform with the thirty scientists and pulled the main switch! The scientists, all of them, blasted into ashes from wild electrical flames! Before the eyes of two million witnesses who had come to the laboratory or were tuned in by television at home! He had slain the world’s greatest scientists!

He recalled the moment of shocked horror that followed. Something radically wrong had happened to the machine. He, Layeville, the inventor of the machine, had staggered backward, his clothes flaming and eating up about him. No time for explanations. Then he had collapsed in the blackness of pain and numbing defeat.

Swept to a hasty trial, Layeville faced jeering throngs calling out for his death. “Destroy the Time Machine!” they cried. “And destroy this murderer with it!”

Murderer! And he had tried to help humanity. This was his reward.

One man had leaped onto the tribunal platform at the trial, crying, “No! Don’t destroy the machine! I have a better plan! A revenge for this⁠—this man!” His finger pointed at Layeville where the inventor sat unshaven and haggard, his eyes failure glazed. “We shall rebuild his machine, take his precious metals, and put up a monument to his slaughtering! We’ll put him on exhibition for life within his executioning device!” The crowd roared approval like thunder shaking the tribunal hall.

Then, pushing hands, days in prison, months. Finally, led forth into the hot sunshine, he was carried in a small rocket car to the center of the city. The shock of what he saw brought him back to reality. They had rebuilt his machine into a towering timepiece with a pendulum. He stumbled forward, urged on by thrusting hands, listening to the roar of thousands of voices damning him. Into the transparent pendulum head they pushed him and clamped it tight with weldings.

Then they set the pendulum swinging and stood back. Slowly, very slowly, it rocked back and forth, increasing in speed. Layeville had pounded futilely at the glass, screaming. The faces became blurred, were only tearing pink blobs before him.

On and on like this⁠—for how long?

He hadn’t minded it so much at first, that first night. He couldn’t sleep, but it was not uncomfortable. The lights of the city were comets with tails that pelted from right to left like foaming fireworks. But as the night wore on he felt a gnawing in his stomach, that grew worse. He got very sick and vomited. The next day he couldn’t eat anything.

They never stopped the pendulum, not once. Instead of letting him eat quietly, they slid the food down the stem of the pendulum in a special tube, in little round parcels that plunked at his feet. The first time he attempted eating he was unsuccessful, it wouldn’t stay down. In desperation he hammered against the cold glass with his fists until they bled, crying hoarsely, but he heard nothing but his own weak, fear-wracked words muffled in his ears.

After some time had elapsed he got so that he could eat, even sleep while travelling back and forth this way. They allowed him small glass loops on the floor and leather thongs with which he tied himself down at night and slept a soundless slumber without sliding.

People came to look at him. He accustomed his eyes to the swift flight and followed their curiosity-etched faces, first close by in the middle, then far away to the right, middle again, and to the left.

He saw the faces gaping, speaking soundless words, laughing and pointing at the prisoner of time traveling forever nowhere. But after awhile the town people vanished and it was only tourists who came and read the sign that said: This is the prisoner of time⁠—John Layeville⁠—who killed thirty of the world’s finest scientists! The school children, on the electrical moving sidewalk stopped to stare in childish awe. The prisoner of time!

Often he thought of that title. God, but it was ironic, that he should invent a time machine and have it converted into a clock, and that he, in its pendulum, should mete out the years⁠—traveling with Time.

He couldn’t remember how long it had been. The days and nights ran together in his memory. His unshaven cheeks had developed a short beard and then ceased growing. How long a time? How long?

Once a day they sent down a tube after he ate and vacuumed up the cell, disposing of any wastes. Once in a great while they sent him a book, but that was all.

The robots took care of him now. Evidently the humans thought it a waste of time to bother over their prisoner. The robots brought the food, cleaned the pendulum cell, oiled the machinery, worked tirelessly from dawn until the sun crimsoned westward. At this rate it could keep on for centuries.

But one day as Layeville stared at the city and its people in the blur of ascent and descent, he perceived a swarming darkness that extended in the heavens. The city rocket ships that crossed the sky on pillars of scarlet flame darted helplessly, frightenedly for shelter. The people ran like water splashed on tiles, screaming soundlessly. Alien creatures fluttered down, great gelatinous masses of black that sucked out the life of all. They clustered thickly over everything, glistened momentarily upon the pendulum and its body above, over the whirling wheels and roaring bowels of the metal creature once a Time Machine. An hour later they dwindled away over the horizon and never came back. The city was dead.

Up and down, Layeville went on his journey to nowhere, in his prison, a strange smile etched on his lips. In a week or more, he knew, he would be the only man alive on earth.

Elation flamed within him. This was his victory! Where the other men had planned the pendulum as a prison it had been an asylum against annihilation now!

Day after day the robots still came, worked, unabated by the visitation of the black horde. They came every week, brought food, tinkered, checked, oiled, cleaned. Up and down, back and forth⁠—the pendulum!

… a thousand years must have passed before the sky again showed life over the dead Earth. A silvery bullet of space dropped from the clouds, steaming, and hovered over the dead city where now only a few solitary robots performed their tasks. In the gathering dusk the lights of the metropolis glimmered on. Other automatons appeared on the rampways like spiders on twisting webs, scurrying about, checking, oiling, working in their crisp mechanical manner.

And the creatures in the alien projectile found the time mechanism, the pendulum swinging up and down, back and forth, up and down. The robots still cared for it, oiled it, tinkering.

A thousand years this pendulum had swung. Made of glass the round disk at the bottom was, but now when food was lowered by the robots through the tube it lay untouched. Later, when the vacuum tube came down and cleaned out the cell it took that very food with it.

Back and forth⁠—up and down.

The visitors saw something inside the pendulum. Pressed closely to the glass side of the cell was the face of a whitened skull⁠—a skeleton visage that stared out over the city with empty sockets and an enigmatical smile wreathing its lipless teeth.

Back and forth⁠—up and down.

The strangers from the void stopped the pendulum in its course, ceased its swinging and cracked open the glass cell, exposing the skeleton to view. And in the gleaming light of the stars the skull face continued its weird grinning as if it knew that it had conquered something. Had conquered time.

The Prisoner Of Time, Layeville, had indeed travelled along the centuries.

And the journey was at an end.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, The Pendulum is a revenge story with a science fiction costume. John Layeville, a brilliant inventor, builds a machine meant to carry people into the future. He invites thirty of the world's best scientists to help him, and when he pulls the main switch in front of two million witnesses, the machine fails and kills all thirty. The crowd demands his death. Instead, one man in the courtroom proposes a worse punishment: rebuild the machine as a giant clock, seal Layeville inside the glass head of its pendulum, and let it swing him back and forth forever, on public display, with a sign naming him the prisoner of time.

The opening paragraph is the key to the whole piece. Bradbury makes you feel the swing before you understand it. Up and down, back and forth, up and down. The sentences rise and fall like the thing they describe, and the repeated phrase becomes a kind of ticking. Notice how the very first thing we get is sensation, not explanation. The inventor has lost track of how long he has been there, and the reader is made to lose track as well.

The title phrase, prisoner of time, is where the story turns from simple cruelty into irony. The crowd thinks it is punishing him with a pointless motion that goes nowhere. But Layeville did claim that he could travel into the future, and in a twisted way the sentence gives him what he asked for. Each swing carries him through the centuries just as every person is carried through time. When the black alien creatures sweep down and kill everyone in the city, the pendulum cell becomes his shelter. The glass that was meant as a cage protects him from the hunger of the creatures, and the robots go on feeding him. He smiles as he realizes he will be the last man alive. The prison has become a refuge.

The final turn is the skeleton. A thousand years later, visitors from space find a skull pressed to the glass, grinning with empty sockets, still swaying. He has lasted until the end of human life, and his smile suggests that he has beaten time simply by outliving the people who condemned him. This ending is deliberately ambiguous. Did he starve out of despair, or did he die content? The grin lets either reading stand.

A reader might miss two quieter layers. One is that the story is about public shaming. The crowd cheers when he is punished, the school children stop to stare, and the tourists read the sign and move on. People turn a human being into an exhibit. The other is the role of the robots, which keep working long after humans have died. They suggest a world in which routine continues without meaning, a theme Bradbury would revisit in later work. The pendulum is both a punishment and a comment on how mechanical and thoughtless life can become.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Pendulum is a crude story by the standards of Bradbury's mature work, and it is more interesting as an early sign of talent than as a finished piece. Still, it has some real strengths, and it is worth being honest about both.

The best thing in it is the rhythm of the prose. The first paragraph is a small triumph. By repeating up and down and back and forth, and by describing the swing as a quick flight skyward, a slowing, a poise, and a nauseating drop, Bradbury turns the sentence itself into a pendulum. The writing then continues to give physical details that stay in the mind: the city lights becoming comets pelting from right to left, the faces turning into tearing pink blobs, the glass loops on the floor and the leather thongs with which Layeville ties himself down to sleep. These are the touches of a writer who already thinks in images. The single most effective idea is the image of a grinning skull swinging in a glass bulb over a dead city, which is genuinely haunting and which a more polished story would be proud of.

The weaknesses are just as visible. The setup requires a great deal of faith. Thirty of the world's most gifted scientists are blasted into ashes in a single instant, in front of two million people, and a mob puts the inventor on trial almost immediately. There is no sense of how this society works, who the judges are, or why a crowd would be allowed to decide a sentence. The man who leaps up with a better plan is introduced and then vanishes, as pulp characters often do. Layeville himself is not much of a character. He is a collection of reactions, such as horror, self-pity, and later a cold satisfaction, and we never learn what he wanted from his invention beyond a vague wish to help humanity.

The alien creatures are the most obvious flaw. They arrive from nowhere, kill everyone, and leave, and they exist entirely to solve the plot. Because the story has not prepared for them, they feel like a trick played on the reader. The thousand years that pass in a line and a half are handled with the casual speed of pulp, but the shift from a realistic revenge tale to an apocalypse does not feel earned.

There is also some overwriting. Phrases like the arching hotness of the earth and failure glazed eyes are strained, and the exclamation points and the broken-up spelling of the words prisoner of time can feel melodramatic. Yet that melodrama is part of the pulp bargain. Taken as a short, intense fable, the story works in spite of itself, because the central image is so strong that the rough edges matter less than they should.

III. The Impact It Had

The Pendulum did not change the course of science fiction. Its importance is biographical, and that is no small thing. It was the first story Ray Bradbury sold to a professional magazine, and it marked the moment when a boy who had been writing in fanzines and mailing manuscripts into the void became a working writer.

Some of the circumstances are worth knowing. Bradbury had fallen in love with science fiction as a child, reading Buck Rogers comic strips, Edgar Rice Burroughs, and the pulps. After his family moved to Los Angeles during the Depression, he found his community in a local science fiction club and began to publish a small fan magazine of his own. Henry Hasse, an established pulp writer who was part of that circle, supplied a plot idea and helped him turn it into a salable story. The pair shared the byline. For a young man selling newspapers on a street corner, being paid for his words was a thrilling proof that the path was real.

After that, Bradbury began selling to a wide range of pulp magazines throughout the 1940s, learning how to write quickly and how to land an ending. He would later say, as many writers do, that his early stories were not very good and that he needed to write a great many bad ones before the good ones appeared. The Pendulum is a clear example of this stage. It shows his ability to build an image and a mood, but not yet the warmth and humanity that would soon distinguish him.

For readers and scholars, the story has a different kind of impact. It serves as a marker in the long record of his career, and it is sometimes mentioned in biographies and bibliographies as the starting point. Because it was a collaboration, it also reminds us that Bradbury's early development depended on friendships and mentors. Writers rarely begin alone.

There are no famous adaptations of the story, and it is not widely taught in classrooms. Its life today is among Bradbury collectors and students of early pulp fiction, who read it for the glimpse of the future master. Its indirect influence may lie in the image it introduced into his imagination. The idea of a person trapped in a mechanism that mocks his ambition, and of time as both a cage and a way out, resonates through later Bradbury stories about clocks, machines, and the strange ways human beings meet their futures.

IV. What Readers Loved

Because The Pendulum is an early pulp story, there is little documented public reaction beyond its being published and later reprinted for the sake of Bradbury's fame. It would be a mistake to claim a lively critical conversation around it. What we can describe is what readers, then and now, tend to enjoy about it.

The first pleasure is the idea at the heart of the story. A punishment that fits the crime in a bitterly poetic way has always been appealing, and the notion that a time machine inventor is turned into the pendulum of a clock is a neat stroke of grim humor. Readers love an irony that clicks into place, and the phrase prisoner of time carries the weight of the entire story in three words. It is the kind of title and tagline that would not look out of place on a classic episode of an old anthology television series.

The second pleasure is the sheer physical immediacy. Many readers have said, in the way readers talk about early Bradbury, that they could feel the nausea of the swinging. The description of how Layeville first gets sick, cannot eat, then slowly adapts until he can eat and sleep while swinging, is oddly convincing. It shows how human beings can get used to almost anything, which is a quietly disturbing observation. The detail of the little glass loops and leather thongs makes the nightmare concrete.

The third is the ending. The skull grinning in the glass, wearing what the story calls an enigmatical smile, is the sort of last image that stays in the memory. Readers like an ending that leaves a question. Did he die insane, or at peace? Did he beat time by outlasting everyone? The story does not say, and that openness is one reason it remains pleasant to discuss.

Finally, fans of Bradbury enjoy the story as a window. They can see in it the beginnings of habits that would later bloom: the love of repetition and incantation, the eye for macabre detail, the willingness to end on a single image rather than an explanation. For people who admire him, reading The Pendulum is like looking at an early sketch by a painter whose later work they love. It is rough, but it is unmistakably the beginning of something.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

It is important to be candid here. There is no substantial record of reviews or critical essays on The Pendulum, because it was a pulp story from 1941 and the pulps were rarely reviewed in the way that books were. What exists is a general consensus among those who write about Bradbury's early work, and some objections that arise naturally from reading it.

The most common observation is that the story is apprentice work. Biographers and critics who discuss Bradbury's beginnings tend to treat it as a sign of promise rather than a success. The plot is thin, the characters are barely sketched, and the science is a placeholder. There is no explanation of what the machine was supposed to do, why it failed, or how it can be turned into a clock. That vagueness is a standard pulp convention, but it leaves the story feeling like a skeleton on which a more thoughtful writer might later hang flesh.

A second objection concerns the collaboration. Because Henry Hasse shares the credit, it can be hard to say how much of the story's best passages are Bradbury's. Some readers assume that the lyrical rhythm of the opening is his, and that the bare plot comes from Hasse, but this is speculation. The fair statement is that the story is a joint work and that its voice is not yet fully the voice of the mature Bradbury.

A third objection is that the story's moral tone is uncertain. We are told that Layeville has killed thirty people, yet the story invites us to sympathize with him, and then to enjoy his survival. The mob is cruel, but the victim is not entirely innocent. A reader may feel that the story does not decide whether Layeville is a martyr, a fool, or a murderer, and that this confusion is more accidental than artful. The final grin of the skull lets him win, but what exactly has he won?

Finally, readers find the middle of the story, where the alien creatures arrive, a convenience. The abrupt apocalypse is not foreshadowed and does not connect to the earlier themes, and many pulp stories of the time used such sudden disasters to wrap up a plot. In a modern reading, that kind of resolution tends to feel arbitrary, however striking the final image may be. These are small flaws in a short piece, but they explain why the story is valued more as a beginning than as a classic.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Pendulum sits at the meeting point of two traditions: the time travel story and the tale of poetic punishment. Its place in each is modest, but the connections are instructive.

The time machine story begins, for most readers, with H. G. Wells, whose 1895 novel The Time Machine established the idea of a vehicle that carries a person through the centuries and shows him a dying Earth. By 1941, time travel was a staple of the pulp magazines, and writers were looking for new twists. Bradbury and Hasse's twist is clever: instead of a traveler who jumps to the future, we have a man who travels there at the ordinary speed of time, by sitting still and swinging. It is a time machine story in which the machine is a clock, and the journey is simply surviving.

The other tradition is the punishment fitted to the crime, an old literary pattern found in Dante's Inferno, where each sinner suffers a penalty that mirrors his sin, and in countless folk tales. The title also invites comparison with Edgar Allan Poe's The Pit and the Pendulum, written in 1842, in which a prisoner is tormented by a swinging blade. Poe's story is about dread and escape, while this one is about endurance and irony, but the shared image of a pendulum as a device of cruelty is surely not an accident. There is also a distant echo of Kafka's tales of punishment machines, in which a mechanism inscribes a sentence on the body of the condemned.

Looking forward, the story anticipates Bradbury's later fascination with clocks, with machines that outlast their makers, and with the idea that human routines continue after humans are gone. The robots that keep tending the pendulum in the dead city are an early version of the automated house in There Will Come Soft Rains, where machines keep making breakfast for a family that no longer exists. That is perhaps the most important line of descent: from this crude pulp tale to one of the most celebrated short stories in the language.

Why read it today? It is short, it takes ten minutes, and it gives the pleasure of seeing a great writer at the start. It also leaves a durable image, a smiling skull in a swinging glass bulb above a silent city. As a lesson in how a single strong idea and a steady rhythm can carry a flawed story, it is worth the time.
