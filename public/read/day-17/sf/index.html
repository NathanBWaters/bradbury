Harvey Milk's Castro Camera at 575 Castro Street and Gilbert Baker's 1978 Rainbow Flag

San Francisco — How a small camera shop in a working-class Irish neighborhood became the campaign headquarters that changed American civil rights—and birthed a global eight-stripe symbol of pride (Gay Liberation & Coalition Era (1972–1978)) • 8 min read

“You gotta give 'em hope. If a gay person makes it, the doors are open to everyone.” — Supervisor Harvey Milk, 'The Hope Speech' (1978)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In March 1973, forty-two-year-old former Wall Street analyst and Broadway producer Harvey Bernard Milk and his partner Scott Smith pooled their last $1,000 to open Castro Camera in a narrow storefront at 575 Castro Street in the Eureka Valley neighborhood. Over the next five years, standing behind a battered wooden counter with an antique barber's chair by the front window, the 'Mayor of Castro Street' forged an unprecedented political coalition uniting LGBTQ+ activists, Teamster beer-truck drivers, Chinese American and Latino working families, firefighters, and senior citizens—winning election to the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in November 1977 as one of the first openly gay elected officials in U.S. history and challenging artist Gilbert Baker in June 1978 to sew the first Rainbow Flag.

Before the 1960s, the sunny valley tucked beneath the eastern flank of Twin Peaks around Castro and 18th streets was known as Eureka Valley or 'Little Scandinavia' and later as a staunchly working-class Irish Catholic parish anchored by Most Holy Redeemer Church and the 1922 Spanish Colonial Revival palace of the Castro Theatre. As postwar suburbanization drew Catholic families out to West Portal and the Peninsula in the late 1960s and Victorian cottage rents dropped along Castro Street, thousands of gay men and lesbians—many dishonorably discharged from the U.S. military at the San Francisco embarkation ports after World War II and the Korean War—moved south from Polk Gulch and the Haight into Eureka Valley.

In late 1972, Harvey Bernard Milk—a Long Island native, former U.S. Navy diving officer aboard the submarine rescue ship USS Kittiwake, former Wall Street investment researcher, and assistant producer for the rock musical Hair—drove to San Francisco with his partner Scott Smith and settled permanently on Castro Street in March 1973 after a local store ruined a roll of their vacation film. Opening Castro Camera at 575 Castro Street with their final $1,000 savings, Milk and Smith unintentionally created the neighborhood's de facto town hall: residents dropped off film rolls not merely for developing, but to hear Milk hold court from an old red velvet barber chair, register to vote, find apartments, or organize protection against police raids and street harassment.

When conservative elders in the existing Merchants Association tried to block two gay men from opening an antique shop on Castro Street in 1973, Milk founded the Castro Village Association—starting the annual Castro Street Fair in August 1974—and demonstrated the economic power of the community by teaming up with Teamsters Local 888 organizer Allan Baird in the 1974 Coors Beer Boycott. The Coors Boycott was a masterclass in coalition politics: when Teamster brewery drivers struck Coors over polygraph tests that probed workers' sexual orientation and political beliefs, Milk convinced every gay bar in the Castro, Polk Street, and the Tenderloin to dump Coors from their taps overnight; in grateful return, the Teamsters Union hired openly gay truck drivers and backed Milk's campaigns.

Despite losing three successive campaigns for public office—the 1973 and 1975 at-large Board of Supervisors races and a 1976 run for the California State Assembly—Milk worked with neighborhood activists to pass Proposition T in November 1976, replacing citywide at-large elections (dominated by downtown corporate donors) with eleven neighborhood-based supervisorial districts. Running in the newly created District 5 (spanning the Castro, Haight-Ashbury, Duboce Triangle, and Noe Valley) with twenty-three-year-old lesbian organizer Anne Kronenberg as his tough-minded campaign manager, Milk won a landslide victory on November 8, 1977, becoming the first openly gay man elected to major public office in California history.

During his eleven months in City Hall in 1978, Supervisor Milk sponsored and passed San Francisco's comprehensive Gay Rights Ordinance banning discrimination in housing and employment (with only one dissenting vote, from District 8 Supervisor Dan White), fought tirelessly for affordable Muni transit passes and senior-citizen housing, and famously pushed the landmark 'Pooper Scooper' canine curb law. Meanwhile, in the spring of 1978, Milk urged his friend Gilbert Baker—a twenty-six-year-old former U.S. Army medic drafted in 1970 who had taught himself to sew protest banners in San Francisco—to design an uplifting visual emblem for the upcoming June 25, 1978 San Francisco Gay Freedom Day Parade to replace the Nazi-era pink triangle.

Working inside the top-floor attic galleries of the Gay Community Center at 330 Grove Street, Baker and thirty volunteers boiled natural dyes in giant plastic trash cans to hand-dye a thousand yards of unbleached cotton muslin in eight symbolic colors: hot pink for sex, red for life, orange for healing, yellow for sunlight, green for nature, turquoise for magic/art, indigo for serenity, and violet for spirit. On the sunny afternoon of June 25, 1978, as 250,000 marchers streamed down Market Street to United Nations Plaza, Baker's two thirty-by-sixty-foot hand-stitched eight-stripe Rainbow Flags billowed in the breeze above Civic Center while Harvey Milk rode in an open convertible and declared to the crowd that gay people across America must 'come out' to shatter the myths of bigots.

Throughout the summer and fall of 1978, Milk traveled across California to debate conservative State Senator John Briggs over Proposition 6 (the Briggs Initiative), a ballot measure that would have mandated the firing of every gay or lesbian public school teacher—and any heterosexual teacher who spoke in support of gay rights—in California. Joined by former Governor Ronald Reagan and President Jimmy Carter in opposing Proposition 6, Milk's grassroots coalition crushed the Briggs Initiative by more than one million votes on November 7, 1978—proving that ordinary voters would reject state-sanctioned persecution when confronted with open human honesty.

Twenty days later, on the morning of November 27, 1978, disgruntled former Supervisor Dan White—who had resigned his seat and then demanded that Mayor George Moscone reappoint him—climbed through a City Hall basement window to bypass metal detectors and assassinated both Mayor Moscone and Supervisor Harvey Milk inside City Hall, prompting 40,000 grieving San Franciscans to march silently in candlelight from Castro Street down Market Street that night. Nine days after opening Castro Camera, Milk had recorded a political will on cassette tape declaring, 'If a bullet should enter my brain, let that bullet destroy every closet door'; today, Designated City Landmark #227 at 575 Castro Street, the rainbow crosswalks at 18th and Castro, and the 100-foot Rainbow Flag flying continuously over Harvey Milk Plaza stand as living monuments to his voice.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Coalition Politics and the 1976 District Election Revolution

How did Harvey Milk move from a three-time losing outsider to a decisive policymaker in City Hall? First, by building cross-class alliances—most famously the 1974 alliance between gay bar owners and Teamster beer drivers, alongside seniors and Asian American groups; second, through the passage of Proposition T in November 1976, which replaced downtown-controlled citywide at-large seats with eleven geographic neighborhood districts, allowing the Castro, Haight, and Mission to elect true grassroots representatives.

2. Gilbert Baker's Eight Stripes to Six: The Material History of the Rainbow Flag

Why did Gilbert Baker's original June 25, 1978 hand-dyed Rainbow Flag at Civic Center have eight stripes instead of today's universal six? After Harvey Milk's assassination in November 1978 caused demand for the flag to skyrocket nationwide, Baker partnered with San Francisco's Paramount Flag Company at Polk and Post streets. Because commercial flag mills lacked mass-produced hot-pink bunting fabric (and later because an odd number of seven stripes was split down the middle by Market Street lamp posts), hot pink and turquoise were combined/dropped in 1979 to yield the classic six-color design.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three historical touchstones: first, 575 Castro Street (City Landmark #227), where Castro Camera functioned as both a neighborhood business and voter-registration hub below Milk's upstairs apartment; second, Proposition 6 (the 1978 Briggs Initiative), whose statewide defeat marked the turning point for modern LGBTQ+ civil rights in America; and third, the 330 Grove Street attic where Gilbert Baker and thirty volunteers dyed the first Rainbow Flags in trash barrels.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Step off the Muni Metro at Castro Station and pause at Harvey Milk Plaza at the corner of Market and Castro streets beneath the giant 20-by-30-foot Rainbow Flag and photo timeline. Walk south down the Rainbow Honor Walk bronze sidewalk plaques past the 1922 Castro Theatre to 575 Castro Street, where the storefront of Castro Camera and its historic sidewalk plaque preserve the heart of Milk's movement.
