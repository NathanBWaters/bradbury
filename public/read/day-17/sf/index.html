Harvey Milk's Castro Camera at 575 Castro Street and Gilbert Baker's 1978 Rainbow Flag

San Francisco — How a small camera shop in a working-class Irish neighborhood became the campaign headquarters that changed American civil rights—and birthed a global eight-stripe symbol of pride (Gay Liberation & Coalition Era (1972–1978)) • 20 min read

Key Context & Why It Matters

In March 1973, forty-two-year-old former Wall Street analyst and Broadway producer Harvey Bernard Milk and his partner Scott Smith pooled their last $1,000 to open Castro Camera in a narrow storefront at 575 Castro Street (37.7600° N, 122.4350° W) in the Eureka Valley neighborhood. Over the next five years, standing behind a battered wooden counter with an antique barber's chair by the front window, the 'Mayor of Castro Street' forged an unprecedented political coalition uniting LGBTQ+ activists, Teamster beer-truck drivers, Chinese American and Latino working families, firefighters, and senior citizens—winning election to the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in November 1977 as one of the first openly gay elected officials in U.S. history and challenging artist Gilbert Baker in June 1978 to sew the first Rainbow Flag.

1. Coalition Politics and the 1976 District Election Revolution

How did Harvey Milk move from a three-time losing outsider to a decisive policymaker in City Hall? First, by building cross-class alliances—most famously the 1974 alliance between gay bar owners and Teamster beer drivers, alongside seniors and Asian American groups; second, through the passage of Proposition T in November 1976, which replaced downtown-controlled citywide at-large seats with eleven geographic neighborhood districts, allowing the Castro, Haight, and Mission to elect true grassroots representatives. 2. Gilbert Baker's Eight Stripes to Six: The Material History of the Rainbow Flag

Why did Gilbert Baker's original June 25, 1978 hand-dyed Rainbow Flag at Civic Center have eight stripes instead of today's universal six? After Harvey Milk's assassination in November 1978 caused demand for the flag to skyrocket nationwide, Baker partnered with San Francisco's Paramount Flag Company at Polk and Post streets. Because commercial flag mills lacked mass-produced hot-pink bunting fabric (and later because an odd number of seven stripes was split down the middle by Market Street lamp posts), hot pink and turquoise were combined/dropped in 1979 to yield the classic six-color design.
