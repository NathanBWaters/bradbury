Jeju's Gotjawal Lava Forests: The Breathable Basalt Sponge Where North and South Collide

Korea — Inside the tangled clinker-lava boulder fields whose geothermal 'breathing holes' keep winter roots warm and summer ferns icy (Quaternary Volcanic Era (100,000 Years Ago) to Present) • 9 min read

“Where the plow breaks against black stone, the forest drinks the monsoon and breathes warm mist through the winter snow.” — Jeju Forest Saying

Key Context & Why It Matters

Covering roughly twelve percent of Jeju Island's surface in four major volcanic belts flanking Mount Hallasan, Gotjawal (a native Jeju dialect compound of got, 'forest,' and jawal, 'tangled rocks and thorns') is one of the rarest volcanological and botanical ecosystems on Earth. Formed between 100,000 and 10,000 years ago when parasitic volcanic cones (oreum) erupted rubbly a'a and blocky pahoehoe basalt lava flows across the island's flanks, Gotjawal terrains could never be cleared for farming. Beneath the mossy boulder piles lie deep subterranean air channels called sumgol ('breathing holes') that vent 15°C geothermal air year-round—allowing Siberian northern-limit ferns and tropical southern-limit camellias to grow on the exact same rock while filtering rainwater into Jeju's freshwater aquifers.

Step off the sunny coastal road of Jocheon-eup or Hangyeong-myeon on Jeju Island into the shaded edge of a Gotjawal forest on an August afternoon when the outside air is a stifling 33°C, and within ten paces a sudden, icy breath rises from the mossy black boulders around your ankles, dropping the temperature to 21°C. Return to that exact same boulder crevice in January when snow blankets the upper slopes of Mount Hallasan and freezing winds whip the Korea Strait, and curl your ungloved hand over the gap between the rocks: a gentle plume of 16°C warm, humid vapor drifts upward, beading the evergreen fern fronds with liquid dew.

In the native Jeju language (Jejueo), got means forest and jawal means a tangled thicket of jagged rocks, brambles, and vines. For centuries, Jeju farmers viewed these four sprawling belts of fractured black basalt—covering 110 square kilometers across Jocheon-Hamdeok, Gujwa-Seongsan, Aewol, and Hangyeong-Andeok—as cursed, useless wasteland because not a single plowshare could penetrate the piled lava rubble. Paradoxically, it was precisely that agricultural uselessness that saved Jeju’s biodiversity. While generations of mainland and coastal farmers cleared fertile volcanic ash soils for barley, millet, and tangerine orchards, the jagged boulder labyrinths of the Gotjawal remained virtually untouched by human axes since the end of the last Ice Age.

For decades, textbooks loosely described Gotjawal as purely Hawaiian-style a'a (rough clinker lava) flows in contrast to smooth, ropy pahoehoe lava tubes. Recent volcanological mapping by the Jeju Research Institute and the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources (KIGAM), however, revealed a far more complex origin: as medium-to-low-viscosity basaltic lava poured down from parasitic volcanic cones (oreum) such as Donggeomun-oreum and Byeongak-oreum between 100,000 and 10,000 years ago, its cooling crust repeatedly inflated, fractured, and autoclaved into chaotic fields of angular boulders, collapsed tumuli, and subterranean voids. Beneath those stacked black basalt blocks—ranging from fist-sized scoria clinkers to house-sized monoliths—lies an interconnected three-dimensional honeycomb of underground cavities known in Jeju dialect as sumgol (literally 'breathing holes' or 'wind holes,' pung-hyeol).

The thermodynamics of a sumgol operate like a natural geothermal heat exchanger. Because deep basalt bedrock ten to thirty meters underground remains at the island’s annual mean temperature of roughly 15°C to 17°C year-round, air trapped in the subterranean lava cavities stays warm in winter and cool in summer. In winter, buoyant warm air rises out of upper crevices (warm-air vents) while dense cold surface air sinks into lower cracks; in summer, dense cold air pours out of lower vents across the mossy forest floor. That constant thermal buffering produces a botanical miracle found almost nowhere else on the planet: inside a single Gotjawal depression a few dozen meters wide, subtropical southern-limit plants from Southeast Asia and Taiwan grow root-to-root with subarctic northern-limit plants from Siberia and Manchuria!

Look down into a cool summer sumgol hollow where cold air pools at 18°C, and you find high-latitude boreal relicts that survived the retreat of the Pleistocene glaciers—such as Jeju-gosarisam (Mankyua chejuense), an ancient, monotypic fern genus discovered in the Seonheul Gotjawal in 2001 that exists nowhere else on Earth—growing just two meters below the canopy of tropical camellias (Camellia japonica), creeping figs, and red-barked Jonggasi-namu (Quercus glauca, ring-cupped evergreen oak). How do towering evergreen oaks, hackberries, and nutmeg yews (Bija-namu) grow to thirty-five meters tall on bare piles of solid volcanic rock where there is virtually zero soil? Watch the succession of a Gotjawal boulder over a thousand years. First, crustose lichens and cushion mosses (seok-i and ikki) colonize the vesicular pores of the rough basalt, trapping windblown dust and morning fog.

Next, shade-loving ferns (gosari) sprout in the damp moss pads, decomposing over centuries into thin pockets of rich organic humus inside the rock crevices. When an acorn or camellia seed drops onto the boulder top, the young sapling sends long, muscular taproots snaking three to five meters across the bare rock surface like pythons—clinging to the jagged basalt with flat root buttresses (panggeun) until its root tips plunge down into the humid, dark sumgol cavities below. Just as critical as its botanical diversity is the Gotjawal’s hydrological role as the 'kidneys and lungs of Jeju Island.' Although Jeju receives the highest annual rainfall in South Korea—nearly 1,900 millimeters a year—the island has almost no perennial surface rivers because rainwater sinks immediately through its porous volcanic rock.

When torrential summer typhoons dump 300 millimeters of rain in a single afternoon across the flanks of Hallasan, the tangled canopy and deep boulder crevices of the Gotjawal act as a giant geological sponge. Over seventy percent of the rainwater falling on a Gotjawal forest infiltrates directly down through the sumgol vents without surface runoff, filtering slowly through layers of porous scoria and basalt before replenishing the pristine underground freshwater lens (samdasu aquifer) that 700,000 Jeju residents drink every day. In the Dongbaekdongsan (Camellia Hill) sector of the Jocheon-Hamdeok Gotjawal near Seonheul-ri—designated a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance in 2011—pahoehoe lava mounds alternate with seasonal rainwater pools called Meonmulkkak. Here, rare endangered fairy shrimp (gin-kkori-tugusaeu), Boreal digging frogs (maengkkongi), and the radiant Fairy Pitta (Palgisae, —a migratory bird with eight jewel-toned plumage colors) breed beneath a canopy that never loses its green leaves.

Even human history hid inside these lava forests. During the nineteenth-century Catholic persecutions and the tragic Jeju 4·3 Uprising of 1948–1949, villagers fled into the unmapped boulder mazes and hidden lava caves (gwe) of the Gotjawal—such as Doteul-gul and Bensille-gul—surviving for months on rainwater dripping from basalt ceilings and wild berries while search parties passed unknowingly thirty feet away. Beginning in the 1990s, when luxury golf-course developers and stone quarries began bulldozing sections of the Gotjawal under the misconception that rocky scrubland had low ecological value, Jeju citizens, botanists, and hydrologists launched the Gotjawal People movement and the Jeju Gotjawal Trust—raising public donations on a 'buy-one-pyeong' civic land-trust model to purchase private lava forest plots and place them under permanent conservation. Walk the quiet earthen boardwalk of the Jeju Gotjawal Provincial Park or Seonheul Dongbaekdongsan after a light spring rain. As mist curls out of the black sumgol vents around serpentine oak roots and a Fairy Pitta calls from the camellia canopy, you can literally feel the volcanic island inhaling and exhaling beneath your boots.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Geothermal Microclimate Physics: How 'Sumgol' Vents Bridge Siberia and the Tropics

Meteorological data loggers placed inside Jeju Gotjawal sumgol ('breathing holes') by Korean forest ecologists demonstrate an extraordinary thermal inversion effect. While outside air temperatures on Jeju swing from -2°C in January to 34°C in August, air emerging from three-meter-deep basalt boulder voids remains within a narrow band of 14°C to 18°C with 90 percent relative humidity year-round. This microclimatic stability allows cold-adapted boreal ferns and warm-adapted subtropical broadleaved evergreens to share the same square meter of lava rock.

2. Mankyua chejuense (Jeju Gosarisam): A Living Fossil Endemic to One Forest

In 2001, botanist Sun Byung-yun and colleagues announced in the journal Taxon the discovery of Mankyua chejuense in the seasonal wetlands of the Seonheul Gotjawal. Belonging to the primitive Ophioglossaceae (adder's-tongue fern) family that diverged over 300 million years ago during the Paleozoic era, it represents a completely distinct monotypic genus found nowhere else on the globe—depending entirely on the seasonal flooding and winter warmth of the Gotjawal's basalt depressions.

3. Key Korean & Jeju Dialect Concepts to Remember

Keep three Jeju ecological terms in mind: first, Gotjawal, combining got (forest) and jawal (tangled volcanic rocks and thorns); second, Sumgol (breathing hole), the subterranean fractures and boulder voids that vent geothermal air and channel rainwater into the island's aquifer; and third, Panggeun, the muscular buttress roots that trees spread across bare basalt boulders where soil is absent.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit Dongbaekdongsan Wetland in Seonheul-ri, Jocheon-eup, where a five-kilometer flat forest loop circles the Meonmulkkak lava pond, and explore the Jeju Gotjawal Provincial Park in Daejeong-eup on the southwestern flank of the island, complete with an elevated canopy observatory and a restored charcoal-kiln site.
