I, Too

Langston Hughes (1925) • 3 min read

Sent to eat in the kitchen when company comes, the darker brother laughs, eats well, and grows strong for the tomorrow when no one will dare deny his seat at the American table.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in late 1924 while twenty-two-year-old Langston Hughes was stranded without a passport or money in Genoa, Italy, and first published in the landmark March 1925 Harlem issue of Survey Graphic (edited by Alain Locke into The New Negro) before closing The Weary Blues (1926). Belonging to the Harlem Renaissance tradition and categorized under Harlem Renaissance, 'I, Too' (1925) unfolds across 18 verbatim lines in 5 stanzas. Carved in stone on the wall of the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture in Washington, D.C., and translated into dozens of languages from Latin America to South Africa, 'I, Too' stands as one of the defining civic poems of modern history.

I, too, sing America.

I am the darker brother.

They send me to eat in the kitchen

When company comes,

But I laugh,

And eat well,

And grow strong.

Tomorrow,

I’ll sit at the table

When company comes.

Nobody’ll dare

Say to me,

“Eat in the kitchen,”

Then.

Besides,

They’ll see how beautiful I am

And be ashamed—

I, too, am America.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in late 1924 while twenty-two-year-old Langston Hughes was stranded without a passport or money in Genoa, Italy, and first published in the landmark March 1925 Harlem issue of Survey Graphic (edited by Alain Locke into The New Negro) before closing The Weary Blues (1926). Within the broader trajectory of Harlem Renaissance, Langston Hughes composed 'I, Too' (1925) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"I, too, sing America."—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Langston Hughes grounded these 18 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'I, Too' lies democratic inclusion, dignity under Jim Crow segregation, and the prophetic reclamation of American identity: answering Walt Whitman’s 'I Hear America Singing,' the 'darker brother' exiled to the domestic kitchen refuses bitterness—instead laughing, eating well, and growing strong until America recognizes both his beauty and its own shame. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"When company comes."—Langston Hughes develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. By the time the poem reaches its closing line—"I, too, am America."—the central metaphor has transformed the speaker’s initial perception, leaving the reader with an earned emotional equilibrium that refuses easy sentimentality.

III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Langston Hughes constructs 'I, Too' using eighteen spare lines across five stanzas bounded by a subtle, triumphant one-word shift from the opening declaration ('I, too, sing America') to the closing realization ('I, too, am America'), using plainspoken domestic metaphor ('eat in the kitchen,' 'be at the table') to transform a household slight into a national constitutional drama. Spanning 18 lines across 5 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Langston Hughes balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "I, too, sing America." and "I, too, am America." to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Carved in stone on the wall of the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture in Washington, D.C., and translated into dozens of languages from Latin America to South Africa, 'I, Too' stands as one of the defining civic poems of modern history. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'I, Too' has remained a touchstone of the Harlem Renaissance canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Langston Hughes’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
