Nightly Poem • Harlem Renaissance

I, Too

Langston Hughes (1925) • 3 min read

Sent to eat in the kitchen when company comes, the darker brother laughs, eats well, and grows strong for the tomorrow when no one will dare deny his seat at the American table.

Key Context & Why It Matters

I, Too is a very short poem by Langston Hughes that says something enormous about American identity. Hughes wrote it in the mid-1920s, during the Harlem Renaissance, the burst of Black art and writing in New York after the First World War. He was in his early twenties, had traveled to Africa and Europe working on ships, and in 1925 was working as a busboy in a Washington, D.C. hotel when the poet Vachel Lindsay discovered his work and helped launch his career. The poem answers Walt Whitman's famous celebration of the country, I Hear America Singing, which left out Black Americans. Hughes steps into that song and claims his place in it. Its form is plain and its tone is calm, even smiling, yet it carries a sharp political argument about who counts as American. As you listen, notice how it moves from the present of exclusion to a confident picture of the future.

I, too, sing America.

I am the darker brother.

They send me to eat in the kitchen

When company comes,

But I laugh,

And eat well,

And grow strong.

Tomorrow,

I’ll sit at the table

When company comes.

Nobody’ll dare

Say to me,

“Eat in the kitchen,”

Then.

Besides,

They’ll see how beautiful I am

And be ashamed—

I, too, am America.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem opens with an echo. I, too, sing America, Hughes says, and anyone who knows Walt Whitman will hear the answer to Whitman's poem I Hear America Singing, in which carpenters, masons, mothers and mechanics each sing their own song, the voices of a working democracy. Whitman's list is generous but notably does not include the descendants of enslaved people in any prominent way. Hughes, in effect, raises his hand: I am here too. The phrase the darker brother is a quiet and clever one. Calling himself a brother places Black Americans inside the national family, and the word darker is not an apology but a simple description. He is not asking to be a guest. He is declaring a kinship.

The next lines move from song to a scene. They send me to eat in the kitchen when company comes. This is an image of segregation and humiliation in domestic form. When guests arrive, the family hides the darker brother in the back. The kitchen is where servants eat, and the scene captures the way Black Americans in Hughes's day were treated as workers who could be used but not seated, welcomed to cook the meal but not to share it. It is also a miniature of the whole country: a house with a front room for display and a back room where the real labor happens.

Then the poem does something unexpected. But I laugh, and eat well, and grow strong. These lines refuse to make the speaker a victim. He keeps his sense of humor, he feeds himself, and he gets stronger precisely in the place where he was put. The kitchen becomes a place of strength rather than shame. This is not resignation. It is the quiet gathering of power, and it prepares the turn: Tomorrow, I'll sit at the table when company comes. Nobody'll dare say to me, eat in the kitchen, then. The confidence in that tomorrow is remarkable, since in 1925 the legal and social barriers were enormous. It is a prophecy and a challenge.

The closing lines are the most delicate. Besides, they'll see how beautiful I am and be ashamed. Notice that the future he imagines is not one of revenge. The others are ashamed, which means conscience has awoken. And the beauty is stressed, in a country that had spent centuries denying Black beauty. The last line returns to the beginning but sharpens it: I, too, am America. The shift from sing to am matters. At first he claims a voice in the national song; by the end he claims the nation itself. A reader might miss that the poem has moved from a request for participation to a statement of ownership, all in the same unhurried tone.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

I, Too is a short poem, and its brevity is part of its craft. In sixty-odd words, Hughes manages a complete argument with a beginning, a complication and a resolution. The free verse is carefully paced: very short lines create a halting, almost whispered effect, with the line Nobody'll dare broken into tiny pieces like footsteps approaching a door. The spacing on the page, especially the break between the present and the tomorrow stanza, gives the poem a before-and-after structure that the reader feels in the pause.

The tone is the poem's greatest achievement. It is calm, wry and self-possessed. Hughes could have written an angry poem, and he wrote many, but here he chose understatement. The line But I laugh, and eat well, and grow strong is a marvel of controlled voice, and it makes the injustice more visible by refusing to dramatize it. A reader feels the pressure of the exclusion more because the speaker will not complain about it. The poem also uses a simple, strong rhetorical device: it takes a famous poem as its starting point and reverses its omission. By turning the first line into a retort, Hughes makes a literary argument and a political one in a single stroke.

Yet the poem has limits, and it is worth being candid about them. The imagery is almost too plain. The kitchen and the table are clear symbols, but they are also somewhat familiar ones, and the poem does not complicate them. A reader looking for ambiguity may find the argument too neat: exclusion now, inclusion tomorrow, shame on the oppressors. The idea that the white company will be ashamed once they see how beautiful the speaker is perhaps assumes a moral responsiveness that history did not always supply. It is touching, but a skeptic could call it optimistic to the point of naivety.

There is also a question about whether the poem asks too little. It wants a seat at the existing table, not a new table. Later writers, especially in the Black Power era, pointed out that the poem accepts the premise of the house and the company, and seeks only admission. But this is to read the poem against its time. In 1925, saying out loud that a Black man belonged at the national table was already a bold act, and the poem's quiet self-assurance does political work that a louder poem might not. Taken together, the strengths outweigh the weaknesses. The poem is not complicated, but it is exact. Every line does a job, and the ending, with its tiny pivot from sing to am, is among the neatest closing moves in American poetry.

III. The Impact It Had

I, Too appeared in the mid-1920s, in a period when Hughes's poems were first reaching a wide audience, and it was included in his first book, The Weary Blues, published by Alfred A. Knopf in 1926. That book established him as a leading voice of the Harlem Renaissance. The story of how he got there is itself part of the poem's legend. In 1925, while working at a Washington hotel, Hughes left some poems beside the plate of the already famous poet Vachel Lindsay, who read them aloud that night to an audience and told the press he had discovered a Negro busboy poet. The anecdote fits the poem's themes of service and recognition almost too neatly, and it brought Hughes his first real attention.

The poem's impact since then has been remarkable for something so small. It has become one of the most quoted statements of Black American belonging. It is read at civil rights commemorations, in classrooms, and at public events, and its final line is often used as a slogan of inclusion. Its title was borrowed for the I, Too, Am Harvard campaign in 2014, in which Black students at Harvard photographed themselves holding signs about their experiences of exclusion, and similar campaigns followed on other campuses. The phrase has appeared on murals, in speeches, and in essays about race and citizenship.

Writers have answered the poem many times. It is part of an ongoing conversation about Whitman's America, extended by many later poets of color who write their own versions of the national roll call. Hughes's own later poem Let America Be America Again, from 1935, picks up its theme with more anger and a wider list of the excluded. Literary scholars treat I, Too as a key example of how Black writers reshaped the American canon by writing themselves into it rather than waiting for admission.

In education, it is widely taught, from middle school through college. It works especially well in lessons about Whitman, about the Harlem Renaissance, and about the history of segregation, and it gives teachers a clear way to talk about intertextuality, the way one poem responds to another. It is also, for many students, an early model of how a short poem can make a political argument. Its influence, then, is less that of a stylistic breakthrough than of a moral and cultural one: it changed how readers imagine the American chorus.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love I, Too for its confidence. The speaker is in a humiliating position, and yet he sounds calm, even amused. That mix of dignity and humor is rare and inspiring, and many readers describe the poem as giving them courage. It is a poem you can say to yourself in a moment of being overlooked, and feel steadier afterward.

They love its brevity. It can be memorized in a minute, and its form, with short lines and a clear pivot, makes it easy to carry. The simple vocabulary opens it to children and new readers, while its literary reference to Whitman rewards those who know more. Teachers have long valued that double accessibility. Students can feel its argument immediately, and then discover the allusion and see a second layer.

Readers also love its generosity. The poem does not hate the people who send the speaker to the kitchen. It imagines their shame, which implies that they can change. That optimism about human conscience is one of the most affecting features of the poem. It speaks to a hope that the nation's best ideals, as Whitman voiced them, can be turned against the nation's failures, and that the country can be called to be what it says it is.

Finally, many Black readers have found a source of pride and solidarity in the poem. The line they'll see how beautiful I am is particularly cherished, because it asserts beauty and worth against a culture that denied both. Hughes's contemporaries in the Harlem Renaissance prized this kind of affirmation, and later generations have returned to it again and again. The poem is a reminder that dignity can be declared before it is granted, and that declaring it is part of how it comes to be granted.

There is a musical pleasure here too, though it is easy to overlook. The poem is built on small repetitions: I, too at the start and I, too at the end, the paired when company comes, and the steady rhythm of laugh, eat well, grow strong. Those repetitions give it the feel of a chant or a short sermon, and people who hear it read aloud often say it lands harder than it does on the page. It has been recited by actors, schoolchildren and public figures at gatherings of every size, and each reading seems to find new force in the plain words. That adaptability is a quiet sign of a poem's strength. It does not depend on one tone of voice. A whisper, a shout and a gentle smile can all carry it, and each makes a slightly different poem, which is part of why people keep returning to it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

I, Too has been well loved, but it has not escaped criticism. One line of objection is that the poem is too accommodating. Writers of later generations, especially in the Black Arts and Black Power movements of the 1960s and 1970s, were skeptical of poems that sought admission to a white-dominated table rather than questioning the table itself. In their view, the poem accepts the framing of the nation as a house in which white people are the hosts. Hughes himself was a more radical thinker than this single poem suggests, and some critics argue that it is unrepresentative of his harder edges.

A related complaint is that the poem is over-optimistic. The idea that people who segregate will simply be ashamed once they see how beautiful the excluded are has struck some readers as unrealistic. History shows that discrimination persisted, sometimes violently, long after poems like this one, and shame is not a reliable force. Some critics have called the poem naive, a gentle hope in the face of brutal facts. Supporters respond that hope is not the same as naivety, and that the poem knows precisely what it is up against, having placed the speaker in the kitchen to begin with.

A third point has to do with the poem's relationship to Whitman. Some scholars argue that by answering Whitman so directly, Hughes accepts Whitman's vision of America as a single chorus, a picture that downplays conflict. Others note that Whitman's poems about democracy were in fact more ambiguous about race than the poem assumes, and that the debate about Whitman's own attitudes complicates the reading. This is more a subject of scholarly discussion than of broad objection.

There is also, as with much Harlem Renaissance writing, a question about audience. Hughes was writing at a time when publishers, patrons and many readers were white, and some critics have asked how far a poem addressed to a white company shapes itself for white approval, hoping for their shame and their recognition. Hughes was aware of this tension. In his 1926 essay The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain, he insisted on writing for himself and his own people first, and said that if white people were pleased, he was glad. Whether this poem entirely escapes the problem is a fair question, and one on which readers differ.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

I, Too sits at the heart of the Harlem Renaissance, and also within a much longer American tradition of poems that argue with the nation about its promises. The tradition starts, in a sense, with the Declaration of Independence and runs through Whitman, through Frederick Douglass's speeches, through Paul Laurence Dunbar and Claude McKay, and on to Hughes. Each tests the country against its own words. McKay's sonnet If We Must Die, written in 1919, is angrier and more martial; Hughes's poem is quieter, but it carries an equally firm demand.

Its direct ancestor is Whitman, whose I Hear America Singing and Song of Myself defined the idea of an inclusive, democratic American voice. Hughes loved Whitman and wrote admiringly of him, and I, Too is partly an act of homage as well as a correction. In return, the poem has descendants in the work of poets such as Gwendolyn Brooks, Lucille Clifton, Amiri Baraka, and more recently Claudia Rankine, Tracy K. Smith and Ross Gay, many of whom write about belonging and exclusion in American life and answer the national tradition with their own counter-voices.

Compare it with Emma Lazarus's The New Colossus, which welcomes the poor and tired to the nation, or with Maya Angelou's Still I Rise, which shares its calm defiance. Compare it also with Hughes's own Theme for English B, in which a Black student in a New York college tells his white instructor that he is part of America and America is part of him. The link between those poems shows how central this idea was to Hughes's lifelong thinking. He was always asking how a Black American could belong to a country that did not yet fully acknowledge him, and always answering that belonging is a fact to be claimed.

Why does it still matter? Because the question it raises, who gets a seat at the table, has never gone away. Each generation has found it newly relevant, whether in debates about civil rights, immigration or representation. The poem remains a clear, concise reminder that the American song has always had more voices than the official versions admitted, and that those voices have not waited quietly. It is brief enough to read in under a minute and strong enough to stay with you for much longer, and it is an excellent first step into Hughes's world and into the Harlem Renaissance.
