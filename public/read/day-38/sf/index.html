June 26, 1945: Signing the United Nations Charter at the War Memorial Opera House and Herbst Theatre

San Francisco — How fifty nations gathered on Van Ness Avenue for nine weeks at the close of World War II to draft 'We the Peoples of the United Nations'—and sign the Charter beneath a circle of blue flags (End of World War II & Birth of the United Nations (April 25 – June 26, 1945)) • 9 min read

“You have created a great instrument for peace and security and human progress in the world. The world must now use it.” — President Harry S. Truman on the stage of the War Memorial Opera House, June 26, 1945

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk along Van Ness Avenue between Grove and McAllister streets past the twin Beaux-Arts facades of Arthur Brown Jr.'s 1932 War Memorial Opera House (301 Van Ness Avenue) and Veterans Building, and you stand at the birthplace of the modern international order. Between April 25 and June 26, 1945—while World War II still raged in the Pacific—850 delegates and 2,500 advisers from fifty Allied nations convened inside the Opera House for the United Nations Conference on International Organization (UNCIO), debated and rewrote the Dumbarton Oaks proposals to open with the immortal words 'We the peoples of the United Nations,' and stepped onto the stage of the Veterans Building Auditorium (now Herbst Theatre) on June 26, 1945, to sign the United Nations Charter.

Why did President Franklin Delano Roosevelt, Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and Premier Joseph Stalin agree at the Yalta Conference in February 1945 that the founding conference of the United Nations would convene not in Geneva, London, or New York, but across the North American continent in San Francisco? Roosevelt chose San Francisco for two profound geopolitical reasons: first, because the city on the Golden Gate was the primary embarkation port for the ongoing Pacific War against Imperial Japan, reminding every European delegate that World War II was a truly global struggle; and second, because San Francisco's monumental Beaux-Arts Civic Center offered the finest paired assembly halls in the Americas.

Designed by San Francisco architectural master Arthur Brown Jr. (who had co-designed City Hall in 1915 with John Bakewell Jr.) and opened in October 1932 as a living memorial to the veterans of World War I, the twin limestone-and-granite buildings at 301 and 401 Van Ness Avenue—the War Memorial Opera House and the Veterans Building—stood side by side separated by a landscaped Memorial Court. Tragically, thirteen days before the conference opened, on April 12, 1945, President Franklin D. Roosevelt died of a cerebral hemorrhage at Warm Springs, Georgia; newly sworn-in President Harry S. Truman immediately announced across the radio that the San Francisco Conference would proceed on April 25 exactly as Roosevelt had planned.

When 850 official delegates, 2,636 advisers and staff, and 2,500 journalists from across the globe descended upon San Francisco in late April 1945, the U.S. Army and Department of State transformed Civic Center into a multilingual diplomatic metropolis: the Fairmount, Mark Hopkins, St. Francis, and Palace hotels housed national delegations, while 2,000 Boy Scouts served as courier messengers and Pacific Telephone operators handled calls in dozens of languages. At 4:30 p.m. on a rainy Wednesday, April 25, 1945, inside the crimson-and-gold auditorium of the War Memorial Opera House, temporary Conference President Edward R. Stettinius Jr. rapped his gavel before a striking stage set designed by Broadway designer Jo Mielziner: four massive golden pillars (representing Roosevelt's Four Freedoms) rising against an azure-blue backdrop framed by the flags of forty-six initial Allied nations.

Although the 'Big Four' sponsoring powers (the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the Republic of China) had drafted a preliminary blueprint at Dumbarton Oaks in 1944 that focused almost exclusively on Great Power Security Council policing, the nine weeks in San Francisco quickly became a democratic rebellion by small and middle powers, Latin American republics, and forty-two accredited American non-governmental civic organizations. First, South African Field Marshal Jan Smuts and U.S. delegate Dean Virginia C. Gildersleeve—the dean of Barnard College and the only woman on the official United States delegation—rewrote the dry legalistic opening of the treaty into a soaring literary Preamble modeled on the U.S. Constitution: 'We the peoples of the United Nations, determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind...'

Second, led by female delegates from Brazil (Bertha Lutz), the Dominican Republic (Minerva Bernardino), Uruguay, and China (Wu Yi-fang)—joined by consultant W. E. B. Du Bois, Mary McLeod Bethune, and Walter White of the NAACP and the American Jewish Committee—the San Francisco delegates forced the Great Powers to weave explicit protections for 'human rights and fundamental freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language, or religion' seven times into the Charter. Third, inside the committee rooms of the Veterans Building on the third and fourth floors, thirty-nine-year-old African American political scientist Dr. Ralph J. Bunche—working past midnight on his typewriter for the U.S. State Department—single-handedly drafted Chapters XI, XII, and XIII of the Charter (the International Trusteeship System and Non-Self-Governing Territories), creating the legal mechanism that enabled the peaceful decolonization of more than eighty nations in Asia and Africa and helped earn Bunche the 1950 Nobel Peace Prize.

Twice during those nine dramatic weeks on Van Ness Avenue, the conference nearly collapsed: first over the admission of Argentina and Ukraine/Byelorussia, and second in late May 1945 when Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov demanded that a permanent Security Council member be able to veto even the discussion of an international dispute—a deadlock broken only after Harry Hopkins flew to Moscow to meet directly with Stalin. Even as the delegates debated inside the Opera House, history thundered through the lobby ticker-tapes: on April 25, U.S. and Soviet troops linked up at the Elbe River; on May 8, 1945 (V-E Day), Nazi Germany surrendered unconditionally, prompting a solemn moment of silence in the Opera House rather than rowdy celebration because young Americans and Allies were still dying on Okinawa.

Finally, on the evening of June 25, 1945, at the ninth plenary session inside the War Memorial Opera House, Lord Halifax asked all fifty national delegations to stand rather than merely raise their hands if they approved the completed 111-article United Nations Charter and Statute of the International Court of Justice—bringing the entire 3,300-seat auditorium to its feet in thunderous applause. The next morning, Tuesday, June 26, 1945, the scene shifted next door to the wood-paneled Veterans Building Auditorium (today's Herbst Theatre, illuminated by Frank Brangwyn's monumental 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition murals of Earth, Air, Fire, and Water).

Inside a forty-eight-foot circle of deep blue carpet surrounded by fifty national flags, sitting at a large circular mahogany table with two leather-bound volumes printed in five official languages (English, French, Chinese, Russian, and Spanish), Chinese diplomat Wellington Koo brushed the first signature onto the UN Charter at 12:12 p.m. in tribute to China as the first nation attacked by the Axis powers—followed over the next eight hours by 282 delegates, ending with President Harry S. Truman witnessing the United States signature. Every diplomat who walked out onto Van Ness Avenue that June afternoon carried a living memory of San Francisco—a memory honored twenty years later in 1965 at UN Plaza, and renewed every time a concertgoer steps into the Herbst Theatre or War Memorial Opera House where fifty nations chose law over war.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. How San Francisco Rewrote Dumbarton Oaks: Human Rights and Decolonization

Why was the nine-week 1945 San Francisco Conference far more than a ceremonial rubber stamp for the 1944 Dumbarton Oaks draft? Because small nations, Latin American women delegates like Bertha Lutz, U.S. delegate Virginia Gildersleeve, and forty-two civic consultant groups (including the NAACP and labor unions) insisted on embedding universal human rights, gender equality, the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC), and Ralph Bunche's Trusteeship decolonization chapters directly into the binding treaty text.

2. Architectural Setting: Arthur Brown Jr.'s 1932 War Memorial Twin Buildings

Notice the architectural symmetry of the two buildings at 301 and 401 Van Ness Avenue where the United Nations was born. Designed by Arthur Brown Jr. and G. Albert Lansburgh and opened in 1932 as a monument to peace after World War I, the War Memorial Opera House hosted the public plenary debates and Truman's closing address, while the Veterans Building Auditorium (today's 916-seat Herbst Theatre, adorned with Frank Brangwyn's eight 1915 murals) hosted the historic eight-hour signing ceremony on June 26, 1945.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this global milestone with three names and places: first, Dean Virginia C. Gildersleeve, who helped craft the Preamble ('We the peoples of the United Nations...'); second, Dr. Ralph J. Bunche, whose late-night drafting in the Veterans Building created the Trusteeship decolonization chapters; and third, the Herbst Theatre stage at 401 Van Ness Avenue, where the round signing table sat beneath fifty flags on June 26, 1945.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk along Van Ness Avenue between Grove and McAllister streets to visit the Memorial Court between the War Memorial Opera House (301 Van Ness) and the Veterans Building (401 Van Ness), where bronze plaques commemorate the 1945 UN Conference. Attend a City Arts & Lectures talk or chamber performance inside the Herbst Theatre to sit in the exact room surrounded by Frank Brangwyn's murals where the UN Charter was signed, then walk east through Civic Center Plaza to United Nations Plaza on Market Street.
