Shin Saimdang's Chochungdo (1504–1551): The Botanist-Painter of Gangneung's Black-Bamboo House

Korea — Inside Korea's oldest residential wooden hanok, how a sixteenth-century woman painted watermelons, field mice, and katydids so lifelike that chickens pecked the silk (Mid-Joseon Dynasty (1504–1551)) • 10 min read

“When Saimdang set her painted screen of summer melons and insects out in the courtyard to dry in the sun, the yard chickens ran over and pecked at the silk butterflies.” — Joseon Oral & Literary Chronicle of Gangneung

Key Context & Why It Matters

Open a South Korean wallet today and pull out the highest-denomination banknote—the golden 50,000-won bill—and on the front you meet Shin Saimdang (1504–1551), while on the reverse side of the 5,000-won bill sits her son, the Neo-Confucian philosopher Yi I (Yulgok), making them the only mother and son depicted together on a nation's currency. Born and raised inside Ojukheon ('Black-Bamboo House,' built in 1452 and preserved as Treasure No. 165 in Gangneung, Gangwon State), Saimdang defied the constraints of sixteenth-century patriarchal society to pioneer Chochungdo ('Grass and Insect Paintings')—rendering humble garden eggplants, watermelons, poppies, dung beetles, and field mice with an empirical entomological precision that anticipated European scientific illustration.

Walk through a rustling grove of Ojuk (—rare black-stemmed bamboo whose culms turn glossy ebony-black in their second year of growth) in Jukheon-dong, Gangneung, near the eastern coast of Gangwon State, and step into the courtyard of Ojukheon (Treasure No. 165). Built around 1452 during the early Joseon Dynasty with curved jusimpo column-head brackets and bevel-cut baekgol timbers, it is one of the oldest surviving wooden residential houses (salimjip) in all of Korea. Inside its side bedroom—named Mongnyongsil (Room of the Dragon Dream)—a remarkable woman named Shin Saimdang (1504–1551) grew up, painted, composed poetry, and in 1536 gave birth to her third son, the philosopher Yi I (pen name Yulgok).

For centuries in late-Joseon and twentieth-century moral textbooks, conservative male writers tried to flatten Shin Saimdang into a one-dimensional symbol of Hyeonmo-yangcheo (Wise Mother and Good Wife), praising her solely because she raised Yulgok. Yet when modern art historians, botanists, and literary scholars examine her surviving paintings and poems—and the historical reason she was able to flourish as an independent artist in the sixteenth century—a far more radical story emerges. How did a woman born in 1504 in provincial Gangneung acquire a classical education in Neo-Confucian philosophy, poetry, calligraphy, embroidery, and landscape painting equal to any male Seonbi scholar in Seoul? The answer lies in the family marriage law of the early-to-mid Joseon Dynasty—before the rigid seventeenth-century patriarchal patrilocal shift!

In 1504, Korea still practiced Namgwi-yeogahon and equal inheritance among sons and daughters : a bride did not leave her parents’ house forever upon marriage; instead, the groom traveled to the bride’s family home for the wedding and the couple lived for years with the wife’s parents! Even more crucially, Saimdang’s father Shin Myeong-hwa and her maternal grandfather Yi Sa-on had five daughters and zero sons. Refusing to treat his five daughters as lesser than sons, Grandfather Yi Sa-on and Shin Myeong-hwa educated young Shin In-seon (who chose her own studio pen name Saimdang, modeling herself after the virtuous scholar-matriarch Tairen) in the classics, histories, and fine arts inside Ojukheon. Even after marrying Yi Won-su in 1522 at age nineteen, Saimdang did not move immediately under her mother-in-law’s roof; with her husband’s full admiration and support, she lived primarily at her maternal home in Gangneung fornearly twenty years!

Beginning at age seven, when she copied the fifteenth-century court master An Gyeon’s (painter of Dream Journey to the Peach Blossom Land) landscapes so skillfully that guests mistook her ink mountains for An Gyeon’s own brush, Saimdang mastered classical monochrome ink landscape (sansuhwa) and grape painting (pododo). There is a famous documented story from her life: at a banquet where a guest’s prized white silk skirt (chima) was accidentally ruined by a spilled bowl of soup, Saimdang picked up an ink brush on the spot and turned the greasy brown stains into clusters of dewy purple grapes and trailing vines—allowing the poor woman to sell the skirt in the market for a fortune!

Yet Saimdang’s most original artistic revolution occurred when she turned her gaze downward from grand imaginary Chinese mountains to the humble dirt of her own Korean kitchen garden (teotbat): inventing the genre of Chochungdo ('Paintings of Grasses/Plants and Insects,' exemplified by the eight-panel folding screens designated Treasure No. 595 at the National Museum of Korea and at Ojukheon Museum). In sixteenth-century Neo-Confucian literati painting, male scholars painted only the 'Four Gentlemen' (Sagunja: plum blossom, orchid, chrysanthemum, and bamboo) or towering pine peaks because they considered garden vegetables, beetles, and mice beneath scholarly dignity. Saimdang looked at the soil outside her maru verandah at Ojukheon and saw an entire universe of ecological drama and maternal tenderness.

Look closely at Panel 1 of her Chochungdo screen (Watermelon and Field Mice, featured as the background watermark on the 50,000-won banknote!): a ripe green-striped summer watermelon has been gnawed open at the bottom, exposing its crimson flesh and glossy black seeds. Two tiny field mice (deuljwi) crouch beside the rind nibbling the sweet pulp, while overhead two butterflies—one yellow, one black swallowtail—hover above pink dianthus pinks and a red-winged katydid clings to a vine tendril. Move to Panel 2 (Eggplant and Ants): two curving purple-blue Joseon eggplants hang heavy from a thorny calyx stem while tiny red ants march up the root and a praying mantis braces its serrated forelegs below a bee. Across the other six panels blossom cockscomb with a shiny dung beetle rolling through the dirt; red poppies with a crested lizard and frog; cucumbers with a mole cricket ; and daylilies with dragonflies.

When twentieth-century entomologists and botanists at Kangwon National University examined Saimdang’s Chochungdo panels under magnification, they were stunned by her empirical biological accuracy. Unlike stylized decorative paintings, Saimdang rendered the exact venation of cucumber leaves, the three-segmented thorax and wing spots of the Korean katydid, and the seasonal bloom synchronization of each flower and insect—proving she had observed living specimens in the Ojukheon garden for hours before touching brush to silk or dyed misi paper! To achieve those luminous, non-fading colors five hundred years ago without synthetic paints, Saimdang compounded her own natural mineral and botanical pigments : grinding azurite and malachite stones for blue and green, roasting oyster shells for opaque white, extracting gamboge and gardenia for yellow, and layering translucent washes (molgolbeop—boneless wash technique paired with fine contour lines) so the eggplant skin gleams with morning dew.

Simultaneously, Saimdang was a poet of piercing emotional honesty. 'Leaving my white-haired mother behind in the home of Imyeong (Gangneung), / My solitary heart aches as I turn my horse toward Seoul. / Looking back, the northern village grows dim in the evening mist; / Only the white clouds drift down over the mountain ridge in the twilight.' Today at Ojukheon in Gangneung, a new generation of practitioners and cultural stewards continues to breathe fresh vitality into Shin Saimdang (16th-century Joseon painter, poet, and calligrapher). When Saimdang died suddenly in May 1551 at age forty-seven while her husband and sons were away on a boat trip down the Han River, her fifteen-year-old son Yulgok spent three years in mourning by her grave in Paju and wrote her biography (Seonbi Haengjang), recording how his mother had been his truest intellectual companion. In 2009, when the Bank of Korea issued the new 50,000-won banknote, they placed Shin Saimdang’s portrait alongside her Mukpodo (Ink Grapes), her Chochungdo eggplants and watermelons, while her son Yulgok’s 5,000-won banknote carries her watermelon-and-cockscomb Chochungdo on its reverse!

Stand in the quiet courtyard of Ojukheon in Gangneung today as the wind clatters through the glossy black bamboo stalks outside Mongnyongsil. Look down at the garden border where summer eggplants, daylilies, and dianthus pinks still bloom: five hundred years after chickens pecked at her drying silk screen, Shin Saimdang still teaches us that genius begins by looking closely at the smallest living things at our feet.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Entomological Realism & 'Molgolbeop' Pigment Layering in Chochungdo

Art-historical and scientific analyses of Shin Saimdang's Chochungdo screens (such as Treasure No. 595) highlight her mastery of combining fine-line contour drawing (gureukbeop) with 'boneless' translucent mineral pigment washes (molgolbeop). By placing the primary botanical subject—an eggplant, watermelon, or poppy—along the central vertical axis for visual stability while arranging insects, amphibians, and field mice in dynamic diagonal trajectories around the perimeter, she infused classical still-life painting with living ecological motion.

2. Social History: How 16th-Century Matrilocal Residence Enabled Female Genius

Historians of Korean gender and family law point out that Shin Saimdang's artistic flourishing at Ojukheon was directly supported by the early-to-mid Joseon legal custom of Namgwi-yeogahon (matrilocal marriage residence) and equal property inheritance between daughters and sons. Living for nearly two decades after marriage at her maternal family home in Gangneung with the support of her parents and husband Yi Won-su gave Saimdang the studio autonomy, library access, and financial independence to paint and write.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three terms in mind: first, Chochungdo (Grass and Insect Paintings), Saimdang's signature genre celebrating kitchen-garden flora and fauna alongside auspicious symbols of fertility; second, Ojukheon (Treasure No. 165), the fifteenth-century black-bamboo hanok in Gangneung where both Saimdang and her son Yulgok were born; and third, Mongnyongsil, the room where Saimdang dreamed of a black dragon rising from the East Sea before giving birth to Yulgok in 1536.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Take the KTX train to Gangneung and visit Ojukheon & Gangneung Municipal Museum in Jukheon-dong, where you can walk through the black-bamboo grove, inspect the 1452 wooden joinery of Mongnyongsil and Munseongsa Shrine, and view Saimdang's Chochungdo screens and calligraphy in the exhibition hall. In Seoul, see her Treasure No. 595 screen at the National Museum of Korea in Yongsan.
