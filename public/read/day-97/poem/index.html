Nightly Poem • War & Witness Poetry

Rain

Edward Thomas (1916) • 3 min read

Lying awake in a military training hut listening to wild midnight rain, a soldier-poet prays that none whom he once loved is dying tonight.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Edward Thomas wrote "Rain" on the seventh of January, 1916, in a cold army hut in Essex, where he was training as a map-reading instructor. He was thirty-seven, a corporal, and a very new poet. A little over a year earlier he had been a worn-out freelance writer who had never published a poem. Now he was producing some of the finest lyrics of the First World War, even though he would not reach the front until early 1917. He was killed at Arras that April. "Rain" matters because it looks directly at the thought of dying without any flag-waving or anger. The speaker lies awake listening to a storm, thinks about death, and finds that the rain has washed away every love he has except one. In a sentence, the poem is a midnight meditation on loneliness, mortality and the strange comfort of an ending that cannot disappoint.

Rain, midnight rain, nothing but the wild rain

On this bleak hut, and solitude, and me

Remembering again that I shall die

And neither hear the rain nor give it thanks

For washing me cleaner than I have been

Since I was born into this solitude.

Blessed are the dead that the rain rains upon:

But here I pray that none whom once I loved

Is dying to-night or lying still awake

Solitary, listening to the rain,

Either in pain or thus in sympathy

Helpless among the living and the dead,

Like a cold water among broken reeds,

Myriads of broken reeds all still and stiff,

Like me who have no love which this wild rain

Has not dissolved except the love of death,

If love it be towards what is perfect and

Cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a single sentence-like stream of thought, eighteen lines long, spoken by a man alone in a hut at midnight. Rain is pounding on the roof, and everything else has fallen away. Thomas opens with the word three times in eight words, "Rain, midnight rain, nothing but the wild rain", and the effect is of someone who can hear nothing else, who is being drummed into silence. The place is "bleak", the situation is "solitude", and the only company is the speaker's own mind.

His mind goes straight to death. He is "remembering again that I shall die", and the word "again" is telling. This is not a sudden shock. It is a thought he returns to, the way a tongue returns to a sore tooth. He imagines a future in which he can "neither hear the rain nor give it thanks". The odd phrase "give it thanks" shows that he feels the rain has done him a kindness. It is "washing me cleaner than I have been since I was born into this solitude". The rain has stripped away habit, pretence and the clutter of ordinary life, leaving him bare.

Then comes a line that sounds like scripture and is also a grim joke: "Blessed are the dead that the rain rains upon." It echoes old sayings about the weather at funerals and the language of the Bible's blessings. But the poem immediately turns away from the dead and toward the living. He prays that nobody he loves is "dying to-night or lying still awake" listening to the rain. That is a remarkable shift. The same storm that comforts him might torment someone else, and he hopes they are spared.

The middle of the poem contains its strangest image. He compares himself, or his helplessness, to "a cold water among broken reeds, myriads of broken reeds all still and stiff". Water in a field of broken reeds is a picture of something moving among things that cannot move. Many readers hear the dead of the war in those stiff reeds, although the poem never says so, and his own body is soon among them.

The close is the real surprise. He admits he has "no love which this wild rain has not dissolved except the love of death". Every other attachment has been rinsed away. What remains is a love of death, and even that he qualifies, "if love it be", because he is not sure it deserves the word. Death is "perfect" and "cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint". The final idea is both bleak and oddly consoling. Nothing in life is certain not to let us down, and death, the poem says, is the one thing that will keep its promise. Thomas does not endorse this. He reports it, as a feeling that the night produces.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The most obvious achievement is how the form carries the feeling. The poem is in blank verse, ten-syllable lines without rhyme, and it is written as one long, unbroken stanza. There is no pause for breath, no tidy division into verses, and that gives the poem the sense of a thought that cannot be stopped. The sentences run across the line breaks, and the clauses pile up, "Either in pain or thus in sympathy, helpless among the living and the dead", until the reader feels something of the pressure that the speaker feels. The repetition of the word rain at the opening works like the tapping on the roof, and it sets a hypnotic tone that never fully lifts.

The vocabulary is also extremely well judged. Almost every word is short and plain, and the few rarer ones, "myriads", "dissolved", "tempest", arrive at moments of the greatest emotional stress, so that they stand out. The word "dissolved" is a particular triumph. It is a rain word, and it is also a word for what love does under pressure, so the image and the idea become one thing. In the final line, "cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint", the storm itself becomes a speaker, and the whole poem lands on a note of quiet, almost cheerful finality.

There is real honesty in the attitude too. The speaker never glorifies death or presents it as a heroic goal. He calls it a love only hesitantly, and he cannot say whether "love" is the right word for his feeling toward something "perfect". That uncertainty is the heart of the poem and a sign of Thomas's integrity as a writer.

On the weaker side, readers sometimes find the poem too dark, or too passive. It offers no action, no consolation and no argument, and a reader who wants the poem to do something may find it bleak to the point of numbness. The syntax of the middle section is also hard. The simile of cold water among broken reeds is not at all easy to picture, and readers disagree about what it is meant to show. Is the speaker the water or the reeds? Is he among the living or the dead? The poem leans on that ambiguity, but a few readers think it tips into vagueness. The scriptural echo of "Blessed are the dead" may also feel heavy to some, a touch of solemnity in a poem that is otherwise unadorned.

It is also fair to say that the poem asks a lot of the reader. It is compressed, ambiguous and somewhat abstract, with very little physical detail beyond the rain and the hut. Still, these are the costs of its strengths. The poem is a rare thing, a lyric of pure inward weather, and few poets of any period have caught so accurately the mood of a mind alone at night with its own mortality.

III. The Impact It Had

Like the rest of Thomas's work, "Rain" had almost no public impact in his lifetime. He wrote it in January 1916 and it was gathered into his Poems, which appeared in 1917 under the name Edward Eastaway, shortly after his death. For a while it sat quietly in a book that few people read. Its influence is a story of slow recognition, a reputation built by other poets who found in it something they needed.

One of the earliest signs of the poem's weight is that it marked a turning point within Thomas's own work. By January 1916 he had been writing verse for only a little over a year, but the poems were growing darker and more direct. "Rain" is often described as the point at which his preoccupation with death and solitude became unmistakable. Edna Longley, who edited his poems, has treated it as among the most intense things he wrote, and it is frequently cited in studies of his career.

Among later poets, the influence is wide. In the middle of the twentieth century, writers who distrusted rhetoric and wanted a poetry of plain speech turned to Thomas as an ancestor. Philip Larkin helped bring his work to a larger readership by giving him a generous place in the Oxford Book of Twentieth Century English Verse in 1973, and the poem fits easily beside Larkin's own meditations on death, such as "Aubade". Ted Hughes and Seamus Heaney wrote about Thomas with respect, and the Welsh poet R. S. Thomas, no relation, is often mentioned as a kindred spirit in his handling of solitude and weather. Contemporary poets still cite "Rain" when they talk about how to write about death without melodrama.

The poem has also shaped how the First World War is remembered. For many years the standard picture of war poetry was written from the trenches. Thomas, who wrote "Rain" in a training camp in England, helped to expand that picture. His work shows that the war had an interior front too, in the minds of men waiting to be sent, and that the anticipation of death can be as poetically powerful as the sight of it. School syllabuses in Britain now often pair "Rain" with the more famous trench poems for exactly this reason.

It has been adapted too. Composers have set Thomas's poetry, and the poem is a common choice at memorial readings, where its mixture of rain, loneliness and acceptance suits the occasion. It has also been a favourite for recitation at the Edward Thomas Fellowship gatherings, where admirers walk the countryside he loved. Those uses are modest, but they reflect the way the poem has travelled, quietly, from page to page and from voice to voice, rather than through any single dramatic moment.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who love "Rain" usually begin by describing a mood rather than a meaning. They talk about the feeling of being awake in the small hours, listening to weather, with the mind drifting toward things it normally avoids. The poem captures that state so exactly that people recognize it at once, even if they have never been in an army hut. Many say it is the best poem they know about insomnia and loneliness.

Another source of affection is its unflinching honesty. The poem does not pretend that death can be argued away, and it does not dress the thought in noble language. It says, in effect, that the speaker has thought about dying and has found it, in a strange way, restful. Readers who have faced illness or grief often say that the poem gave them permission to feel something they had been ashamed of. The courage lies in how plainly the feeling is stated.

The craft has also won admiration. Poets, in particular, love the way the poem seems to be saying one thing while the sounds are saying another. The soft repeated syllables of the opening, the long, flowing sentence, and the sudden hardness of "stiff" and "broken" in the middle give a physical sense of drizzle turning to cold. People often mention the final line as one of the great endings in English verse. "Cannot, the tempest tells me, disappoint" is quiet, but it has the force of a door closing.

The poem is also loved for the way it widens. In the first six lines it is about one man; by line eight it is thinking about everyone he has ever loved; by line twelve it holds the living and the dead together. Readers sense a growing circle of compassion even inside a poem about isolation. The prayer that none of his loved ones is lying awake is, for many, the most moving moment, because it shows a man in despair still thinking of others.

Fellow writers have been generous. Edna Longley has called attention to its intensity, and many modern poets name it among their favourites. There is a particular affection for the way it sits in a small book by a writer who died young. Readers know that Thomas would be killed little more than a year after writing it, and that knowledge colours every reading. It is hard not to hear the poem as a kind of premonition, although it is wise to remember that he wrote it as a man who might equally have survived. The tenderness readers feel for the poem is partly tenderness for him, and partly the plain relief of finding a poem that tells the truth about a hard night.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

As with the rest of Thomas's work, there was little immediate criticism of "Rain" because there was little immediate readership. The first reviews of his poems were respectful but muted, and for decades his place in English poetry remained uncertain. The pushback came later, and it came in several forms.

The most basic objection was one of style. When modernism took hold in the 1920s, critics who admired Eliot and Pound found the Georgian manner too gentle and too English. Thomas was often lumped in with poets who wrote about fields and lanes and who seemed to have missed the shattering effects of the war. "Rain" is not a typical Georgian poem, but in the eyes of some critics it carried the same faults of quietness and restraint. They felt it did not do enough with language and did not confront the age.

A second objection concerns the poem's attitude to death. Some readers have found its "love of death" troubling, even morbid. Is the poem inviting us to find death attractive? Does the speaker, who was about to go to war, romanticize his own end? These questions deserve a serious answer. Defenders point out that the speaker hedges the phrase, "if love it be", and that he is describing a mood of exhaustion and not a plan. But the discomfort is real, and some critics have felt that the final lines come too close to a death wish. Others note that the poem was written in a period of Thomas's life when he suffered from deep depressions, and they read it with that in mind.

There are also literary objections. The middle of the poem is hard to follow, and the simile of cold water among broken reeds has been called obscure and strained. A few critics say the biblical echo of "Blessed are the dead" is too neat, or that it places a borrowed phrase at the centre of an otherwise original poem. Others find the whole poem static. It does not move toward any event, and for readers who expect a lyric to arrive somewhere, the final turn to death can seem less like a conclusion than a stopping point.

Finally, there is the argument about whether the poem belongs in the category of war poetry at all. Some scholars object to anthologies that present it as a war poem when the war is never mentioned, while others argue that its silence is the point. That debate has no neat resolution. What most modern critics agree on is that the objections are largely matters of taste, and that the poem's reputation has grown steadily as readers have come to value the very qualities, understatement and inwardness, that earlier critics resisted.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place "Rain" in literary history, it helps to think of three overlapping traditions. The first is English nature poetry, from Wordsworth through Hardy, in which weather and landscape reflect the inner life. The second is the elegy, the poem that mourns the dead, and the third is the meditation on death that runs from the Bible and the Renaissance through Keats and beyond. Thomas's poem sits where these three meet, but it is a lyric in which nature does not console. The rain is not a symbol of renewal. It is a presence that dissolves.

Among the poem's ancestors, one of the most interesting is Hardy, whose bleak and honest verse about time and loss Thomas greatly admired. Another is Keats, whose "Ode to a Nightingale" contains the famous line about being "half in love with easeful Death". Thomas's "love of death" is a harder, more tentative cousin of that feeling. Without Keats's rich language, Thomas arrives at a similar thought, and the contrast is instructive. Keats is intoxicated, while Thomas is merely tired and clear-eyed.

Among his contemporaries, Wilfred Owen and Isaac Rosenberg wrote of death with extraordinary power, and comparisons with them are natural. Where Owen documents the physical horror and the pity of war, Thomas describes the mind's quiet approach to its own ending. It is a different register, and a necessary one. Alongside Robert Frost, whose poems of stark solitude, such as "Desert Places," share Thomas's candour about emptiness, he belongs to a modern tradition of the plain-spoken lyric about fear.

Looking forward, "Rain" feeds into the British poetry of understatement that runs through Larkin, Hughes and Heaney, and into the many contemporary poems that deal with grief and mortality in an ordinary voice. It is also a forerunner of the confessional impulse in poetry, the willingness to say what it is like to be alone with the thought of dying, though it achieves this without the self-display that sometimes marks the later movement.

Why read it now? Partly because the experience it describes is permanent. Everyone, at some point, lies awake and thinks about the end. Few poems keep us such quiet company on those nights. And partly because it models a way of facing difficult feeling. It does not look away, and it does not shout. It lets the rain fall, and it tells the truth about what the speaker hears in it. In eighteen lines, it shows that a short poem written in a cold hut can say something that long books on the subject never quite manage.
