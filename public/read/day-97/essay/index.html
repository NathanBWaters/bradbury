Meditations, Book III: Written at Carnuntum

Marcus Aurelius (c. 173 AD) • 15 min read

Never value anything as profitable to thyself which shall compel thee to break thy promise, to lose thy self-respect, to hate any man, to suspect, to curse, to act the hypocrite.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Book Three of Marcus Aurelius's private notebook is traditionally marked as written at Carnuntum, a Roman fortress on the Danube, not far from modern Vienna. Marcus was emperor from 161 to 180, and in these years he was commanding armies against Germanic tribes while a terrible plague, which historians now call the Antonine Plague, ravaged the empire. He wrote in Greek, to himself, in the plain and demanding language of Stoicism, a school of philosophy that said the only thing truly yours is the quality of your own thinking and choices. These notes were never meant for readers, which is a large part of why they are trusted. Book Three is one of the most concentrated of the twelve. It asks how a person should spend a life that is shrinking by the day, and it answers with an odd mix of death, bread crust, and duty. Its meaning, stated simply, is this: you may not have long, your mind will not last forever, so be good and clear and calm now.

We ought to consider not only that our life is daily wasting away and a smaller part of it is left, but another thing also must be taken into the account, that if a man should live longer, it is quite uncertain whether the understanding will still continue sufficient for the comprehension of things, and retain the power of contemplation which strives to acquire the knowledge of the divine and the human. For if he shall begin to fall into dotage, perspiration and nutrition and imagination and appetite, and whatever else there is of the kind, will not fail; but the power of making use of ourselves, and filling up the measure of our duty, and clearly separating all appearances, and considering whether a man should now depart from life, and whatever else of the kind absolutely requires a disciplined reason, all this is already extinguished. We must make haste then, not only because we are daily nearer to death, but also because the conception of things and the understanding of them cease first.

We ought to observe also that even the things which follow after the things which are produced according to nature contain something pleasing and attractive. For instance, when bread is baked some parts are split at the surface, and these parts which thus open, and have a certain fashion contrary to the purpose of the baker’s art, are beautiful in a manner, and in a peculiar way excite a desire for eating. And again, figs, when they are quite ripe, gape open; and in the ripe olives the very circumstance of their being near to rottenness adds a peculiar beauty to the fruit. And the ears of corn bending down, and the lion’s eyebrows, and the foam which flows from the mouth of wild boars, and many other things⁠—though they are far from being beautiful, if a man should examine them severally⁠—still, because they are consequent upon the things which are formed by nature, help to adorn them, and they please the mind; so that if a man should have a feeling and deeper insight with respect to the things which are produced in the universe, there is hardly one of those which follow by way of consequence which will not seem to him to be in a manner disposed so as to give pleasure. And so he will see even the real gaping jaws of wild beasts with no less pleasure than those which painters and sculptors show by imitation; and in an old woman and an old man he will be able to see a certain maturity and comeliness; and the attractive loveliness of young persons he will be able to look on with chaste eyes; and many such things will present themselves, not pleasing to every man, but to him only who has become truly familiar with nature and her works.

Hippocrates after curing many diseases himself fell sick and died. The Chaldæi foretold the deaths of many, and then fate caught them too. Alexander, and Pompeius, and Caius Caesar, after so often completely destroying whole cities, and in battle cutting to pieces many ten thousands of cavalry and infantry, themselves too at last departed from life. Heraclitus, after so many speculations on the conflagration of the universe, was filled with water internally and died smeared all over with mud. And lice destroyed Democritus; and other lice killed Socrates. What means all this? Thou hast embarked, thou hast made the voyage, thou art come to shore; get out. If indeed to another life, there is no want of gods, not even there. But if to a state without sensation, thou wilt cease to be held by pains and pleasures, and to be a slave to the vessel, which is as much inferior as that which serves it is superior: for the one is intelligence and deity; the other is earth and corruption.

Do not waste the remainder of thy life in thoughts about others, when thou dost not refer thy thoughts to some object of common utility. For thou losest the opportunity of doing something else when thou hast such thoughts as these, What is such a person doing, and why, and what is he saying, and what is he thinking of, and what is he contriving, and whatever else of the kind makes us wander away from the observation of our own ruling power. We ought then to check in the series of our thoughts everything that is without a purpose and useless, but most of all the overcurious feeling and the malignant; and a man should use himself to think of those things only about which if one should suddenly ask, What hast thou now in thy thoughts? With perfect openness thou mightest, immediately answer, This or That; so that from thy words it should be plain that everything in thee is simple and benevolent, and such as befits a social animal, and one that cares not for thoughts about pleasure or sensual enjoyments at all, nor has any rivalry or envy and suspicion, or anything else for which thou wouldst blush if thou shouldst say that thou hadst it in thy mind. For the man who is such and no longer delays being among the number of the best, is like a priest and minister of the gods, using too the deity which is planted within him, which makes the man uncontaminated by pleasure, unharmed by any pain, untouched by any insult, feeling no wrong, a fighter in the noblest fight, one who cannot be overpowered by any passion, dyed deep with justice, accepting with all his soul everything which happens and is assigned to him as his portion; and not often, nor yet without great necessity and for the general interest, imagining what another says, or does, or thinks. For it is only what belongs to himself that he makes the matter for his activity; and he constantly thinks of that which is allotted to himself out of the sum total of things, and he makes his own acts fair, and he is persuaded that his own portion is good. For the lot which is assigned to each man is carried along with him and carries him along with it. And he remembers also that every rational animal is his kinsman, and that to care for all men is according to man’s nature; and a man should hold on to the opinion not of all, but of those only who confessedly live according to nature. But as to those who live not so, he always bears in mind what kind of men they are both at home and from home, both by night and by day, and what they are, and with what men they live an impure life. Accordingly, he does not value at all the praise which comes from such men, since they are not even satisfied with themselves.

Labour not unwillingly, nor without regard to the common interest, nor without due consideration, nor with distraction; nor let studied ornament set off thy thoughts, and be not either a man of many words, or busy about too many things. And further, let the deity which is in thee be the guardian of a living being, manly and of ripe age, and engaged in matter political, and a Roman, and a ruler, who has taken his post like a man waiting for the signal which summons him from life, and ready to go, having need neither of oath nor of any man’s testimony. Be cheerful also, and seek not external help nor the tranquility which others give. A man then must stand erect, not be kept erect by others.

If thou findest in human life anything better than justice, truth, temperance, fortitude, and, in a word, anything better than thy own mind’s self-satisfaction in the things which it enables thee to do according to right reason, and in the condition that is assigned to thee without thy own choice; if, I say, thou seest anything better than this, turn to it with all thy soul, and enjoy that which thou hast found to be the best. But if nothing appears to be better than the deity which is planted in thee, which has subjected to itself all thy appetites, and carefully examines all the impressions, and, as Socrates said, has detached itself from the persuasions of sense, and has submitted itself to the gods, and cares for mankind; if thou findest everything else smaller and of less value than this, give place to nothing else, for if thou dost once diverge and incline to it, thou wilt no longer without distraction be able to give the preference to that good thing which is thy proper possession and thy own; for it is not right that anything of any other kind, such as praise from the many, or power, or enjoyment of pleasure, should come into competition with that which is rationally and politically or practically good. All these things, even though they may seem to adapt themselves to the better things in a small degree, obtain the superiority all at once, and carry us away. But do thou, I say, simply and freely choose the better, and hold to it.⁠—But that which is useful is the better.⁠—Well then, if it is useful to thee as a rational being, keep to it; but if it is only useful to thee as an animal, say so, and maintain thy judgement without arrogance: only take care that thou makest the inquiry by a sure method.

Never value anything as profitable to thyself which shall compel thee to break thy promise, to lose thy self-respect, to hate any man, to suspect, to curse, to act the hypocrite, to desire anything which needs walls and curtains: for he who has preferred to everything intelligence and daemon and the worship of its excellence, acts no tragic part, does not groan, will not need either solitude or much company; and, what is chief of all, he will live without either pursuing or flying from death; but whether for a longer or a shorter time he shall have the soul enclosed in the body, he cares not at all: for even if he must depart immediately, he will go as readily as if he were going to do anything else which can be done with decency and order; taking care of this only all through life, that his thoughts turn not away from anything which belongs to an intelligent animal and a member of a civil community.

In the mind of one who is chastened and purified thou wilt find no corrupt matter, nor impurity, nor any sore skinned over. Nor is his life incomplete when fate overtakes him, as one may say of an actor who leaves the stage before ending and finishing the play. Besides, there is in him nothing servile, nor affected, nor too closely bound to other things, nor yet detached from other things, nothing worthy of blame, nothing which seeks a hiding-place.

Reverence the faculty which produces opinion. On this faculty it entirely depends whether there shall exist in thy ruling part any opinion inconsistent with nature and the constitution of the rational animal. And this faculty promises freedom from hasty judgement, and friendship towards men, and obedience to the gods.

Throwing away then all things, hold to these only which are few; and besides bear in mind that every man lives only this present time, which is an indivisible point, and that all the rest of his life is either past or it is uncertain. Short then is the time which every man lives, and small the nook of the earth where he lives; and short too the longest posthumous fame, and even this only continued by a succession of poor human beings, who will very soon die, and who know not even themselves, much less him who died long ago.

To the aids which have been mentioned let this one still be added:⁠—Make for thyself a definition or description of the thing which is presented to thee, so as to see distinctly what kind of a thing it is in its substance, in its nudity, in its complete entirety, and tell thyself its proper name, and the names of the things of which it has been compounded, and into which it will be resolved. For nothing is so productive of elevation of mind as to be able to examine methodically and truly every object which is presented to thee in life, and always to look at things so as to see at the same time what kind of universe this is, and what kind of use everything performs in it, and what value everything has with reference to the whole, and what with reference to man, who is a citizen of the highest city, of which all other cities are like families; what each thing is, and of what it is composed, and how long it is the nature of this thing to endure which now makes an impression on me, and what virtue I have need of with respect to it, such as gentleness, manliness, truth, fidelity, simplicity, contentment, and the rest. Wherefore, on every occasion a man should say: this comes from God; and this is according to the apportionment and spinning of the thread of destiny, and suchlike coincidence and chance; and this is from one of the same stock, and a kinsman and partner, one who knows not however what is according to his nature. But I know; for this reason I behave towards him according to the natural law of fellowship with benevolence and justice. At the same time however in things indifferent I attempt to ascertain the value of each.

If thou workest at that which is before thee, following right reason seriously, vigorously, calmly, without allowing anything else to distract thee, but keeping thy divine part pure, as if thou shouldst be bound to give it back immediately; if thou holdest to this, expecting nothing, fearing nothing, but satisfied with thy present activity according to nature, and with heroic truth in every word and sound which thou utterest, thou wilt live happy. And there is no man who is able to prevent this.

As physicians have always their instruments and knives ready for cases which suddenly require their skill, so do thou have principles ready for the understanding of things divine and human, and for doing everything, even the smallest, with a recollection of the bond which unites the divine and human to one another. For neither wilt thou do anything well which pertains to man without at the same time having a reference to things divine; nor the contrary.

No longer wander at hazard; for neither wilt thou read thy own memoirs, nor the acts of the ancient Romans and Hellenes, and the selections from books which thou wast reserving for thy old age. Hasten then to the end which thou hast before thee, and throwing away idle hopes, come to thy own aid, if thou carest at all for thyself, while it is in thy power.

They know not how many things are signified by the words stealing, sowing, buying, keeping quiet, seeing what ought to be done; for this is not effected by the eyes, but by another kind of vision.

Body, soul, intelligence: to the body belong sensations, to the soul appetites, to the intelligence principles. To receive the impressions of forms by means of appearances belongs even to animals; to be pulled by the strings of desire belongs both to wild beasts and to men who have made themselves into women, and to a Phalaris and a Nero: and to have the intelligence that guides to the things which appear suitable belongs also to those who do not believe in the gods, and who betray their country, and do their impure deeds when they have shut the doors. If then everything else is common to all that I have mentioned, there remains that which is peculiar to the good man, to be pleased and content with what happens, and with the thread which is spun for him; and not to defile the divinity which is planted in his breast, nor disturb it by a crowd of images, but to preserve it tranquil, following it obediently as a god, neither saying anything contrary to the truth, nor doing anything contrary to justice. And if all men refuse to believe that he lives a simple, modest, and contented life, he is neither angry with any of them, nor does he deviate from the way which leads to the end of life, to which a man ought to come pure, tranquil, ready to depart, and without any compulsion perfectly reconciled to his lot.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Book Three begins with a hard fact. Life is wasting away a little every day, and there is a second problem layered on top of it. Even if we live long, our minds may not. Marcus points out that the body can go on breathing, eating and imagining while the sharper power of reason, the ability to judge duty and to decide whether it is time to leave life, may already be gone. So there is an urgency. We must hurry, he says, not only because death is closer each day but because understanding fails before life does. This is not morbid for its own sake. It is a deadline, and it is meant to make the reader act.

Then the book does something unexpected and lovely. Marcus turns to the beauty of things that are not pretty. Bread cracks open as it bakes, in a way the baker did not plan, yet it looks appetizing. Ripe figs split. Olives near rottenness have a special beauty. Ears of grain bend, a lion's brow frowns, foam drips from a boar's mouth. Taken one by one, he admits, such things are far from beautiful, but as consequences of nature's workings they please the mind. The person with a deep feeling for nature will see even the gaping jaws of real beasts with no less pleasure than painted ones, and will see a certain ripeness in old men and women. This is Marcus training himself to see the natural process, including aging and decay, as beautiful rather than frightening.

Next comes a roll call of the dead. Hippocrates, the great physician, healed many and then fell ill and died. Astrologers predicted other people's deaths and then fate caught them. Alexander, Pompey and Caesar, who destroyed whole cities, all departed life too. The philosopher Heraclitus, who had speculated about the world ending in fire, died swollen with water and covered in mud, and lice destroyed Democritus, and other lice killed Socrates. The grim humor is deliberate. Then comes the famous push: you have embarked, made the voyage, come to shore, so get out. If there is another life, the gods are there too. If not, you will be free of pain and pleasure.

The rest is about attention. Marcus warns himself not to waste his remaining life wondering what other people are doing and thinking and scheming. He wants a mind so clean that if anyone asked what he was thinking, he could answer openly. The final portrait is of the good man, who is like a priest of the gods, unharmed by pain, untouched by insult, a fighter in the noblest fight. Strikingly, Marcus says that many things are shared by good and bad alike, such as following impressions and being pulled by desire. What belongs only to the good man is contentment with what happens, a tranquil inner divinity, and a refusal to say what is false or do what is unjust. Underneath the cosmic language, he is saying that character is the one thing you can build, and it is enough.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Book Three is among the strongest books of the Meditations, and the reason is its balance. Many of the entries elsewhere are drily doctrinal. Here Marcus keeps a firm hold on the concrete. The bread, the figs and the olives are astonishing in context. A man threatened by plague and war, who is meditating on death, stops to look at the cracks in a loaf. That detail does more to convince us of his serenity than any abstract claim about nature being good. It also shows a real aesthetic mind. He is not merely preaching acceptance, he is demonstrating how to see in a way that makes acceptance natural.

The list of dead heroes works similarly. It is a rhetorical device as old as moral writing, but Marcus gives it a mocking edge that stops it from being solemn. The image of lice killing Socrates, the greatest of philosophers, deflates both grandeur and fear at once. The directness of get out, after the voyage metaphor, has a sharp rhythm. It is the kind of line a reader remembers precisely because it refuses to be consoling in the usual way.

There are, however, weaknesses. The argument from decaying fruit to the beauty of all natural consequences is charming but not rigorous. One can ask whether the beauty of a split fig really extends to disease, famine or the suffering of the plague victims around him. The reasoning slides, perhaps too easily, from what is pleasant to what is acceptable. A skeptical modern reader may feel that this is aesthetic consolation standing in for moral answers.

The book is also uneven in structure. The opening on the failing mind, the beauty passage, the roll call of the dead and the praise of the good man are different notes, strung together without strong transitions. This is the nature of a private notebook, but it means the book rewards rereading more than a first pass. Some of the language about the divinity planted in the breast is formulaic Stoic vocabulary, and unless you are sympathetic to the system it can feel like boilerplate.

There is also a faint note of superiority in the picture of the good man. Marcus describes the person who sees nature rightly as having a deeper insight that is not pleasing to every man. That hint of an elite of the wise sits uneasily with his insistence elsewhere that all people share in reason. Still, the whole is persuasive because it is so evidently sincere. Where lesser moralists would simply instruct, Marcus tells us what he is trying to feel, and his effort is what we trust. The final sentences, in which the good man comes to the end of life pure, tranquil and ready to depart, are not ornate, but they earn their calm.

III. The Impact It Had

Book Three has been influential less as an individual unit and more as a section that readers extract and quote. Because the Meditations survives as twelve books of loosely connected notes, later readers have often browsed it, and Book Three offers some of the most quotable passages. The idea that the mind may fail before the body is a staple of discussions of aging. The image of the cracked loaf and the ripe fig is a favorite among writers who want to praise imperfection in nature.

On the history of ideas, it belongs to the larger story of how the Meditations carried Stoic thought across the centuries. The first printed edition appeared in 1559, and English translations by Meric Casaubon in 1634 and George Long in 1862 made it a standard of moral reading. Long's version is the one used here. The Victorian critic Matthew Arnold's essay of 1863 celebrated Marcus as a noble pagan, and many readers of that era carried the book as a kind of secular scripture. Within that history, passages about death and duty, such as the hurried tone of Book Three, helped establish the popular image of Marcus as a solemn, tired, honest emperor.

In the twentieth century the French philosopher Pierre Hadot, in The Inner Citadel, used passages like these to argue that the Meditations are spiritual exercises, techniques of attention performed repeatedly. Book Three's training in seeing beauty in unlovely things, and its drill in keeping one's thoughts open to inspection, fit that view neatly. Hadot's reading was important in persuading scholars that ancient philosophy was a practice rather than just a set of theories, which helped inspire a modern interest in philosophy as a way of life.

The text also feeds modern conversations about mortality. Writers on death and dying, doctors and chaplains, and people reflecting on aging have drawn on Marcus's idea that facing the end clearly frees us to live. The recent popularity of Stoicism, in books, podcasts and workshops, has spread lines from the Meditations widely, and Book Three's call to stop spending thoughts on what other people are doing is a frequent source for talks on digital distraction and envy, though that is our application, not his.

In culture, the broad figure of Marcus has been shaped by this sort of material. The 2000 film Gladiator drew on the popular image of a thoughtful, weary philosopher emperor, though its plot is fiction. Book Three itself did not change politics or law. Its impact is quiet and personal, passed along by readers who find in it a way to look at their own decline without panic. That kind of influence is hard to measure, but it has been remarkably durable.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers love most in Book Three is its mixture of courage and tenderness. It is a book about dying that is not afraid, yet it is not cold. The passage about bread and figs and olives is widely cherished because it shows a mind taking pleasure in small, odd, ordinary beauties. People who think of Stoicism as grim are often surprised to find an ancient emperor delighting in the cracks on a loaf. It makes the philosophy feel human.

Readers also respond to its honesty about the mind's fragility. The fear of mental decline is common to many, and here is a voice from nearly two thousand years ago naming it without flinching. Marcus's insistence that we must use our powers now is felt as a gift, not a threat. Those caring for aging parents, or facing their own aging, often find in this passage a strange mixture of sadness and clarity. The text says plainly that time and mental strength are limited, and then asks what we will do with them.

The humor is another source of affection. The deaths of great men are listed with a dry wit that releases tension. A reader steeped in fear of mortality can laugh at the lice, and the laughter loosens something. The line about having embarked, made the voyage and come to shore, with the curt instruction to get out, has been admired for being both blunt and kind. It treats death as an arrival, not a catastrophe.

There is admiration, too, for the ideal of an open mind. The test of whether you could answer openly if someone asked what you were thinking is a practical standard. It appeals to people who want to live without hidden envy, scheming or shame, and it is easy to try. Many readers say they use it as a daily check. It is a good example of how Marcus converts large philosophy into small, usable acts.

Writers and teachers have also loved the book's rhythm of attention. It moves from time, to nature, to history, to conscience, and then to the portrait of a person at peace, as though building a ladder of thought. Readers feel they have been taken somewhere by the end. And finally, there is the cumulative impression of the author himself, a busy, sick, burdened ruler who pauses to look at bread. That picture of a powerful man choosing humility, decency and quiet wonder in the middle of a long war is what keeps people returning. The book gives them permission to treat their own short lives as something that can still be shaped with care, even when circumstances are harsh and unlikely to improve.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because Book Three comes from a private notebook, there are no contemporary reviews of it, and honest discussion has to be about how later readers have reacted. Their objections tend to cluster around three issues.

The first is the move from beauty to acceptance. Critics point out that finding beauty in a cracked loaf is one thing and finding it in suffering is another. Marcus's claim that everything following from nature has something pleasing in it can look like aesthetic comfort offered in place of an answer to real evil. In an empire devastated by plague and constantly at war, some readers find it disconcerting that the emperor meditates on the loveliness of ripe figs. Defenders respond that the whole point is training perception, not denying pain, but the objection has force, especially for readers who have watched loved ones die badly.

The second is the attitude to death and the afterlife. Marcus remains agnostic, saying that either there is another life, with gods present, or a state without sensation. Some readers admire this honesty, while others find the argument too neat, like a coin flip where both sides are made to look good. Religious critics, particularly in later Christian tradition, have sometimes treated Marcus as a noble pagan whose philosophy lacks real hope. Others, from the opposite direction, find his talk of divinity in the breast too mystical.

The third concerns detachment from other people. Marcus warns himself not to spend thought on what others are doing, saying and plotting. Some commentators read this as healthy attention to what he can control. Others worry that it encourages a kind of withdrawal, an unwillingness to be curious about, or responsible for, other people's suffering. That worry is complicated by Marcus's own insistence elsewhere that humans are made for cooperation, which suggests he meant something narrower, a refusal to waste energy on gossip and suspicion.

Beyond the passage itself, critics of the author as a ruler shape how this book is received. His reign included persecution of Christians in some provinces, and he chose his unfit son Commodus as heir. Readers sometimes say that a book of exalted calm is easier to write than to live. The honest scholarly reply is that we cannot excuse his failings, and that the Meditations should be read as a record of an aspiration rather than a proof of achievement.

Lastly, some scholars question whether the order and context claimed by the subscriptions, such as Carnuntum, is reliable. The notes at the ends of the books were added by someone, perhaps not Marcus, and the books may not have been composed in the order we have them. That uncertainty is a reminder to hold the historical frame loosely, while still taking the words seriously.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Book Three sits within Roman Stoicism, a philosophy that taught people to focus on what they can control and to see virtue as the only real good. The school began in Athens with Zeno of Citium, passed through Seneca, a statesman who wrote about death, anger and time, and Epictetus, a former slave whose lectures were recorded by his student Arrian. Marcus builds on all of them. The theme of using time well is deeply Senecan, since Seneca wrote a famous essay on the shortness of life. The practical drills for the mind come from Epictetus.

The meditation on death, however, has an even longer ancestry. In Plato's account, Socrates says that philosophy is a rehearsal for dying, and Marcus's list of dead heroes, with Socrates among them, echoes that tradition. The Epicureans, rivals of the Stoics, also taught that death should not be feared, though for different reasons, arguing that when we are dead we feel nothing. Marcus considers both options in the passage, which shows how Stoics could borrow from a competing view.

Later works carry the thread. Montaigne, the French essayist of the sixteenth century, wrote that to philosophize is to learn to die, borrowing from the ancients, and his essays share Marcus's habit of reflecting on small things, like the cracks in a loaf, to reach large truths. Pascal's notebooks on human frailty, and the diaries of religious writers who address themselves in the second person, belong to a similar family. In the modern era the book's attention to death and attention to ordinary beauty has parallels with writers who find consolation in nature, though they would not share Marcus's confidence in a rational cosmos.

Within the Meditations itself, Book Three is a kind of compact summary. It is shorter and sharper than Books Two and Four, and many readers use it as an entry point. Its mix of mortality, natural beauty, and moral resolve lays out the whole project of the work in miniature.

Why read it now? We live longer than people did in Marcus's time, which makes his worry about the mind outliving the body feel sharper, not weaker. We are also more distracted and more prone to wonder what everyone else is doing and thinking, which is exactly the habit he tells himself to drop. The book gives a plain framework for these modern problems, rooted in the experience of a man who had little time to spare and used it to practice seeing clearly. It is short enough to read in a quarter of an hour, and it may be one of the best quarter-hours in ancient literature.
