Heo Jun's Dongui Bogam (1613): Writing an Encyclopedia of Accessible Medicine in the Ashes of War

Korea — How a royal physician born of secondary status spent sixteen years through war and exile translating 600 native herbs into Hangul so commoners could heal themselves (Joseon Dynasty (1596–1613 / UNESCO Memory of the World, 2009)) • 20 min read

“In country villages ten thousand ri from the capital, people die with expensive Chinese prescriptions in their hands because they do not know that the healing herb grows right beside their own garden fence.” — Heo Jun, Dongui Bogam (1613)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Completed in 1610 and printed with woodblocks by the Royal Medical Bureau (Naeuiwon) in November 1613, the twenty-five-volume Dongui Bogam ('Principles and Practice of Eastern Medicine' / literally 'Precious Mirror of Eastern Medicine') was the first medical encyclopedia in history inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register (2009). Commissioned by King Seonjo in the wake of the devastating 1592–1598 Imjin War—which left Korea's population decimated by famine and epidemic typhus—and authored solo through exile by royal physician Heo Jun (1539–1615), the book revolutionized public health by reorganizing medicine around preventive care and translating over 600 medicinal plants into phonetic Hangul so ordinary villagers could gather remedies from their own hillsides.

Open Volume 22 (*Tang-aek-pyeon*, 'On Decoctions and Materia Medica') of the original **1613 woodblock edition** of the **Dongui Bogam** (동의보감, 東醫寶鑑, National Treasure No. 319), housed in the National Library of Korea and the Kyujanggak Archive in Seoul. Under the Classical Chinese heading for every single one of more than **637 botanical, mineral, and animal medicines**—from dandelion (*포공영*, *pogongyeong*) and plantain leaf (*차전자*, *chajeonja*) to balloon-flower root (*길경*, *gilgyeong*)—appears its everyday **native Korean vernacular name printed in phonetic Hangul** (*민들레*, *질경이*, *도라지*)! Why did **Heo Jun** (허준, 許浚, 1539–1615), Chief Royal Physician (*Su-ui*) of the Joseon Court, insist on printing humble Hangul plant names inside the kingdom’s most authoritative medical encyclopedia? Because Heo Jun had walked through the smoking ruins of the **1592–1598 Imjin War** and watched thousands of Korean peasants die of treatable fevers while clutching Classical Chinese prescriptions whose expensive imported ingredients (*Tang-jae*, 당재) could only be bought from Beijing merchants for silver! In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Joseon Dynasty (1596–1613 / UNESCO Memory of the World, 2009), the rise of Dongui Bogam (동의보감 1613 25-volume medical encyclopedia) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering Heo Jun (허준 royal physician of secondary seoeol status, 1539–1615) and Hyangyak & Hangul herb names (향약과 한글 약재명 native Korean botanical medicine) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Like engineer Jang Yeong-sil a century and a half earlier, Heo Jun knew what it meant to be an outsider in Joseon society. Born in 1539 into the distinguished Yangcheon Heo clan (growing up near Gayang-dong on the southern bank of the Han River in Seoul), Heo Jun was the son of a magistrate father and a concubine mother. Under Joseon’s rigid **Seoeol** (서얼, illegitimate/secondary son) discrimination laws, Heo Jun was barred for life from sitting for the prestigious literary civil service examination (*Mungwa*). Behind the surviving monuments and archival records of Gayang-dong in Seoul and Sancheong stood flesh-and-blood practitioners whose daily discipline rarely made headlines in royal annals. Turning instead to the technical examination (*Japgwa*) in **medicine** (*Uigwa*), where talent mattered more than pedigree, the young scholar studied classical texts and field botany with such ferocious intensity that by age thirty he was appointed to the **Naeuiwon** (내의원, Royal Dispensary) inside Changdeokgung and Gyeongbokgung Palaces. Before Heo Jun, Chinese medical encyclopedias organized books around abstract diseases or thousand-herb lists, leaving a village doctor bewildered when a patient walked in with a symptom. Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Medical Taxonomy: Organizing 25 Volumes Around 'Jeong-Gi-Sin' and Anatomy

Medical historians studying the 1613 Dongui Bogam highlight its structural breakthrough over Ming Dynasty compendia like the Bencao Gangmu. Instead of burying clinical symptoms under thousands of drug entries, Heo Jun structured Volumes 1–4 around internal physiology (Jeong essence, Gi vital energy, Sin mind/spirit) and Volumes 5–8 around external anatomy from head to toe, followed by pathology, pharmacology, and acupuncture—creating a logical diagnostic decision tree that any practitioner could navigate in minutes. Studying the structural interplay between Dongui Bogam (동의보감 1613 25-volume medical encyclopedia) and Heo Jun (허준 royal physician of secondary seoeol status, 1539–1615) within the context of the Joseon Dynasty (1596–1613 / UNESCO Memory of the World, 2009) demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint. Taken together, these insights from Gayang-dong in Seoul and Sancheong deepen our appreciation for the enduring legacy of Dongui Bogam (동의보감 1613 25-volume medical encyclopedia) and Heo Jun (허준 royal physician of secondary seoeol status, 1539–1615).

2. Public Health Democratization: 'Hyangyak' Native Herbs and 'Danbang' Single Remedies

Before the Dongui Bogam, aristocratic physicians prescribed imported Chinese botanicals (Tangjae) whose Classical Chinese names were incomprehensible to Korean farmers. By printing the phonetic Hangul vernacular name alongside the harvest season and local habitat for 637 native Korean herbs (hyangyak) and concluding each disease chapter with Danbang (단방, single-ingredient remedies using common household ingredients like ginger, mugwort, or radish), Heo Jun turned a royal text into a village public-health manual. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Gayang-dong in Seoul and Sancheong, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Dongui Bogam (동의보감 1613 25-volume medical encyclopedia) to Hyangyak & Hangul herb names (향약과 한글 약재명 native Korean botanical medicine) across the centuries.

Keep three medical terms in mind: first, Dongui Bogam (동의보감, 'Precious Mirror of Eastern Medicine'), Heo Jun's 25-volume 1613 encyclopedia; second, Bul-chi-i-byeong Chi-mi-byeong (불치이병 치미병—'The wise physician treats illness before it arises rather than after it has broken out'), the preventive Yangsaeng philosophy opening the book; and third, Hyangyak (향약), native Korean medicinal herbs grown in local soil.
