Soldier’s Home

Ernest Hemingway (1925) • 14 min read

“Krebs went to the war from a Methodist college in Kansas.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Soldier's Home, by Ernest Hemingway, first appeared in 1925 and was gathered into his first major book of stories, In Our Time. It is about a young Marine named Harold Krebs, who comes home to a small town in Oklahoma long after the parades are over, and finds that nobody wants to hear the truth about what he saw. Hemingway himself came home from the First World War wounded and restless, and moved back in with his parents in Illinois, so the story carries some of his own friction. The writing is famously plain. Short sentences, repeated words, and almost no explanation of feelings let you sense the numbness that Krebs carries. This was a new way of writing about war, and it influenced many later authors. The story asks a quiet question that still matters: what happens to a person who has been through something enormous, when everyone around him wants life to go back to normal? Listen for the lies Krebs tells, and for the scene with his mother at the breakfast table.

Krebs went to the war from a Methodist college in Kansas. There is a picture which shows him among his fraternity brothers, all of them wearing exactly the same height and style collar. He enlisted in the Marines in 1917 and did not return to the United States until the second division returned from the Rhine in the summer of 1919.

There is a picture which shows him on the Rhine with two German girls and another corporal. Krebs and the corporal look too big for their uniforms. The German girls are not beautiful. The Rhine does not show in the picture.

By the time Krebs returned to his home town in Oklahoma the greeting of heroes was over. He came back much too late. The men from the town who had been drafted had all been welcomed elaborately on their return. There had been a great deal of hysteria. Now the reaction had set in. People seemed to think it was rather ridiculous for Krebs to be getting back so late, years after the war was over.

At first Krebs, who had been at Belleau Wood, Soissons, the Champagne, St. Mihiel and in the Argonne did not want to talk about the war at all. Later he felt the need to talk but no one wanted to hear about it. His town had heard too many atrocity stories to be thrilled by actualities. Krebs found that to be listened to at all he had to lie, and after he had done this twice he, too, had a reaction against the war and against talking about it. A distaste for everything that had happened to him in the war set in because of the lies he had told. All of the times that had been able to make him feel cool and clear inside himself when he thought of them; the times so long back when he had done the one thing, the only thing for a man to do, easily and naturally, when he might have done something else, now lost their cool, valuable quality and then were lost themselves.

His lies were quite unimportant lies and consisted in attributing to himself things other men had seen, done or heard of, and stating as facts certain apocryphal incidents familiar to all soldiers. Even his lies were not sensational at the pool room. His acquaintances, who had heard detailed accounts of German women found chained to machine guns in the Argonne forest and who could not comprehend, or were barred by their patriotism from interest in, any German machine gunners who were not chained, were not thrilled by his stories.

Krebs acquired the nausea in regard to experience that is the result of untruth or exaggeration, and when he occasionally met another man who had really been a soldier and they talked a few minutes in the dressing room at a dance he fell into the easy pose of the old soldier among other soldiers: that he had been badly, sickeningly frightened all the time. In this way he lost everything.

During this time, it was late summer, he was sleeping late in bed, getting up to walk down town to the library to get a book, eating lunch at home, reading on the front porch until he became bored and then walking down through the town to spend the hottest hours of the day in the cool dark of the pool room. He loved to play pool.

In the evening he practiced on his clarinet, strolled down town, read and went to bed. He was still a hero to his two young sisters. His mother would have given him breakfast in bed if he had wanted it. She often came in when he was in bed and asked him to tell her about the war, but her attention always wandered. His father was noncommittal.

Before Krebs went away to the war he had never been allowed to drive the family motor car. His father was in the real estate business and always wanted the car to be at his command when he required it to take clients out into the country to show them a piece of farm property. The car always stood outside the First National Bank building where his father had an office on the second floor. Now, after the war, it was still the same car.

Nothing was changed in the town except that the young girls had grown up. But they lived in such a complicated world of already defined alliances and shifting feuds that Krebs did not feel the energy or the courage to break into it. He liked to look at them, though. There were so many good-looking young girls. Most of them had their hair cut short. When he went away only little girls wore their hair like that or girls that were fast. They all wore sweaters and shirt waists with round Dutch collars. It was a pattern. He liked to look at them from the front porch as they walked on the other side of the street. He liked to watch them walking under the shade of the trees. He liked the round Dutch collars above their sweaters. He liked their silk stockings and flat shoes. He liked their bobbed hair and the way they walked.

When he was in town their appeal to him was not very strong. He did not like them when he saw them in the Greek’s ice cream parlor. He did not want them themselves really. They were too complicated. There was something else. Vaguely he wanted a girl but he did not want to have to work to get her. He would have liked to have a girl but he did not want to have to spend a long time getting her. He did not want to get into the intrigue and the politics. He did not want to have to do any courting. He did not want to tell any more lies. It wasn’t worth it.

He did not want any consequences. He did not want any consequences ever again. He wanted to live along without consequences. Besides he did not really need a girl. The army had taught him that. It was all right to pose as though you had to have a girl. Nearly everybody did that. But it wasn’t true. You did not need a girl. That was the funny thing. First a fellow boasted how girls mean nothing to him, that he never thought of them, that they could not touch him. Then a fellow boasted that he could not get along without girls, that he had to have them all the time, that he could not go to sleep without them.

That was all a lie. It was all a lie both ways. You did not need a girl unless you thought about them. He learned that in the army. Then sooner or later you always got one. When you were really ripe for a girl you always got one. You did not have to think about it. Sooner or later it would come. He had learned that in the army.

Now he would have liked a girl if she had come to him and not wanted to talk. But here at home it was all too complicated. He knew he could never get through it all again. It was not worth the trouble. That was the thing about French girls and German girls. There was not all this talking. You couldn’t talk much and you did not need to talk. It was simple and you were friends. He thought about France and then he began to think about Germany. On the whole he had liked Germany better. He did not want to leave Germany. He did not want to come home. Still, he had come home. He sat on the front porch.

He liked the girls that were walking along the other side of the street. He liked the look of them much better than the French girls or the German girls. But the world they were in was not the world he was in. He would like to have one of them. But it was not worth it. They were such a nice pattern. He liked the pattern. It was exciting. But he would not go through all the talking. He did not want one badly enough. He liked to look at them all, though. It was not worth it. Not now when things were getting good again.

He sat there on the porch reading a book on the war. It was a history and he was reading about all the engagements he had been in. It was the most interesting reading he had ever done. He wished there were more maps. He looked forward with a good feeling to reading all the really good histories when they would come out with good detail maps. Now he was really learning about the war. He had been a good soldier. That made a difference.

One morning after he had been home about a month his mother came into his bedroom and sat on the bed. She smoothed her apron.

“I had a talk with your father last night, Harold,” she said, “and he is willing for you to take the car out in the evenings.”

“Yeah?” said Krebs, who was not fully awake. “Take the car out? Yeah?”

“Yes. Your father has felt for some time that you should be able to take the car out in the evenings whenever you wished but we only talked it over last night.”

“I’ll bet you made him,” Krebs said.

“No. It was your father’s suggestion that we talk the matter over.”

“Yeah. I’ll bet you made him,” Krebs sat up in bed.

“Will you come down to breakfast, Harold?” his mother said.

“As soon as I get my clothes on,” Krebs said.

His mother went out of the room and he could hear her frying something downstairs while he washed, shaved and dressed to go down into the dining-room for breakfast. While he was eating breakfast his sister brought in the mail.

“Well, Hare,” she said. “You old sleepyhead. What do you ever get up for?”

Krebs looked at her. He liked her. She was his best sister.

“Have you got the paper?” he asked.

She handed him the Kansas City Star and he shucked off its brown wrapper and opened it to the sporting page. He folded the Star open and propped it against the water pitcher with his cereal dish to steady it, so he could read while he ate.

“Harold,” his mother stood in the kitchen doorway, “Harold, please don’t muss up the paper. Your father can’t read his Star if it’s been mussed.”

“I won’t muss it,” Krebs said.

His sister sat down at the table and watched him while he read.

“We’re playing indoor over at school this afternoon,” she said. “I’m going to pitch.”

“Good,” said Krebs. “How’s the old wing?”

“I can pitch better than lots of the boys. I tell them all you taught me. The other girls aren’t much good.”

“Yeah?” said Krebs.

“I tell them all you’re my beau. Aren’t you my beau, Hare?”

“You bet.”

“Couldn’t your brother really be your beau just because he’s your brother?”

“I don’t know.”

“Sure you know. Couldn’t you be my beau, Hare, if I was old enough and if you wanted to?”

“Sure. You’re my girl now.”

“Am I really your girl?”

“Sure.”

“Do you love me?”

“Uh, huh.”

“Will you love me always?”

“Sure.”

“Will you come over and watch me play indoor?”

“Maybe.”

“Aw, Hare, you don’t love me. If you loved me, you’d want to come over and watch me play indoor.”

Krebs’s mother came into the dining-room from the kitchen. She carried a plate with two fried eggs and some crisp bacon on it and a plate of buckwheat cakes.

“You run along, Helen,” she said. “I want to talk to Harold.”

She put the eggs and bacon down in front of him and brought in a jug of maple syrup for the buckwheat cakes. Then she sat down across the table from Krebs.

“I wish you’d put down the paper a minute, Harold,” she said.

Krebs took down the paper and folded it.

“Have you decided what you are going to do yet, Harold?” his mother said, taking off her glasses.

“No,” said Krebs.

“Don’t you think it’s about time?” His mother did not say this in a mean way. She seemed worried.

“I hadn’t thought about it,” Krebs said.

“God has some work for everyone to do,” his mother said. “There can be no idle hands in His Kingdom.”

“I’m not in His Kingdom,” Krebs said.

“We are all of us in His Kingdom.”

Krebs felt embarrassed and resentful as always.

“I’ve worried about you so much, Harold,” his mother went on. “I know the temptations you must have been exposed to. I know how weak men are. I know what your own dear grandfather, my own father, told us about the Civil War and I have prayed for you. I pray for you all day long, Harold.”

Krebs looked at the bacon fat hardening on his plate.

“Your father is worried, too,” his mother went on. “He thinks you have lost your ambition, that you haven’t got a definite aim in life. Charley Simmons, who is just your age, has a good job and is going to be married. The boys are all settling down; they’re all determined to get somewhere; you can see that boys like Charley Simmons are on their way to being really a credit to the community.”

Krebs said nothing.

“Don’t look that way, Harold,” his mother said. “You know we love you and I want to tell you for your own good how matters stand. Your father does not want to hamper your freedom. He thinks you should be allowed to drive the car. If you want to take some of the nice girls out riding with you, we are only too pleased. We want you to enjoy yourself. But you are going to have to settle down to work, Harold. Your father doesn’t care what you start in at. All work is honorable as he says. But you’ve got to make a start at something. He asked me to speak to you this morning and then you can stop in and see him at his office.”

“Is that all?” Krebs said.

“Yes. Don’t you love your mother, dear boy?”

“No,” Krebs said.

His mother looked at him across the table. Her eyes were shiny. She started crying.

“I don’t love anybody,” Krebs said.

It wasn’t any good. He couldn’t tell her, he couldn’t make her see it. It was silly to have said it. He had only hurt her. He went over and took hold of her arm. She was crying with her head in her hands.

“I didn’t mean it,” he said. “I was just angry at something. I didn’t mean I didn’t love you.”

His mother went on crying. Krebs put his arm on her shoulder.

“Can’t you believe me, mother?”

His mother shook her head.

“Please, please, mother. Please believe me.”

“All right,” his mother said chokily. She looked up at him. “I believe you, Harold.”

Krebs kissed her hair. She put her face up to him.

“I’m your mother,” she said. “I held you next to my heart when you were a tiny baby.”

Krebs felt sick and vaguely nauseated.

“I know, Mummy,” he said. “I’ll try and be a good boy for you.”

“Would you kneel and pray with me, Harold?” his mother asked.

They knelt down beside the dining-room table and Krebs’s mother prayed.

“Now, you pray, Harold,” she said.

“I can’t,” Krebs said.

“Try, Harold.”

“I can’t.”

“Do you want me to pray for you?”

“Yes.”

So his mother prayed for him and then they stood up and Krebs kissed his mother and went out of the house. He had tried so to keep his life from being complicated. Still, none of it had touched him. He had felt sorry for his mother and she had made him lie. He would go to Kansas City and get a job and she would feel all right about it. There would be one more scene maybe before he got away. He would not go down to his father’s office. He would miss that one. He wanted his life to go smoothly. It had just gotten going that way. Well, that was all over now, anyway. He would go over to the schoolyard and watch Helen play indoor baseball.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Soldier's Home is about a man who has come home and cannot come all the way back. Harold Krebs went to war from a Methodist college in Kansas, fought in some of the worst battles of the American war in France, and returned in the summer of 1919, long after the town's welcome parties for the draftees had ended. The story is very careful about timing. Krebs "came back much too late." The hero's welcome has already been used up, and the town has moved on to feeling a little bored by the war.

The first problem is that nobody wants to listen. At first Krebs does not want to talk about the war. Later he feels the need to, but the town has heard so many atrocity stories that real experience seems dull. To be listened to at all, he has to lie. His lies are small. He borrows things other men saw and adopts stories that every soldier knows. But the lying poisons the memories. The moments in the war that had made him feel "cool and clear" inside, the times when he did the one thing a man should do, lose their value once he cheapens them by talking. This is the core of the story. Krebs is not damaged by violence alone. He is damaged by having no honest way to carry what happened.

Notice the opening paragraph, which describes two photographs. In one, he sits with his fraternity brothers, all in identical collars. In another, he is on the Rhine with two German girls, and the narrator notes that the Rhine does not show in the picture. That is the story in miniature: a surface that records almost nothing of the real experience.

The second problem is that Krebs wants a life without effort or consequences. He watches the young girls of the town from a distance and likes the look of them, but he does not want the "intrigue and the politics" of courting, and he does not want to tell any more lies. He says he wants "to live along without consequences." This is both honest and sad. It shows how thoroughly the war has drained his energy, and how he is hiding from anything that might require him to feel.

Then comes the breakfast scene, which is the emotional center. His mother, who loves him and wants to help, tells him that God has work for everyone and that there can be no idle hands in His Kingdom. She asks, "Don't you love your mother, dear boy?" He answers "No," and then "I don't love anybody." Then he apologizes, kisses her hair, and kneels to pray, but cannot. He lets his mother pray for him.

At the end, Krebs decides he will go to Kansas City and find a job. This is not triumph. It is escape. He does not want a scene. He wants his life to go smoothly. He will go and watch his sister Helen play indoor baseball, which is the one simple, uncomplicated pleasure in his world. The story ends on that small gesture, and the reader feels the loss behind it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Soldier's Home is one of the cleanest pieces of short fiction in the language, and its biggest strength is control. Nearly every sentence does two jobs. It tells you what is happening in the plainest possible words, and it also reveals a state of mind. Take the line about the photograph with the Rhine missing. It is flat, almost comic, and yet it tells us exactly how little the pictures capture. The same is true of the long sequence in which Krebs eats breakfast, reads the sports page, and walks to the library. The repetition of "he liked" and "he did not want" creates a rhythm of withdrawal. We feel his wish to be left alone without ever being told to.

The structure is also well judged. The first half describes Krebs's days and thoughts, building a mood of drift. The second half collapses that drift into a single scene, so that the abstract problem becomes a concrete conflict at a breakfast table. This is smart, and it prepares us to understand why he says what he says. The mother is drawn with real compassion. She is not a villain. She speaks in clichés about God and work, but she is frightened and loving, and the story lets us see that. That fairness keeps it from being a simple sermon against religion or small-town life.

There are weaknesses. The story's reliance on repetition can occasionally feel mechanical, particularly in the middle paragraphs, where Hemingway's "he liked" and "he did not want" sentences stack up almost like a formula. Some readers find the long middle section static, a series of statements rather than scenes. The mother's dialogue also tilts toward caricature in places, especially in the prayer, and the references to her father and the Civil War are slightly clumsy. The title is a subtle irony, since the soldier has no home, but it also announces the theme a bit loudly.

There is another problem of perspective. Krebs's numbness is presented as almost entirely a matter of the war and the town's indifference, but the story is slightly vague about what exactly happened to him. The narrator refuses to explain, which is part of the design. Yet some readers feel the vagueness protects Hemingway from the harder task of showing us a damaged mind from the inside. It is a story about trauma that never names it, and that restraint can be read as either wise or evasive.

Even so, the ending is hard to fault. The last sentences are almost cruelly simple, and they close the story without resolving it. Krebs will leave. He will watch Helen play. He will keep things simple. The reader knows he is still stuck. That is a tough, truthful ending, and it is why the story has lasted.

III. The Impact It Had

When Soldier's Home appeared in 1925, it was part of a small, bold group of stories that announced a new voice in American writing. It was first printed in a collection of contemporary writers edited by Robert McAlmon, and then collected in In Our Time, Hemingway's first major book of stories, published in the United States by Boni and Liveright. At the time he was a young American in Paris, sharpening his style among a circle of experimental writers. The book established the approach that made him famous, and Soldier's Home showed it at its most disciplined.

The story's biggest impact was on how American writers dealt with war and its aftermath. Most earlier fiction about soldiers focused on battle, sacrifice, or patriotism. Hemingway focused instead on the quiet days after, when a man cannot explain himself. This shift helped create a whole tradition of postwar writing. It echoes in novels about veterans after the Second World War, and in stories about Vietnam and the later conflicts. Writers like Tim O'Brien and many others who write about the difficulty of telling the truth about war are working in a path Hemingway helped clear. The story's central insight, that telling war stories can corrupt the memory of them, appears again and again in veterans' writing.

It also played a major role in popularizing what became known as the iceberg theory. Hemingway later explained that a writer could omit things he knew, and the story would still feel their weight. In Soldier's Home, the omitted material is enormous, and the story depends on the reader's sense of it. Many later short story writers took the lesson, including those who wrote domestic and working-class stories in a stripped-down, minimalist style.

The story has also lived in classrooms. It is anthologized widely, taught in high schools and colleges, and often used to introduce close reading, because its techniques are easy to see. Students are asked to look at the repeated words, the absence of explanation, and the single decisive scene.

For Hemingway himself, the story belongs to a theme he returned to for years: the man who comes back from war and cannot fit into ordinary life. A related story in the same book, Big Two-Hearted River, shows another veteran seeking peace in nature. The two pieces together are often read as a pair, one showing the failure of home and the other the attempt at self-repair.

Finally, the story has fed a broader cultural conversation about how societies treat returning soldiers. The first line about the parades being over has become a kind of shorthand for the way a country can honor its soldiers in public and then ignore them in private. That idea remains painfully familiar.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved Soldier's Home most of all for its honesty. It declines to make Krebs a hero or a victim. He is just a man who is tired, confused, and unable to explain it, and many readers recognize that feeling from their own lives, even if they have never been to war. The story's emotional truth is not confined to soldiers. Anyone who has returned from a transforming experience, whether a long illness, a hard time abroad, or a loss, and found that others cannot or will not listen, knows something of Krebs's predicament.

Many also love the story's treatment of lying. It is a startlingly accurate observation that talking about a precious experience in a way that pleases others can ruin it. The idea that Krebs's lies make the best memories lose their value is subtle, and readers often point to it as one of Hemingway's deepest insights. It is the sort of idea that you only realize is true after you read it.

The prose style has won countless admirers. Writers and readers who find the long sentences of other novelists exhausting have often found relief in Hemingway's directness. Admirers have praised how the plain sentences can carry feeling without decoration. The line about the German girls in the photograph, who were not beautiful, and the Rhine that does not show, is often quoted as a perfect example of understated irony.

The breakfast scene is another high point. Readers have long been moved by the mother's loving, suffocating pressure and by Krebs's unexpected cruelty and quick remorse. It feels like a real argument, in which both people are hurting and neither is entirely wrong. The moment when he cannot pray and asks his mother to pray for him is regularly singled out as unforgettable. It conveys so much about faith, family, and distance in a few words.

The ending also earns affection for its quiet tenderness. Krebs's sister Helen, who adores him, provides a ray of simple warmth, and the fact that he chooses to watch her play baseball suggests that he has not given up on human connection altogether. Readers like this because it is hopeful without being sentimental.

Taken together, these qualities explain why the story is so often named as one of the best American short stories of the twentieth century. It is short, accessible, and deeply felt, and it speaks to anyone who has ever felt like a stranger in the place they once called home.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a story as admired as Soldier's Home has attracted criticism. The most common objection concerns Hemingway's style. Critics from the 1920s onward have said that his plain, repetitive prose can be monotonous, and that what looks like depth is sometimes just blankness. Parodies of his manner often borrow the cadence of this very story, with its steady drumbeat of short statements. For some readers, the repeated phrases such as "he did not want" cross the line from effective rhythm into mannerism.

Another complaint is about the characters. Krebs's mother, in particular, can seem a type, the pious and smothering small-town woman, rather than a fully imagined person. Some readers feel Hemingway was settling scores with his own mother, and that the portrait is unfair. This is a reasonable reading, since Hemingway did have a difficult relationship with his mother after he returned from the war, and he later spoke harshly of her. The story's sympathy for her is real but limited.

Some critics have also questioned the moral outlook. Krebs's wish to live without consequences can look less like a wound and more like an immature refusal of responsibility. A reader who is less sympathetic may see a young man who is sulking at home, being supported by his parents, and refusing even to look for work. The story does not clearly resolve whether we should pity him or push him. Some find that ambiguity rich. Others find that it leaves the story a little too comfortable with his passivity.

There are also questions about how the war is handled. The story names battles, but we never see them. Some critics have said that this lets Hemingway borrow the authority of war experience without having to dramatize it. Others, particularly veterans, have argued the opposite, that the story gets the aftermath right. Both views are part of the continuing conversation.

The treatment of women is another point of criticism. The girls in the story are seen purely from the outside, as objects of Krebs's vague desire and his disinterest. Women have little interior life in the story apart from the mother, and even she is seen through Krebs's irritation. This pattern in Hemingway's work has drawn comment from feminist critics for decades.

Finally, a few readers argue that the story is overrated simply because it is a classroom favorite, and that its tidy symbolism and carefully planned omissions are easier to teach than to love. That seems unfair to me, but it is a view held by some.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Soldier's Home sits at the center of modernist short fiction, the movement that, in the early twentieth century, rejected the long, explanatory, moralizing style of the nineteenth century. Modernists such as Hemingway, Ezra Pound, and Gertrude Stein believed that language should be stripped of ornament, and that the reader should be trusted to supply meaning. Hemingway learned this in Paris, and Soldier's Home is one of its purest products.

It also belongs to the large body of writing produced by the First World War. Along with Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front, Wilfred Owen's poems, and Hemingway's own A Farewell to Arms, it helped define the idea of the Lost Generation, the young people who survived the war but found themselves unable to believe in the old promises. What sets Soldier's Home apart is that it is entirely set after the war, in a place that seems untouched by it. The distance between the quiet town and the unspeakable memory is the whole drama.

Looking backward, the story descends from the realism of writers such as Anton Chekhov and Stephen Crane. Chekhov's stories also hinge on small moments and unspoken pain. Sherwood Anderson, whose Winesburg, Ohio explores lonely small-town lives, was an early supporter of Hemingway and a clear influence on this kind of tale. The theme of the returning soldier goes back much further, to the Odyssey, in which Odysseus comes home to a place that has changed and a family that does not quite know him. Krebs is a modern, unheroic version of that figure.

Looking forward, the story's descendants are everywhere. After the Second World War, the film The Best Years of Our Lives and many novels dealt with returning veterans. Later writers about Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, including Tim O'Brien and many younger authors, have returned to the same problem of unshareable experience. Outside war, American short fiction from Raymond Carver to many contemporary writers continues to use Hemingway's lean, understated approach.

It matters today because the problem it describes has not changed. Societies still celebrate soldiers in ceremonies and then leave them to manage alone. People still struggle to explain extreme experiences to those who have not lived them. And families still try to help in ways that make things worse. The story is short enough to read in twenty minutes, and it offers no easy answers. What it gives is recognition. If you have ever felt that your inner life could not be shared, Krebs will feel familiar, and that is why it is worth reading.
