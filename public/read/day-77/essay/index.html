On the Enjoyment of Unpleasant Places

Robert Louis Stevenson (1874) • 16 min read

Things looked at patiently from one side after another generally end by showing a side that is beautiful.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Robert Louis Stevenson was only about twenty-three when this essay appeared in 1874, in a magazine called The Portfolio, long before Treasure Island made him famous. He was a sickly young Scotsman from a family of lighthouse engineers, training for a career he did not want, and writing was already his private ambition. This essay shows him teaching himself how to see. His idea is simple and a little cheeky: any place, however bleak, can give you something, if you stay long enough and look in the right spirit. He builds the case from his own memory of a windswept, treeless stretch of the northern Scottish coast, where he found his deepest feeling of peace in a sheltered hollow between two ruined castles. It is an early example of the warm, conversational style he later perfected, and it is a gentle reminder that happiness in a landscape is partly something you bring with you. Listen for how he moves from argument to memory to a small moment of real stillness.

It is a difficult matter to make the most of any given place, and we have much in our own power. Things looked at patiently from one side after another generally end by showing a side that is beautiful. A few months ago some words were said in the Portfolio as to an “austere regimen in scenery”; and such a discipline was then recommended as “healthful and strengthening to the taste.” That is the text, so to speak, of the present essay. This discipline in scenery, it must be understood, is something more than a mere walk before breakfast to whet the appetite. For when we are put down in some unsightly neighbourhood, and especially if we have come to be more or less dependent on what we see, we must set ourselves to hunt out beautiful things with all the ardour and patience of a botanist after a rye plant. Day by day we perfect ourselves in the art of seeing nature more favourably. We learn to live with her, as people learn to live with fretful or violent spouses: to dwell lovingly on what is good, and shut our eyes against all that is bleak or inharmonious. We learn, also, to come to each place in the right spirit. The traveller, as Brantôme quaintly tells us, “fait des discours en soi pour se soutenir en chemin”; and into these discourses he weaves something out of all that he sees and suffers by the way; they take their tone greatly from the varying character of the scene; a sharp ascent brings different thoughts from a level road; and the man’s fancies grow lighter as he comes out of the wood into a clearing. Nor does the scenery any more affect the thoughts than the thoughts affect the scenery. We see places through our humours as through differently coloured glasses. We are ourselves a term in the equation, a note of the chord, and make discord or harmony almost at will. There is no fear for the result, if we can but surrender ourselves sufficiently to the country that surrounds and follows us, so that we are ever thinking suitable thoughts or telling ourselves some suitable sort of story as we go. We become thus, in some sense, a centre of beauty; we are provocative of beauty, much as a gentle and sincere character is provocative of sincerity and gentleness in others. And even where there is no harmony to be elicited by the quickest and most obedient of spirits, we may still embellish a place with some attraction of romance. We may learn to go far afield for associations, and handle them lightly when we have found them. Sometimes an old print comes to our aid; I have seen many a spot lit up at once with picturesque imaginations, by a reminiscence of Callot, or Sadeler, or Paul Brill. Dick Turpin has been my lay figure for many an English lane. And I suppose the Trossachs would hardly be the Trossachs for most tourists if a man of admirable romantic instinct had not peopled it for them with harmonious figures, and brought them thither with minds rightly prepared for the impression. There is half the battle in this preparation. For instance: I have rarely been able to visit, in the proper spirit, the wild and inhospitable places of our own Highlands. I am happier where it is tame and fertile, and not readily pleased without trees. I understand that there are some phases of mental trouble that harmonise well with such surroundings, and that some persons, by the dispensing power of the imagination, can go back several centuries in spirit, and put themselves into sympathy with the hunted, houseless, unsociable way of life that was in its place upon these savage hills. Now, when I am sad, I like nature to charm me out of my sadness, like David before Saul; and the thought of these past ages strikes nothing in me but an unpleasant pity; so that I can never hit on the right humour for this sort of landscape, and lose much pleasure in consequence. Still, even here, if I were only let alone, and time enough were given, I should have all manner of pleasures, and take many clear and beautiful images away with me when I left. When we cannot think ourselves into sympathy with the great features of a country, we learn to ignore them, and put our head among the grass for flowers, or pore, for long times together, over the changeful current of a stream. We come down to the sermon in stones, when we are shut out from any poem in the spread landscape. We begin to peep and botanise, we take an interest in birds and insects, we find many things beautiful in miniature. The reader will recollect the little summer scene in Wuthering Heights⁠—the one warm scene, perhaps, in all that powerful, miserable novel⁠—and the great feature that is made therein by grasses and flowers and a little sunshine: this is in the spirit of which I now speak. And, lastly, we can go indoors; interiors are sometimes as beautiful, often more picturesque, than the shows of the open air, and they have that quality of shelter of which I shall presently have more to say.

With all this in mind, I have often been tempted to put forth the paradox that any place is good enough to live a life in, while it is only in a few, and those highly favoured, that we can pass a few hours agreeably. For, if we only stay long enough we become at home in the neighbourhood. Reminiscences spring up, like flowers, about uninteresting corners. We forget to some degree the superior loveliness of other places, and fall into a tolerant and sympathetic spirit which is its own reward and justification. Looking back the other day on some recollections of my own, I was astonished to find how much I owed to such a residence; six weeks in one unpleasant countryside had done more, it seemed, to quicken and educate my sensibilities than many years in places that jumped more nearly with my inclination.

The country to which I refer was a level and treeless plateau, over which the winds cut like a whip. For miles and miles it was the same. A river, indeed, fell into the sea near the town where I resided; but the valley of the river was shallow and bald, for as far up as ever I had the heart to follow it. There were roads, certainly, but roads that had no beauty or interest; for, as there was no timber, and but little irregularity of surface, you saw your whole walk exposed to you from the beginning: there was nothing left to fancy, nothing to expect, nothing to see by the wayside, save here and there an unhomely-looking homestead, and here and there a solitary, spectacled stone-breaker; and you were only accompanied, as you went doggedly forward, by the gaunt telegraph-posts and the hum of the resonant wires in the keen sea-wind. To one who had learned to know their song in warm pleasant places by the Mediterranean, it seemed to taunt the country, and make it still bleaker by suggested contrast. Even the waste places by the side of the road were not, as Hawthorne liked to put it, “taken back to Nature” by any decent covering of vegetation. Wherever the land had the chance, it seemed to lie fallow. There is a certain tawny nudity of the South, bare sunburnt plains, coloured like a lion, and hills clothed only in the blue transparent air; but this was of another description⁠—this was the nakedness of the North; the earth seemed to know that it was naked, and was ashamed and cold.

It seemed to be always blowing on that coast. Indeed, this had passed into the speech of the inhabitants, and they saluted each other when they met with “Breezy, breezy,” instead of the customary “Fine day” of farther south. These continual winds were not like the harvest breeze, that just keeps an equable pressure against your face as you walk, and serves to set all the trees talking over your head, or bring round you the smell of the wet surface of the country after a shower. They were of the bitter, hard, persistent sort, that interferes with sight and respiration, and makes the eyes sore. Even such winds as these have their own merit in proper time and place. It is pleasant to see them brandish great masses of shadow. And what a power they have over the colour of the world! How they ruffle the solid woodlands in their passage, and make them shudder and whiten like a single willow! There is nothing more vertiginous than a wind like this among the woods, with all its sights and noises; and the effect gets between some painters and their sober eyesight, so that, even when the rest of their picture is calm, the foliage is coloured like foliage in a gale. There was nothing, however, of this sort to be noticed in a country where there were no trees and hardly any shadows, save the passive shadows of clouds or those of rigid houses and walls. But the wind was nevertheless an occasion of pleasure; for nowhere could you taste more fully the pleasure of a sudden lull, or a place of opportune shelter. The reader knows what I mean; he must remember how, when he has sat himself down behind a dyke on a hillside, he delighted to hear the wind hiss vainly through the crannies at his back; how his body tingled all over with warmth, and it began to dawn upon him, with a sort of slow surprise, that the country was beautiful, the heather purple, and the faraway hills all marbled with sun and shadow. Wordsworth, in a beautiful passage of the “Prelude,” has used this as a figure for the feeling struck in us by the quiet by-streets of London after the uproar of the great thoroughfares; and the comparison may be turned the other way with as good effect:⁠—

“Meanwhile the roar continues, till at length, Escaped as from an enemy, we turn Abruptly into some sequester’d nook, Still as a shelter’d place when winds blow loud!”

I remember meeting a man once, in a train, who told me of what must have been quite the most perfect instance of this pleasure of escape. He had gone up, one sunny, windy morning, to the top of a great cathedral somewhere abroad; I think it was Cologne Cathedral, the great unfinished marvel by the Rhine; and after a long while in dark stairways, he issued at last into the sunshine, on a platform high above the town. At that elevation it was quite still and warm; the gale was only in the lower strata of the air, and he had forgotten it in the quiet interior of the church and during his long ascent; and so you may judge of his surprise when, resting his arms on the sunlit balustrade and looking over into the Place far below him, he saw the good people holding on their hats and leaning hard against the wind as they walked. There is something, to my fancy, quite perfect in this little experience of my fellow-traveller’s. The ways of men seem always very trivial to us when we find ourselves alone on a church-top, with the blue sky and a few tall pinnacles, and see far below us the steep roofs and foreshortened buttresses, and the silent activity of the city streets; but how much more must they not have seemed so to him as he stood, not only above other men’s business, but above other men’s climate, in a golden zone like Apollo’s!

This was the sort of pleasure I found in the country of which I write. The pleasure was to be out of the wind, and to keep it in memory all the time, and hug oneself upon the shelter. And it was only by the sea that any such sheltered places were to be found. Between the black worm-eaten headlands there are little bights and havens, well screened from the wind and the commotion of the external sea, where the sand and weeds look up into the gazer’s face from a depth of tranquil water, and the seabirds, screaming and flickering from the ruined crags, alone disturb the silence and the sunshine. One such place has impressed itself on my memory beyond all others. On a rock by the water’s edge, old fighting men of the Norse breed had planted a double castle; the two stood wall to wall like semidetached villas; and yet feud had run so high between their owners, that one, from out of a window, shot the other as he stood in his own doorway. There is something in the juxtaposition of these two enemies full of tragic irony. It is grim to think of bearded men and bitter women taking hateful counsel together about the two hall-fires at night, when the sea boomed against the foundations and the wild winter wind was loose over the battlements. And in the study we may reconstruct for ourselves some pale figure of what life then was. Not so when we are there; when we are there such thoughts come to us only to intensify a contrary impression, and association is turned against itself. I remember walking thither three afternoons in succession, my eyes weary with being set against the wind, and how, dropping suddenly over the edge of the down, I found myself in a new world of warmth and shelter. The wind, from which I had escaped, “as from an enemy,” was seemingly quite local. It carried no clouds with it, and came from such a quarter that it did not trouble the sea within view. The two castles, black and ruinous as the rocks about them, were still distinguishable from these by something more insecure and fantastic in the outline, something that the last storm had left imminent and the next would demolish entirely. It would be difficult to render in words the sense of peace that took possession of me on these three afternoons. It was helped out, as I have said, by the contrast. The shore was battered and bemauled by previous tempests; I had the memory at heart of the insane strife of the pygmies who had erected these two castles and lived in them in mutual distrust and enmity, and knew I had only to put my head out of this little cup of shelter to find the hard wind blowing in my eyes; and yet there were the two great tracts of motionless blue air and peaceful sea looking on, unconcerned and apart, at the turmoil of the present moment and the memorials of the precarious past. There is ever something transitory and fretful in the impression of a high wind under a cloudless sky; it seems to have no root in the constitution of things; it must speedily begin to faint and wither away like a cut flower. And on those days the thought of the wind and the thought of human life came very near together in my mind. Our noisy years did indeed seem moments in the being of the eternal silence; and the wind, in the face of that great field of stationary blue, was as the wind of a butterfly’s wing. The placidity of the sea was a thing likewise to be remembered. Shelley speaks of the sea as “hungering for calm,” and in this place one learned to understand the phrase. Looking down into these green waters from the broken edge of the rock, or swimming leisurely in the sunshine, it seemed to me that they were enjoying their own tranquillity; and when now and again it was disturbed by a wind ripple on the surface, or the quick black passage of a fish far below, they settled back again (one could fancy) with relief.

On shore too, in the little nook of shelter, everything was so subdued and still that the least particular struck in me a pleasurable surprise. The desultory crackling of the whin-pods in the afternoon sun usurped the ear. The hot, sweet breath of the bank, that had been saturated all day long with sunshine, and now exhaled it into my face, was like the breath of a fellow-creature. I remember that I was haunted by two lines of French verse; in some dumb way they seemed to fit my surroundings and give expression to the contentment that was in me, and I kept repeating to myself⁠—

“Mon cœur est un luth suspendu, Sitôt qu’on le touche, il résonne.”

I can give no reason why these lines came to me at this time; and for that very cause I repeat them here. For all I know, they may serve to complete the impression in the mind of the reader, as they were certainly a part of it for me.

And this happened to me in the place of all others where I liked least to stay. When I think of it I grow ashamed of my own ingratitude. “Out of the strong came forth sweetness.” There, in the bleak and gusty North, I received, perhaps, my strongest impression of peace. I saw the sea to be great and calm; and the earth, in that little corner, was all alive and friendly to me. So, wherever a man is, he will find something to please and pacify him: in the town he will meet pleasant faces of men and women, and see beautiful flowers at a window, or hear a cage-bird singing at the corner of the gloomiest street; and for the country, there is no country without some amenity⁠—let him only look for it in the right spirit, and he will surely find.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The essay begins with a thesis, and the thesis is practical. It is hard to make the most of any given place, Stevenson says, but we have much in our own power, and things looked at patiently from one side after another usually end up showing a beautiful side. He calls this an austere regimen in scenery, a discipline for the eyes. That is the text, as he puts it, of the whole piece. The point is not to take a brisk walk before breakfast. If we are stuck somewhere ugly, we must hunt out beauty with the patience of a botanist, and learn to see nature more favourably, the way people learn to live with a difficult spouse: dwell on what is good, and close your eyes to the rest.

Then he adds something more interesting. We are not just looking at scenery; we are part of it. We see places through our moods as through coloured glass. We are, he says, a term in the equation, a note in the chord. If we surrender to the country around us and tell ourselves suitable stories as we go, we can become a centre of beauty, as a gentle person draws gentleness out of others. And when the landscape will not cooperate, we have fallbacks: borrow romance from old prints, as he does with Dick Turpin on an English lane, or shrink our attention down to flowers in the grass, birds and insects, or step indoors.

From there he reaches his paradox: any place is good enough to live a life in, though only a few are good enough to pass a few hours agreeably. Stay long enough and memories grow up around the dullest corners like flowers. He then tells the story that proves it. Six weeks in one unpleasant countryside, a level treeless plateau where the wind cut like a whip, taught him more than years in places he liked better. The locals greeted each other with Breezy, breezy, and the telegraph wires hummed bleakly overhead.

The heart of the essay is a sheltered cove he visited on three afternoons, where two ruined castles stood wall to wall like semidetached villas, and one owner had once shot the other in his own doorway. The place offered something precisely because of the wind: the joy of escaping it. He compares this to Wordsworth's quiet side street after the roar of the main road and to a traveller on a cathedral roof who found the air still while people far below leaned into a gale.

The layer a first reader might miss is the final turn. The peace Stevenson felt came from contrast, from knowing the hard wind was just over the edge. The wind became almost a gift. He closes ashamed of his ingratitude for a place he liked least, and with the quiet moral that wherever a man is, he will find something to please him, if he looks in the right spirit.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

This is a young man's essay, and it has both the charm and the flaws that implies. The strongest thing about it is the movement. It starts as an argument, slides into reminiscence, and by the end has become a small piece of lyric prose, so that the thesis is demonstrated rather than merely stated. The cove passage is the best writing in it. The rocks, the green water, the crackling whin-pods in the afternoon sun, and the warm breath of a bank that has soaked up sunshine all day and gives it back like the breath of a fellow creature, are sharply observed and truly felt. The comparison of the wind to the wind of a butterfly's wing, set against a great field of still blue, is a lovely way to turn a scene into a thought about human life.

There is also craft in the structure of the paradox. Most of us would say that pretty places make us happy. Stevenson shows that our happiness depends on contrast and on attention, which is a more subtle claim and a more useful one. The advice is memorable because it comes in concrete form: look at the grass, listen to the pods, hug yourself behind the dyke.

The weaknesses are equally visible. The opening is stiff and overloaded with borrowed learning. There are references to Brantôme in French, to Callot and Sadeler and Paul Brill, to Hawthorne, Wordsworth, Shelley, Emily Brontë and a couplet of French verse, and some of them feel like a young writer showing off his reading. The prose can slip into the ornamental, with pairs of adjectives and balanced clauses that look more like exercises in the style of the Victorian essayists he admired than the loose, personal voice he would soon develop. The Wuthering Heights aside is charming, but a reader without that novel in mind might feel left out.

There is a more serious limit as well. The philosophy is a comfortable one. To advise a reader to find pleasure in an unpleasant place assumes that the reader is a visitor with the freedom to leave, and with the leisure to walk to a cove three afternoons running. For the people who actually live and work in a bleak district, with no choice of residence, the advice would sound easy. Stevenson partly acknowledges this by calling it a paradox, but he does not wrestle with it.

Finally, the argument wobbles in the middle. He says any place is good enough to live in, yet the evidence is mostly about a shelter from the wind, which is a pleasure available only in some corners. Even so, these are the faults of a gifted apprentice. The best of the essay is already the real Stevenson, and the rest is a promising sketch.

III. The Impact It Had

It would be an overstatement to say that this essay changed the course of literature. It appeared in a specialist magazine, The Portfolio, which was devoted mainly to the visual arts, and it was among the first pieces Stevenson published at a time when he was still known only to family and friends. Its main importance is as an early marker. It shows that the writer who would later produce Travels with a Donkey in the Cevennes, Treasure Island and Kidnapped already had a distinctive habit of mind: a belief that attention and spirit can transform a place, and a fondness for the moral drawn lightly from an anecdote.

Within Stevenson's own career, the essay belongs to the group of early pieces on landscape and travel that taught him how to write about places. The training mattered. When he later wrote about the Scottish coast, the Cevennes, the American West and eventually the South Seas, he drew on the same method of attending closely to small details and letting mood colour the scene. His fiction also owes a debt, since the vivid sense of weather, tide and coastline that gives Kidnapped and Treasure Island their atmosphere was first practised here. And his family's work building lighthouses along the Scottish coast supplied the setting, since he first went to the northern harbours as a trainee engineer.

Beyond Stevenson, the essay is part of a large tradition of writing that treats the experience of place as something we make as well as find. Later nature writers and travel writers have made similar arguments, that attention is a form of love and that even drab landscapes reward patient looking. The idea has an echo in modern talk of mindfulness, though Stevenson's version is much more literary and less self-improving. Readers who enjoy the contemporary genre of the walking essay will recognise its ancestry.

The documented record here is limited. There is little evidence of a specific, dramatic public reaction to this particular essay when it first appeared, and it has never been a headline work. It has mostly survived as part of the collected essays and travel pieces, where it is read for its quality and for what it tells us about the young author.

In teaching, it is sometimes used as a gentle model of the personal essay: it has a clear thesis, a vivid example, and a graceful close. Students can see the structure plainly, and the cove passage is a fine specimen of descriptive writing. Its modest reputation makes it a discovery for many readers rather than a set text.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers seem to value most is the generosity of the idea. Many people have had the experience of being stuck in a place they disliked, a dull town, a rented room, a wet holiday, and Stevenson tells them that the experience may not be wasted. That is a consoling message, and he delivers it without preaching. The tone is of a friend who has tried the thing himself and is a little ashamed to report that it worked.

Readers also love the cove. The passage about the two ruined castles, the hot breath of the sunny bank and the green water enjoying its own tranquillity gives a clear and physical sense of a particular afternoon, and it does so in language that is musical without being showy. The detail of the man on the cathedral top, standing above other men's business and above their climate in a golden zone like Apollo's, is one of those images that stays with people. Anthologists have often been drawn to such passages, and they work especially well read aloud.

Another source of affection is the honesty of the narrator. He admits that he cannot find the right humour for wild Highland scenery, that sad thoughts of past ages bring him only an unpleasant pity, and that he likes nature to charm him out of sadness like David playing before Saul. He also admits that he does not know why two lines of French verse came to him in the cove, and repeats them because he cannot explain them. This candour about the limits of his own taste makes his advice feel earned. Readers tend to trust a guide who confesses his own preferences.

There is also real pleasure in the form. The essay lets a thought wander, and takes the reader along from argument to anecdote to memory, so that the effect is of conversation rather than lecture. Admirers of Stevenson's later essays, including the famous ones in Virginibus Puerisque, often find in this early piece the seeds of that conversational charm.

It is fair to say that general readers and later admirers of Stevenson have tended to treat the early landscape essays as small pleasures: slight, but graceful, and bearing the unmistakable mark of a writer in the making. For those who love walking, weather or quiet places, the essay often becomes a quiet favourite.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because this essay has never been a famous battleground, there is no dramatic body of controversy to report. What can be said is that readers and critics of Stevenson's early work have raised a few recurring reservations, and some of them apply fairly here.

The first is the charge of literary self-consciousness. Stevenson himself later described his youthful years as a time of playing the sedulous ape, imitating admired writers in order to learn their tricks. Critics have seen the same apprenticeship in these early essays: the elaborate sentences, the allusions to painters and poets, and the careful balance of phrases. Some readers find the opening paragraphs mannered and a little cold compared with the later, warmer Stevenson. The essay is a graceful performance, they say, but it sometimes feels like a performance.

The second is the matter of slightness. The argument that we can find beauty almost anywhere is attractive, but it can seem thin, even complacent. A reader who has lived in real hardship, in an ugly industrial town or a place of poverty, may feel that the advice trivialises the difficulty. Seeing beauty in a windswept coast for six weeks is not the same as living there for life. This objection is not fatal, since Stevenson presents his view as a paradox and an exercise rather than a law, but it is a fair one.

Third, there is a note of class and privilege in the traveller's stance. The narrator wanders, selects, and goes home to reflect. His dismissal of the Highlands as a place where he cannot find the right humour assumes that landscape exists to supply pleasant moods to the visitor, and some modern readers find that outlook too consumerist. The people who actually lived in those places appear only as the passing figures of a stone-breaker or a homestead.

Fourth, the passage about the old feud, with its bearded men and bitter women taking hateful counsel, leans on a romantic and slightly stagey picture of the past that a historian might find thin.

None of this makes the essay a failure. Most criticism of it amounts to saying that it is a young man's work, polished but not yet deep, and that its pleasures are modest. That is likely a fair verdict, and the essay's quiet success lies in how well it does a small thing.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This essay sits in the great tradition of the English familiar essay, the conversational, personal piece that wanders from observation to reflection. That tradition runs from Montaigne, the sixteenth-century Frenchman who invented the form, through Addison and Steele, Charles Lamb and William Hazlitt, to Stevenson's own contemporaries. Stevenson was a conscious heir of Lamb and Hazlitt, and he wrote essays throughout his life. This one, from his early twenties, shows him finding his voice within that tradition.

It also belongs to the Romantic and Victorian habit of writing about landscape as a matter of feeling. Wordsworth stands behind it, and is quoted directly, as does Shelley, and the allusion to Wuthering Heights shows that Stevenson knew how a novel could use a little patch of sunshine and grass. At the same time, the essay is a reaction against the grand Romantic taste for sublime mountains. Stevenson prefers the tame and the small. In that he anticipates a modern sensibility that values the ordinary landscape, from the nature writing of the twentieth century to the growing interest in everyday places.

Among his own works, it belongs with the pieces that grew out of his early wandering in Scotland and France, and it points ahead to the travel books that made him known. It also foreshadows a moral thread that runs through much of his writing, an optimistic doctrine that happiness is a duty and a skill that can be practised. His later essays on that theme are better known and stronger, but the seed is here.

If you compare it with other writers on place, you can see the contrast. Henry David Thoreau, writing a couple of decades earlier, also urged close attention to a modest local landscape, though with a more severe and challenging spirit. Stevenson is gentler and more social. Later essayists of walking and place, from Virginia Woolf's London wanderings to modern nature writers, share his faith that the quality of attention changes the quality of a place.

Why read it now? Because most of us spend much of our lives in places we did not choose, and because the habit it recommends, looking again, patiently, from one side after another, costs nothing and works more often than we expect. It is short, it is lovely in its last pages, and it leaves you a little more willing to find the shelter in whatever wind you are standing in.
