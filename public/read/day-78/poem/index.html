Nightly Poem • War & Witness Poetry

Futility

Wilfred Owen (1918) • 3 min read

Move him into the sun: beside a young soldier frozen to death in French snow, Owen asks why the ancient sun that woke the clays of earth cannot rouse one still-warm body.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Futility is a fourteen line poem by Wilfred Owen, and it is one of the shortest and saddest things written about the First World War. Owen was a young English officer and poet. He wrote it in the spring of 1918, and it appeared that June in a London weekly called The Nation, which makes it one of the very few of his poems to be published while he was alive. He was killed in action in early November, a week before the war ended. By then Owen had seen horrors at the front and spent time recovering from shell shock, and he had decided that his job as a poet was to tell the truth about what he saw. This poem tells it in the quietest possible way. There is no battle, no gunfire, no enemy. A soldier lies dead in the snow, and his comrades are asked to move him into the sunlight. Listen for how the questions get bigger.

Move him into the sun—

Gently its touch awoke him once,

At home, whispering of fields unsown.

Always it woke him, even in France,

Until this morning and this snow.

If anything might rouse him now

The kind old sun will know.

Think how it wakes the seeds—

Woke, once, the clays of a cold star.

Are limbs so dear-achieved, are sides

Full-nerved—still warm—too hard to stir?

Was it for this the clay grew tall?

—O what made fatuous sunbeams toil

To break earth’s sleep at all?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The scene is almost embarrassingly simple. A man has died, and someone is speaking to the others around him. Move him into the sun, the speaker says. That is the whole action. Everything else is memory and thought. The speaker recalls that the sun's touch once woke this man gently, at home, whispering of fields unsown. That phrase suggests a farm boy or a country soldier, someone who used to rise to the warmth of a spring morning and the prospect of work in the fields, the planting that had not yet been done. Always it woke him, even in France, the poem says, and then the sentence stops short: until this morning and this snow.

Notice the weight of that last phrase. The sun has always been the thing that got him up. Today there is snow. Today he does not wake. The speaker is not stupid, and does not really believe the sun can bring him back, but he cannot stop himself from trying. If anything might rouse him now, the kind old sun will know. That word kind is important. The sun is imagined as a loving presence, almost a parent, who would surely be able to do what it has always done.

The second stanza lifts the poem into a much bigger frame. Think how it wakes the seeds, says the speaker, and then goes further, to say that the sun once woke the clays of a cold star. This refers to the formation of life on earth. In the older story, the sun's energy warmed the lifeless clay of a cold planet and somehow produced living things. So the sun is responsible not only for this man's mornings but for the whole history of life.

Then comes the pain. If the sun could do all that, if it could turn inert clay into walking, feeling bodies, why can it not stir this one? Are limbs so dear achieved, are sides full nerved, still warm, too hard to stir? The man's body is still warm, and it was achieved at enormous cost over countless ages. The final two lines turn the question into a bitter accusation. Was it for this the clay grew tall? And the poem ends with the word fatuous, meaning foolish and pointless, aimed at the sunbeams. The sun toiled to break earth's sleep at all, and what was the point?

So the poem moves from a small, tender gesture to a cosmic despair. It is not only asking why this man died. It is asking whether life was ever worth beginning, if it can be ended so casually. The word futility in the title is the answer it fears.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Futility is a very economical poem, and its economy is its triumph. In fourteen short lines, split into two stanzas of seven, it covers a personal moment, a memory of home, a scientific idea about the origins of life, and an existential complaint. It does this with almost no ornament. Compared with some of Owen's more crowded poems, this one is clean and clear.

The tone is extraordinary. The opening line is spoken in a soft, practical voice, the way someone might speak to a friend carrying a stretcher. Because the speaker is calm, we feel the control that is holding grief down. Then the control starts to give way. The syntax in the second stanza becomes a series of questions, each longer and more strained than the last, until the final line spills out with an impatient, broken anger. The shape of the sentences enacts the emotion.

The pararhyme again does subtle work. Owen pairs words such as sun, once and unsown, and France and snow with now and know in a loosely echoing pattern. The rhymes almost match, then slide off, and the feeling is of something just out of reach, like the sun that cannot rouse the dead. This is a case where a sound technique seems to mean something in itself.

The poem is also notable for the way it uses a plain, almost childlike image, the sun, and loads it with a lot of weight. The idea that the sun is kind, that it wakes seeds, and that it once woke the clay of a star gives the poem a mythic reach without any grand language. The sun here is both a literal heat source and an old symbol of life, divinity and hope.

On the critical side, there are some places where the poem is less sure. The phrase limbs so dear achieved, with its inverted wording, has a slightly strained quality, and a few readers find the second stanza a little more abstract and philosophical than the first, losing some of the physical immediacy that makes the opening so strong. The thought of the cold star, though striking, asks the reader to follow a leap that is not fully explained. And the final word, fatuous, is a bit of a stretch. It is an unusual word for a poem so otherwise simple, and it can seem like the poet reaching for a sharp, clever insult at the moment where plain feeling might have served.

There is also a question of whether the poem's despair is earned or merely asserted. The poem does not argue its case. It simply poses questions that have no answer, which some readers find profound and others find a little easy. Still, in the space of fourteen lines, the objections feel small. The poem hits its mark, and does so in a way that is impossible to forget.

III. The Impact It Had

The immediate impact of Futility was modest. It appeared in a respected London weekly in the summer of 1918, but Owen was not a famous poet, and the poem did not change the course of the war or his career. What it did was establish him, for the few who noticed, as a serious new voice, one of the handful of poems that appeared in print before his death. After he was killed, the larger story began.

When Siegfried Sassoon and others gathered Owen's work for publication in 1920, and then when later editions appeared, Futility was one of the poems that readers returned to. It has remained among the most anthologized of all the First World War poems, and is often paired with Anthem for Doomed Youth and Dulce et Decorum Est. In British schools it is widely taught, in part because it is so short, in part because it offers a clear example of how a poem can build from an everyday image to a huge idea.

The poem's reach has extended well beyond the classroom. It has been used in remembrance services and read at memorials, and it is quoted whenever people want to express the sense of waste in a young death. The opening line has become a kind of shorthand for the tender, helpless gesture of caring for the dead.

Its influence on poets has been steady. Later war poets, from the Second World War onward, have taken from Owen the lesson that a poem about death can be very small and very quiet. Poets who write about grief of any kind, not only war, often work in the same register, the plain voice, the sudden leap to larger questions. In particular, the strategy of using a natural image, like the sun, to hold a philosophical doubt about the whole point of existence has been widely imitated.

The poem has also been set to music. Owen's poems have drawn many composers, and Britten's War Requiem is the best known example, though it draws on other Owen poems than this one.

Perhaps the most important effect is the way it shaped our sense of what a war poem can be. Before Owen, many people expected war poems to deal in battle scenes, courage and sacrifice. Futility has no battle and no courage, only the aftermath, a body in the snow and a voice asking why. That quiet focus on a single death, and on the question of meaning, has become one of the central modes of modern war writing.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love Futility first for its humanity. The poem begins with a gesture that anyone can understand, the instinct to put a dead friend in the warmth. It does not require any knowledge of history. You do not need to know which battle or which year. You only need to have ever wished, against reason, that someone you loved could be woken. That universality is the heart of its appeal.

They love its brevity. Many readers have said that they can memorize it easily, and that they carry it around. It is a poem you can take in at one breath, and yet it unfolds with surprising depth on repeated reading. The sense that so much is packed into so few lines gives it an almost proverbial feel.

The sun has been another favorite element. Readers admire the way Owen takes a familiar, comforting image and quietly turns it into a source of pain. The sun in the first stanza is a friend, the sun in the second is an agent of cosmic creation, and then, in the last lines, the sun is accused of foolishness. That arc, from comfort to doubt, mirrors the way grief can sour the things that once consoled us. People who have lost someone often find that the warmth of a spring day feels cruel, and this poem names that feeling exactly.

Many admirers also value its honesty about doubt. Owen had been raised in a devout Christian household and had once considered the church as a career. Futility is not a rejection of faith in a simple way, but it does question the idea of a purposeful creation. Readers who struggle with similar questions have found in it a companion, a recognition that the loss of one person can shake the whole structure of belief.

The poem's quiet tone has been praised by other writers as a model of restraint. In a period when much of the writing about the war was either shrill propaganda or shouting protest, Owen's gentle, grieving voice stood out. Poets and critics have often held it up as proof that understatement can be more powerful than outrage.

Finally, there is a simple admiration for the ending. The last line, with its fatuous sunbeams, may be the bitterest and most memorable thing in the poem. Many readers say they feel a jolt at it, because after the tenderness of the beginning, the reversal is so sudden. It leaves a lingering ache, and for many people that is exactly why they return to the poem.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Futility has been so widely admired that real criticism of it is less common than for some of Owen's other poems, and it is fair to say that no single famous attack on it exists. Still, various objections have been raised by critics and readers over the years, and they are worth setting out honestly.

One concerns the poem's philosophical thinness. Some readers have felt that the move from a dead soldier to the origins of life is a leap that the poem has not earned, and that its despair is asserted instead of argued. The questions it asks, why did life begin at all, was it for this, are the kind that cannot be answered, and a skeptic might say that a poem posing them has simply declared its own sadness and moved on. The conclusion feels inevitable because the tone has already prepared us for it.

A related complaint is about its rhetorical stance. The poem ends by blaming the sun, or the universe, for the soldier's death. Critics have pointed out that this shifts attention away from the human causes of the war, the generals, the governments and the machinery of killing. Where Owen's other poems are fiercely specific in naming what kills his men, this one blames nothing in particular. For readers who value Owen's political edge, the cosmic framing can seem like an evasion, a sorrow so large that it lets everyone off the hook.

There is, too, the matter of diction and syntax. The inversions, the slightly archaic phrasing, the choice of the word fatuous, have struck some readers as stagey. A few critics have felt that the poem's final line is more clever than felt, a flourish that draws attention to itself. A poem so concerned with plain grief, on this view, ought to have ended more plainly.

Some historians and scholars have raised the broader point that Owen's war poetry, in its sense of pure waste, does not represent the full range of soldiers' feelings, and that Futility, as an especially bleak example, has been used to stand for a view of the war as meaningless that many veterans did not share. This is a criticism not so much of the poem itself as of how it has been used.

Finally, a few readers have found the poem sentimental, because the central image, the dead boy from the fields woken by the sun, draws on a familiar picture of rural innocence. They argue that it idealizes the soldiers as farm boys and leans on a well worn pastoral sweetness. Others reply that the sentiment is genuine and held in check, and that the sharp last lines cut the sweetness. As with many short poems, the disagreement turns on how much feeling a few lines can bear.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Futility sits within the First World War poetry that grew out of the trenches, but it also belongs to broader traditions. The most obvious is the elegy, the poem of mourning. It lacks the formal apparatus of the great English elegies, the pastoral landscapes and the consoling resurrection, and in that absence it declares something about the modern age. The traditional elegy ends in consolation, and this one ends in a question mark.

It also belongs to a long line of poems that address the sun. From the ancient hymns of Egypt to the Romantic poets, the sun has been a symbol of life, truth and divinity. Owen takes that inheritance and reverses it. The sun is still the source of life, but it appears powerless or even stupid in the face of death. This is a distinctly modern move, one that echoes the loss of confidence in an ordered, benevolent universe that spread across Europe after 1914. Readers sometimes compare it with Thomas Hardy, whose poems also turn a cold eye on the idea of a caring creation.

Among his contemporaries, Futility is a close cousin of Sassoon's brief, bitter poems, though Owen's is sadder and less satirical. It is also related, in its compression and imagery, to the work of Isaac Rosenberg, who wrote about the war with a strange and ancient sense of earth and body. Alongside these, it forms part of a body of poetry that changed what English readers expected from verse about war.

Looking forward, the poem anticipates the existential tone of later twentieth century literature, in which the question of whether life has any purpose is asked in the face of mass death. One can hear its note in poets responding to the Second World War and the Holocaust, and in many writers who confront loss without the support of religious certainty. Its way of moving from a single body to the universe resembles the method of many later elegies, which find the cosmic in the intimate.

Poets who write about grief today, not only in connection with war, still draw on its example. The idea that a poem can be built around a simple request and a few questions, and that it can end without answers, has become one of the standard patterns of contemporary elegy.

Why read it now? Because it demonstrates, perhaps better than any other short poem, how much feeling can be carried by a few plain words. It takes less than a minute to read, and it is the kind of poem that reaches anyone who has stood next to a loss that makes no sense. It does not argue, and it does not comfort. It simply asks the oldest question there is, and then lets the silence after it speak.
