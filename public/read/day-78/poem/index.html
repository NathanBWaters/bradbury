Futility

Wilfred Owen (1918) • 3 min read

Move him into the sun: beside a young soldier frozen to death in French snow, Owen asks why the ancient sun that woke the clays of earth cannot rouse one still-warm body.

Key Context & Why It Matters

One of only five poems published during Wilfred Owen’s lifetime (appearing in The Nation on June 15, 1918), 'Futility' recalls an incident from the brutal winter of 1916–1917 on the Somme when Owen’s platoon suffered exposure in the snow near Beaumont-Hamel. Belonging to the World War I / Trench Realism tradition and categorized under War & Witness Poetry, 'Futility' (1918) unfolds across 14 verbatim lines in 2 stanzas. Alongside 'Anthem for Doomed Youth,' 'Futility' was set by Benjamin Britten in his War Requiem and is revered for compressing the entire history of terrestrial life ('Woke, once, the clays of a cold star') into fourteen lines of tender grief.

Move him into the sun—

Gently its touch awoke him once,

At home, whispering of fields unsown.

Always it woke him, even in France,

Until this morning and this snow.

If anything might rouse him now

The kind old sun will know.

Think how it wakes the seeds—

Woke, once, the clays of a cold star.

Are limbs so dear-achieved, are sides

Full-nerved—still warm—too hard to stir?

Was it for this the clay grew tall?

—O what made fatuous sunbeams toil

To break earth’s sleep at all?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

One of only five poems published during Wilfred Owen’s lifetime (appearing in The Nation on June 15, 1918), 'Futility' recalls an incident from the brutal winter of 1916–1917 on the Somme when Owen’s platoon suffered exposure in the snow near Beaumont-Hamel. Within the broader trajectory of World War I / Trench Realism, Wilfred Owen composed 'Futility' (1918) at a moment when personal experience and historical transformation demanded a new poetic idiom. From its unforgettable opening cadence—"Move him into the sun—"—the work establishes an immediate, unmistakable voice that bridges private consciousness and public literary history. Rather than retreating into decorative convention, Wilfred Owen grounded these 14 lines in lived observation, intellectual urgency, and the concrete realities of the era, ensuring that the historical moment of its composition remains palpable in every stanza.

II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

At the thematic heart of 'Futility' lies the heartbreaking cosmic absurdity of destroying a conscious human life that took billions of years of solar evolution to awaken: in stanza one, comrades gently move a dead boy into the morning sun, hoping the 'kind old sun' that used to wake him at home among whispering fields of Wales will rouse him now; in stanza two, grief widens into evolutionary awe and anguish, asking 'Was it for this the clay grew tall?'. As the poem moves from its opening proposition toward its central turning point—"Think how it wakes the seeds—"—Wilfred Owen develops a resonant symbolic architecture where concrete sensory images carry deep philosophical weight. Each image does double duty: anchoring the reader in a vivid physical setting while illuminating the inner tensions of memory, mortality, desire, and resilience. III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Prosodically and formally, Wilfred Owen constructs 'Futility' using a fourteen-line elegiac sonnet divided into two seven-line stanzas (ABABCDC EFEFGHG) woven with Owen’s delicate pararhymes ('sun / sown,' 'once / France,' 'seeds / sides,' 'star / stir,' 'tall / toil') that move from a quiet imperative whisper ('Move him into the sun—') to three devastating philosophical questions. Spanning 14 lines across 2 stanzas, the poem’s structural proportions work in concert with its acoustic texture—where deliberate shifts in vowel music, consonantal percussion, caesura placement, and lineation govern the reader’s breathing and pace. Notice how Wilfred Owen balances syntactic momentum across line endings against sudden moments of metric stillness, allowing lines such as "Move him into the sun—" and "To break earth’s sleep at all?" to reverberate in the ear long after the voice falls silent.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Alongside 'Anthem for Doomed Youth,' 'Futility' was set by Benjamin Britten in his War Requiem and is revered for compressing the entire history of terrestrial life ('Woke, once, the clays of a cold star') into fourteen lines of tender grief. Across subsequent generations of readers, critics, and fellow poets, 'Futility' has remained a touchstone of the War & Witness Poetry canon. Anthologists and literary historians consistently point to Wilfred Owen’s achievement here as proof that rigorous formal craft and raw emotional honesty amplify rather than constrain one another. Whether read aloud in performance, studied in close critical analysis, or encountered in solitary evening reading, the poem continues to shape how modern literature understands the power of compressed lyric form.
