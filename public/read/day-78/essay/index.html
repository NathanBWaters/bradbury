Roads

Robert Louis Stevenson (1873) • 14 min read

Nothing captures the unrest and poetry of the human spirit like the white ribbon of a country road winding over the hill into the unknown.

Key Context & Why It Matters

In 1873 a young Robert Louis Stevenson, then in his early twenties and still half-heartedly training to be an engineer in Edinburgh, published this essay in a magazine called The Portfolio. It is one of his earliest pieces of published prose, and it is about something so ordinary we hardly notice it: the road. Stevenson was the son and grandson of builders of lighthouses and harbours, so he grew up around people who made things out of rock and engineering, but he wanted to write. Here he argues that the best way to learn to love landscape is not through grand mountains but through quiet country, where a winding road becomes endless company. A road, he says, has life and character, it connects us to other people, and above all it carries our longing for whatever lies beyond the next hill. The essay is slow, ornate and a little bookish, but it contains the seed of his lifelong love of walking and travel. Listen for the way an everyday thing turns into a kind of poetry.

No amateur will deny that he can find more pleasure in a single drawing, over which he can sit a whole quiet forenoon, and so gradually study himself into humour with the artist, than he can ever extract from the dazzle and accumulation of incongruous impressions that send him, weary and stupefied, out of some famous picture-gallery. But what is thus admitted with regard to art is not extended to the (so-called) natural beauties no amount of excess in sublime mountain outline or the graces of cultivated lowland can do anything, it is supposed, to weaken or degrade the palate. We are not at all sure, however, that moderation, and a regimen tolerably austere, even in scenery, are not healthful and strengthening to the taste; and that the best school for a lover of nature is not to the found in one of those countries where there is no stage effect⁠—nothing salient or sudden⁠—but a quiet spirit of orderly and harmonious beauty pervades all the details, so that we can patiently attend to each of the little touches that strike in us, all of them together, the subdued note of the landscape. It is in scenery such as this that we find ourselves in the right temper to seek out small sequestered loveliness. The constant recurrence of similar combinations of colour and outline gradually forces upon us a sense of how the harmony has been built up, and we become familiar with something of nature’s mannerism. This is the true pleasure of your “rural voluptuary,”⁠—not to remain awestricken before a Mount Chimborazo; not to sit deafened over the big drum in the orchestra, but day by day to teach himself some new beauty⁠—to experience some new vague and tranquil sensation that has before evaded him. It is not the people who “have pined and hungered after nature many a year, in the great city pent,” as Coleridge said in the poem that made Charles Lamb so much ashamed of himself; it is not those who make the greatest progress in this intimacy with her, or who are most quick to see and have the greatest gusto to enjoy. In this, as in everything else, it is minute knowledge and long-continued loving industry that make the true dilettante. A man must have thought much over scenery before he begins fully to enjoy it. It is no youngling enthusiasm on hilltops that can possess itself of the last essence of beauty. Probably most people’s heads are growing bare before they can see all in a landscape that they have the capability of seeing; and, even then, it will be only for one little moment of consummation before the faculties are again on the decline, and they that look out of the windows begin to be darkened and restrained in sight. Thus the study of nature should be carried forward thoroughly and with system. Every gratification should be rolled long under the tongue, and we should be always eager to analyse and compare, in order that we may be able to give some plausible reason for our admirations. True, it is difficult to put even approximately into words the kind of feelings thus called into play. There is a dangerous vice inherent in any such intellectual refining upon vague sensation. The analysis of such satisfactions lends itself very readily to literary affectations; and we can all think of instances where it has shown itself apt to exercise a morbid influence, even upon an author’s choice of language and the turn of his sentences. And yet there is much that makes the attempt attractive; for any expression, however imperfect, once given to a cherished feeling, seems a sort of legitimation of the pleasure we take in it. A common sentiment is one of those great goods that make life palatable and ever new. The knowledge that another has felt as we have felt, and seen things, even if they are little things, not much otherwise than we have seen them, will continue to the end to be one of life’s choicest pleasures.

Let the reader, then, betake himself in the spirit we have recommended to some of the quieter kinds of English landscape. In those homely and placid agricultural districts, familiarity will bring into relief many things worthy of notice, and urge them pleasantly home to him by a sort of loving repetition; such as the wonderful life-giving speed of windmill sails above the stationary country; the occurrence and recurrence of the same church tower at the end of one long vista after another: and, conspicuous among these sources of quiet pleasure, the character and variety of the road itself, along which he takes his way. Not only near at hand, in the lithe contortions with which it adapts itself to the interchanges of level and slope, but far away also, when he sees a few hundred feet of it upheaved against a hill and shining in the afternoon sun, he will find it an object so changeful and enlivening that he can always pleasurably busy his mind about it. He may leave the riverside, or fall out of the way of villages, but the road he has always with him; and, in the true humour of observation, will find in that sufficient company. From its subtle windings and changes of level there arises a keen and continuous interest, that keeps the attention ever alert and cheerful. Every sensitive adjustment to the contour of the ground, every little dip and swerve, seems instinct with life and an exquisite sense of balance and beauty. The road rolls upon the easy slopes of the country, like a long ship in the hollows of the sea. The very margins of waste ground, as they trench a little farther on the beaten way, or recede again to the shelter of the hedge, have something of the same free delicacy of line⁠—of the same swing and wilfulness. You might think for a whole summer’s day (and not have thought it any nearer an end by evening) what concourse and succession of circumstances has produced the least of these deflections; and it is, perhaps, just in this that we should look for the secret of their interest. A footpath across a meadow⁠—in all its human waywardness and unaccountability, in all the grata protervitas of its varying direction⁠—will always be more to us than a railroad well engineered through a difficult country. No reasoned sequence is thrust upon our attention: we seem to have slipped for one lawless little moment out of the iron rule of cause and effect; and so we revert at once to some of the pleasant old heresies of personification, always poetically orthodox, and attribute a sort of freewill, an active and spontaneous life, to the white ribbon of road that lengthens out, and bends, and cunningly adapts itself to the inequalities of the land before our eyes. We remember, as we write, some miles of fine wide highway laid out with conscious aesthetic artifice through a broken and richly cultivated tract of country. It is said that the engineer had Hogarth’s line of beauty in his mind as he laid them down. And the result is striking. One splendid satisfying sweep passes with easy transition into another, and there is nothing to trouble or dislocate the strong continuousness of the main line of the road. And yet there is something wanting. There is here no saving imperfection, none of those secondary curves and little trepidations of direction that carry, in natural roads, our curiosity actively along with them. One feels at once that this road has not has been laboriously grown like a natural road, but made to pattern; and that, while a model may be academically correct in outline, it will always be inanimate and cold. The traveller is also aware of a sympathy of mood between himself and the road he travels. We have all seen ways that have wandered into heavy sand near the seacoast, and trail wearily over the dunes like a trodden serpent. Here we too must plod forward at a dull, laborious pace; and so a sympathy is preserved between our frame of mind and the expression of the relaxed, heavy curves of the roadway. Such a phenomenon, indeed, our reason might perhaps resolve with a little trouble. We might reflect that the present road had been developed out of a tract spontaneously followed by generations of primitive wayfarers; and might see in its expression a testimony that those generations had been affected at the same ground, one after another, in the same manner as we are affected today. Or we might carry the reflection further, and remind ourselves that where the air is invigorating and the ground firm under the traveller’s foot, his eye is quick to take advantage of small undulations, and he will turn carelessly aside from the direct way wherever there is anything beautiful to examine or some promise of a wider view; so that even a bush of wild roses may permanently bias and deform the straight path over the meadow; whereas, where the soil is heavy, one is preoccupied with the labour of mere progression, and goes with a bowed head heavily and unobservantly forward. Reason, however, will not carry us the whole way; for the sentiment often recurs in situations where it is very hard to imagine any possible explanation; and indeed, if we drive briskly along a good, well-made road in an open vehicle, we shall experience this sympathy almost at its fullest. We feel the sharp settle of the springs at some curiously twisted corner; after a steep ascent, the fresh air dances in our faces as we rattle precipitately down the other side, and we find it difficult to avoid attributing something headlong, a sort of abandon, to the road itself.

The mere winding of the path is enough to enliven a long day’s walk in even a commonplace or dreary countryside. Something that we have seen from miles back, upon an eminence, is so long hid from us, as we wander through folded valleys or among woods, that our expectation of seeing it again is sharpened into a violent appetite, and as we draw nearer we impatiently quicken our steps and turn every corner with a beating heart. It is through these prolongations of expectancy, this succession of one hope to another, that we live out long seasons of pleasure in a few hours’ walk. It is in following these capricious sinuosities that we learn, only bit by bit and through one coquettish reticence after another, much as we learn the heart of a friend, the whole loveliness of the country. This disposition always preserves something new to be seen, and takes us, like a careful cicerone, to many different points of distant view before it allows us finally to approach the hoped-for destination.

In its connection with the traffic, and whole friendly intercourse with the country, there is something very pleasant in that succession of saunterers and brisk and businesslike passersby, that peoples our ways and helps to build up what Walt Whitman calls “the cheerful voice of the public road, the gay, fresh sentiment of the road.” But out of the great network of ways that binds all life together from the hill-farm to the city, there is something individual to most, and, on the whole, nearly as much choice on the score of company as on the score of beauty or easy travel. On some we are never long without the sound of wheels, and folk pass us by so thickly that we lose the sense of their number. But on others, about little-frequented districts, a meeting is an affair of moment; we have the sight far off of someone coming towards us, the growing definiteness of the person, and then the brief passage and salutation, and the road left empty in front of us for perhaps a great while to come. Such encounters have a wistful interest that can hardly be understood by the dweller in places more populous. We remember standing beside a countryman once, in the mouth of a quiet by-street in a city that was more than ordinarily crowded and bustling; he seemed stunned and bewildered by the continual passage of different faces; and after a long pause, during which he appeared to search for some suitable expression, he said timidly that there seemed to be a great deal of meeting thereabouts. The phrase is significant. It is the expression of town-life in the language of the long, solitary country highways. A meeting of one with one was what this man had been used to in the pastoral uplands from which he came; and the concourse of the streets was in his eyes only an extraordinary multiplication of such “meetings.”

And now we come to that last and most subtle quality of all, to that sense of prospect, of outlook, that is brought so powerfully to our minds by a road. In real nature, as well as in old landscapes, beneath that impartial daylight in which a whole variegated plain is plunged and saturated, the line of the road leads the eye forth with the vague sense of desire up to the green limit of the horizon. Travel is brought home to us, and we visit in spirit every grove and hamlet that tempts us in the distance. Sehnsucht⁠—the passion for what is ever beyond⁠—is livingly expressed in that white ribbon of possible travel that severs the uneven country; not a ploughman following his plough up the shining furrow, not the blue smoke of any cottage in a hollow, but is brought to us with a sense of nearness and attainability by this wavering line of junction. There is a passionate paragraph in Werther that strikes the very key. “When I came hither,” he writes, “how the beautiful valley invited me on every side, as I gazed down into it from the hilltop! There the wood⁠—ah, that I might mingle in its shadows! there the mountain summits⁠—ah, that I might look down from them over the broad country! the interlinked hills! the secret valleys! Oh to lose myself among their mysteries! I hurried into the midst, and came back without finding aught I hoped for. Alas! the distance is like the future. A vast whole lies in the twilight before our spirit; sight and feeling alike plunge and lose themselves in the prospect, and we yearn to surrender our whole being, and let it be filled full with all the rapture of one single glorious sensation; and alas! when we hasten to the fruition, when there is changed to here, all is afterwards as it was before, and we stand in our indigent and cramped estate, and our soul thirsts after a still ebbing elixir.” It is to this wandering and uneasy spirit of anticipation that roads minister. Every little vista, every little glimpse that we have of what lies before us, gives the impatient imagination rein, so that it can outstrip the body and already plunge into the shadow of the woods, and overlook from the hilltop the plain beyond it, and wander in the windings of the valleys that are still far in front. The road is already there⁠—we shall not be long behind. It is as if we were marching with the rear of a great army, and, from far before, heard the acclamation of the people as the vanguard entered some friendly and jubilant city. Would not every man, through all the long miles of march, feel as if he also were within the gates?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Stevenson begins with an analogy from art. Anyone who loves pictures knows that you can get more pleasure from sitting a whole quiet morning with a single drawing than from rushing through a famous gallery and leaving stupefied. We admit that for art, he says, but we do not extend the point to nature. We assume no amount of sublime mountains can dull the palate. He suggests the opposite: a rather austere regimen even in scenery may be healthy for the taste, and the best school for a lover of nature is a country with no stage effects, where a quiet harmony runs through all the details.

In such quiet scenery, he says, we learn patience and the habit of small observation. The true pleasure of what he calls the rural voluptuary is not to stand awestruck before a great peak or sit deafened by the big drum in an orchestra, but to teach oneself some new beauty every day. He adds that it takes years: most people's heads are growing bare before they can see all that a landscape holds. Notice his humility about the difficulty of putting such feelings into words, and his remark that sharing a feeling with another person is one of life's choicest pleasures.

Then he turns to his subject. In placid farm country, the road becomes the chief object of interest. Its curves adapt to the slopes, it shines in the distance against a hill, and it follows the ground like a long ship in the hollows of the sea. Its edges have a free delicacy. A footpath across a meadow, with all its human waywardness, will always mean more to us than a railway engineered through hard country, because it seems to slip us out of the iron rule of cause and effect. Here is the key idea: natural roads are alive because they have been grown by countless travellers' choices. He contrasts a highway laid out to follow Hogarth's line of beauty, which is correct but cold, with the saving imperfection of real tracks.

He goes on to say that the mood of a road matches the mood of the walker, and that winding paths prolong expectancy. A road hides what it will show next, and so, step by step, we learn the country as we learn the heart of a friend. Roads also carry company: the traveller, the farmer, a countryman who once said that there seemed a great deal of meeting in the city.

The last and most important layer is longing. The road leads the eye toward the horizon and stands for what the Germans call Sehnsucht, the passion for what is always beyond. Quoting Goethe's Werther, he says distance is like the future. The road lets imagination run ahead of the body, so that we feel as if we were already within the gates, like a soldier marching at the rear of an army that hears the cheering as the vanguard enters a city.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

At its best this essay is graceful, and its core idea is original: a road is a living line, not merely a means of getting somewhere. The sentence about the road rolling on the easy slopes like a long ship in the hollows of the sea is the kind of image that makes an argument memorable. So is the comparison of a straight, engineered highway to a model that is academically correct but inanimate and cold. Stevenson has a fine gift for finding a general truth inside a small observation, and the contrast between a footpath and a railway is a lovely way to express a worry about modern life that was only beginning to be voiced.

The structure is also sound. It moves from a general principle about art and scenery to the particular subject, then through a sequence of qualities, form, mood, expectation, company, and finally the metaphysical longing that closes it. The countryman's remark about a great deal of meeting is a small masterpiece of anecdote: it makes the point about solitude and town life in one strange, sweet phrase.

But the weaknesses show, and some of them are the weaknesses of youth. The prose is heavily ornamented. Sentences stretch and double back on themselves, quotations pile up, and Latin and German phrases are dropped in without help. A modern reader meeting grata protervitas, Sehnsucht, a rural voluptuary and a quotation from Werther in a few pages may feel that the author is performing for an audience of educated Victorians. The royal we, which Stevenson uses throughout, adds to the stiffness. He later wrote far more plainly and warmly, and this essay is not yet that writer.

The argument also loses focus. The opening, about the gallery and the drawing, takes a long time to reach roads, and the long discussion of why a road's mood matches ours, with its tentative explanations about generations of wayfarers and bushes of wild roses, wanders without settling. There is a charming reasonableness in his admission that reason will not carry us all the way, but it also reads as an unfinished thought.

There is, too, a slight sentimentality in the treatment of country life. The countryman is a figure of quaint simplicity, and the solitary meetings on the road are made to sound more poetic than they might feel to those who walked them out of necessity. This is the view of a young man who can choose his walks.

Still, the ending is excellent. The image of the marching army, hearing the cheers of a city not yet reached, is both original and moving, and it lifts the essay above its mannerisms. A flawed piece, then, but one with a real idea at its centre and a few lines of lasting beauty.

III. The Impact It Had

Roads was one of Stevenson's first published essays, and its significance lies mainly in what it shows about the writer he would become. At the time he was a young Edinburgh man, the son of a family of famous lighthouse engineers, who was expected to follow them into the profession. He studied engineering, then law, and was called to the Scottish bar in 1875, but he spent his energy on writing. Appearing in The Portfolio, an art magazine, in 1873, this essay was an early public step on that path.

The essay is a statement of an attitude that stayed with him: that travel is not about arriving, that the journey itself is the substance, and that a walker sees more than a person in a hurry. In his later book Virginibus Puerisque, he wrote the famous line that to travel hopefully is a better thing than to arrive. That idea is already present here in the claim that it is through the prolongations of expectancy that we live out long seasons of pleasure in a few hours' walk. His first travel book, An Inland Voyage, and then Travels with a Donkey in the Cevennes, which many consider the founding classic of modern walking literature, carry this outlook into practice.

Beyond Stevenson's own career, the essay joins a stream of writing about walking and roads that has grown ever since. Writers on walking from Thoreau before him to many modern authors share his conviction that the slow pace reveals the world. It is also an early example of an aesthetic argument for the irregular, the grown and the organic over the planned and engineered, a theme that would be taken up in discussions of town planning, garden design and the picturesque. His contrast between the natural road and the highway built to a theoretical line anticipates later debates about whether designed landscapes can ever feel alive.

I must be careful here. There is little documented evidence of how this particular essay was received on publication, and it did not make Stevenson famous. It has survived as part of his collected essays and travel writing, and is read by admirers of his work and by anyone interested in the literature of walking. That is a modest legacy, but a real one.

As for teaching, it is sometimes used as an example of Victorian descriptive prose and as an early look at Stevenson's style, valued more for its place in his development than for its status as a standalone classic.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers who love Stevenson's essays tend to prize the way he turns attention into a kind of pleasure. Roads gives them a vocabulary for something they already feel: the way a path curving out of sight pulls us forward, and the particular joy of seeing a bit of road far off, shining on a hillside, long before we reach it. The idea that we learn the country bit by bit, as we learn the heart of a friend, through one coquettish reticence after another, is an image that readers have found delightful, and it applies to more than roads.

The central metaphor of longing also lands for many. The road as a white ribbon of possible travel that cuts through the uneven country, bringing the ploughman and the cottage smoke close to us, expresses an almost universal human feeling, the pull of the horizon. The quotation from Werther about distance being like the future gives this a philosophical depth, and the closing image of marching at the rear of an army that hears the cheers from the front is the sort of ending that readers remember. For people who love to walk, drive or cycle, the essay feels like a small confirmation that their pleasure is a real and respectable one.

Readers have also enjoyed the humour and humanity that peek out between the ornate sentences. The countryman who found that there seemed to be a great deal of meeting in the city is a perfect little story. The road that trails wearily over the dunes like a trodden serpent, matching the walker's heavy mood, is vivid and funny. And the contrast between the life-giving natural road and the one made to pattern gives the essay an argument that anyone who has been disappointed by a too-perfect planned town will recognise.

Admirers of Stevenson's work as a whole often point to the essay as evidence of how early his gift for observation and rhythm appeared. Even in a youthful piece, the cadence of the sentences has a musical quality that makes it a pleasure to read aloud. This essay drew little specific famous praise, but it is generally regarded by Stevenson readers as a charming and revealing early work.

For those who come to it fresh, the surprise is how much a piece about roads has to say about desire, expectation and the way imagination outruns the body. That unexpected depth is what many readers say they love.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

As with many of Stevenson's early essays, there is no famous controversy attached to Roads. The criticisms that can fairly be made are the ones that readers of his early prose have tended to raise.

The most common is that the style is overworked. The essay is full of long, balanced sentences, learned allusions and decorative adjectives. In later life Stevenson himself was frank about having learned to write by imitating admired authors, and critics have seen that apprenticeship here. Some find the result more like an exercise in Victorian fine writing than the natural voice of a man talking. A reader who has just enjoyed the plain vigour of Treasure Island may be surprised by the stately, slightly breathless manner of this earlier essay.

A related complaint is that the argument is loose. The essay drifts from art to scenery, to roads, to the psychology of walking and finally to longing, with little to bind these together except the writer's enthusiasm. Some of the speculation, about why the shape of a road should echo our mood, is tentative and unresolved. A more disciplined essayist might have cut it.

There is also a question of tone. The essay speaks for a cultivated, leisured traveller, and its talk of the rural voluptuary and of studying nature for a lifetime assumes time, money and education. For most people who used roads in the 1870s, a road was a way to get to work, to market or to the next town, not a source of aesthetic delight. The quaint figure of the countryman who has never seen a crowd condescends a little to the people it admires.

Some modern readers also object to the nostalgia of the piece, with its preference for the winding natural road over the engineered highway or railway. Whatever the charm of that view, it can be seen as a romantic resistance to a modern world that offered real benefits, from faster travel to better access. Stevenson himself, the child of engineers, was not so simple, and it is a little ironic that the engineer's son praises the road that no engineer designed.

None of these complaints is severe. They add up to the verdict that the essay is a gifted beginner's work, richer in promise than in perfection. The promise, though, is considerable.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Roads belongs to the long literary tradition of the walker's essay, in which a person on foot finds in the commonplace a reason for reflection. Behind it stand the Romantic poets, above all Wordsworth, who walked thousands of miles and made walking a source of poetry, and the English essayists William Hazlitt and Charles Lamb, whom Stevenson admired. Hazlitt's essay on going a journey is an obvious relation. Through Stevenson, the tradition passes forward to later writers who treat walking as a way of seeing, among them Edward Thomas, who wrote beautifully of English paths, and many modern authors of the long walk and the journey on foot.

The essay also draws on the German Romantic idea of longing, the Sehnsucht Stevenson explicitly names, and the quotation from Goethe's The Sorrows of Young Werther ties it to the European Romantic mood. Walt Whitman's Song of the Open Road, quoted in the essay, shows a link to the American celebration of the road as a symbol of freedom, a symbol that would later reappear in the literature of the highway, the tramp and the journey across a continent. Stevenson's own time in America would deepen that connection.

What descends from it is, in part, the very modern idea that the way we move through a landscape shapes how we see it. Writers on cycling, hiking and slow travel echo his argument, and so do critics of the modern motorway who complain that fast, straight roads cut us off from the country. His preference for the grown path over the planned line also has kinship with later thinkers on cities and gardens, who prize the organic over the imposed.

Compare it with Thoreau's essay Walking, written in the 1850s. Thoreau also treats walking as a form of wisdom, but he is more fierce and prophetic, and reaches for wildness. Stevenson is gentler, prizes the tame and the moderate, and finds his transcendence not in wilderness but in a farm road bending around a hill. The difference tells you something about the two temperaments and the two landscapes.

Why read it now? Partly for the fresh sentences and the image of the marching army. Partly because in a time of satnavs and straight lines, it reminds us of the pleasure of not knowing what is around the bend. And partly to see a famous writer at the start, already looking up the road, and already, as his essay says, a little ahead of his own body.
