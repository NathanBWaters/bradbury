Sonnet 130: My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun

William Shakespeare (1609) • 3 min read

Mocking the artificial clichés of Petrarchan courtly sonneteers, Shakespeare insists his flesh-and-blood mistress treads on the ground—and is all the rarer for being real.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Part of the 'Dark Lady' sequence (Sonnets 127–152) in the 1609 Quarto, Sonnet 130 is a brilliant satirical anti-blazon aimed at the Elizabethan sonnet craze of the 1590s (such as Thomas Watson, Samuel Daniel, and Giles Fletcher), in which poets mechanically compared every lady’s eyes to suns, lips to coral, breasts to snow, and breath to perfume. Belonging to the English Renaissance / Elizabethan tradition and categorized under Elizabethan & Metaphysical, 'Sonnet 130: My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun' (1609) unfolds across 14 verbatim lines in 1 stanza. By dismantling artificial courtly flattery to celebrate an authentic, imperfect human being who walks on solid earth, Sonnet 130 paved the way for modern realist love poetry from John Donne to Philip Larkin.

My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun;

Coral is far more red than her lips’ red;

If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;

If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.

I have seen roses damask’d, red and white,

But no such roses see I in her cheeks;

And in some perfumes is there more delight

Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.

I love to hear her speak, yet well I know

That music hath a far more pleasing sound;

I grant I never saw a goddess go;

My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:

And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare

As any she belied with false compare.
