Sonnet 130: My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun

William Shakespeare (1609) • 3 min read

Mocking the artificial clichés of Petrarchan courtly sonneteers, Shakespeare insists his flesh-and-blood mistress treads on the ground—and is all the rarer for being real.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Sonnet 130 is Shakespeare's funniest love poem, and also one of his most honest. It belongs to a group within his 1609 collection of sonnets that is aimed at a mysterious dark-haired woman, often called the Dark Lady. Nobody knows who she was, or even whether she was real. What we do know is the fashion the poem is laughing at. In the 1590s, English poets were writing sonnets by the dozen, copying a style from Italy in which the beloved's eyes were suns, her lips were coral, her cheeks were roses, and her skin was snow. Readers had heard it all before. Shakespeare takes that list and turns it upside down, describing a woman who is simply a woman. Then, in the last two lines, he springs a surprise. The poem is a joke on bad flattery, but its larger meaning is that real love does not need exaggeration to be genuine.

My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun;

Coral is far more red than her lips’ red;

If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;

If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.

I have seen roses damask’d, red and white,

But no such roses see I in her cheeks;

And in some perfumes is there more delight

Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.

I love to hear her speak, yet well I know

That music hath a far more pleasing sound;

I grant I never saw a goddess go;

My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:

And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare

As any she belied with false compare.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Sonnet 130 begins as an insult and ends as a compliment, and the whole poem is the journey between the two. To enjoy it, it helps to know what it is rebelling against.

In the decades before Shakespeare wrote this, English love poetry had settled into a habit. A poet would adore a lady and describe her body piece by piece, comparing each part to something grand or precious. Her eyes shone like the sun. Her lips were red as coral, her breasts white as snow, her hair like golden wire, her cheeks like roses, her breath like perfume, her voice like music, and her walk like a goddess gliding. This list of comparisons is called a blazon. By the time Shakespeare came along, it had turned into a cliche, and most of the comparisons were so exaggerated that no real woman could live up to them.

So the speaker opens by refusing. "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun." Here "mistress" simply means the woman he loves. Then he goes down the list, and flips each item. Coral is redder than her lips. Snow is whiter than her breasts, which are "dun," a dull grayish brown. If hair is like wires, then "black wires grow on her head." That last one is the joke at its sharpest, because he takes the stock phrase about golden wires literally, and makes it ridiculous.

The second group of four lines continues. He has seen "roses damask'd, red and white," meaning roses streaked with both colors, but not in her cheeks. Some perfumes smell better than "the breath that from my mistress reeks." It is worth knowing that "reeks" did not carry the smell of rot that it has for us. It meant to rise or be given off. Still, the line is meant to sting, and modern readers will hear it that way, which probably adds to the fun.

In the third group he admits that he loves to hear her speak, though music sounds better, and that he has never seen a goddess walk, but his mistress "treads on the ground." That final phrase is wonderfully flat, and a very deliberate puncturing of the old fantasy.

Then the couplet changes the mood: "And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare / As any she belied with false compare." In plain language, he finds his beloved as special as any woman who has ever been lied about with false comparisons. The poem has not been an attack on her at all. It has been an attack on the flatterers. By admitting every flaw, he makes his love believable, and the closing lines carry the force of the one thing he has not exaggerated.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Sonnet 130 is a delight, and a good example of Shakespeare's comic intelligence. Its genius lies in its construction. The reader is led through twelve lines of denial, building a rhythm of expectation, and then the last two lines upend it. Without the couplet, the poem would be a cruel joke about a woman's looks. With it, the whole poem reverses meaning in an instant, as a person who has been grumbling suddenly says something tender. It is a perfect use of the sonnet's built-in turn.

The comedy is also skillful. Shakespeare picks details that sound precise and a little brutal, such as "dun" and "black wires," so that the reader laughs and then feels slightly guilty for laughing. The structure of the jokes matters too. By running through the same pattern, comparison and denial, he creates a steady comic beat, and by breaking the pattern in the third group of lines, with the lovely flat "treads on the ground," he keeps it fresh.

There is also real linguistic care. Notice that the poem uses very plain words, mostly one syllable. That plainness is itself the argument. A poem that wants to say "she is real" should speak in a real voice, and this one does. Compare the pomp of the poems it mocks.

Now for the less shining side. The first is that the poem's satire is somewhat easy. Shooting at an old cliche is not very difficult, and a modern reader who has never read the sonnets it mocks may feel they are watching someone argue with a ghost. The joke lands better with context than without it.

The second, and more serious, is that the poem is not entirely kind to the woman. A reader today may feel an uncomfortable edge in the lines about her breath and her hair. The intention is to mock the poets, but the actual details are about a woman's body, and the speaker is happy to list her failings. Some readers feel the tenderness of the couplet does not fully pay back that sting.

The third is that the poem is quite unevenly weighted. The first twelve lines are all the same trick, and the real emotion arrives in only two. There is a risk that the final claim feels a little thin next to the extended joke. Some readers would like more of the love and less of the list.

Finally, the ending is slightly ambiguous. "I think my love as rare" is hedged by the word "think," and the phrase "false compare" can suggest that every lover exaggerates. It is not entirely clear whether the speaker is being sincere or ironic. This ambiguity can be seen as a strength or a weakness, but it prevents the poem from being a simple declaration of devotion. On balance the craft is superb, and the faults are the faults of a poem that is trying to be comic and sincere at once.

III. The Impact It Had

The influence of Sonnet 130 is not about changing history so much as changing tone. It stands as one of the best-known examples of a love poem that makes fun of love poems, and it helped establish the idea that you can mock a convention and still honor what lies underneath it.

In Shakespeare's own time, the poem was part of a lively conversation about the sonnet itself. By the 1590s, the form was so popular that it was ripe for parody, and other writers also joked about the standard comparisons. Shakespeare's version proved the most memorable. It is a reminder that even in the age of the sonnet, poets were tired of the style and playing games with it.

Within Shakespeare's own work, the poem points to a stance he returns to throughout his plays. Time after time, his characters puncture inflated language. In comedies like Much Ado About Nothing, Benedick and Beatrice use wit to sidestep romantic cliche. In plays like Love's Labour's Lost, characters write extravagant love verses and are teased for it. Sonnet 130 is a compact example of the same attitude: mistrust of hot air, and trust in plain truth.

For later poets, the poem became a model for anti-romantic romance. Writers who wanted to speak of love without sentimentality have found a precedent here. You can hear its spirit in the plain-spoken love poems of the twentieth century, and in writers who deliberately avoid grand metaphors. It has also inspired many parodies and imitations, from schoolchildren's assignments to humorous verses and greeting cards. Many people have written their own versions, comparing a partner to something unglamorous and then ending with sincere affection.

In schools, the poem is a favorite because it is so easy to teach. Students can immediately grasp the joke, and in doing so they learn what a convention is. A teacher can lay out the original list of flattering comparisons, ask students to see how each one is reversed, and then ask why the final couplet changes everything. It is a gentle introduction to irony and to the shape of a sonnet.

The poem has also been used in popular culture, including in advertisements, television, and as the basis for comic recitations. Its first line is among the most recognized in the entire sequence.

Finally, there is its quiet effect on how people think about beauty. The poem suggests that real affection looks at a real person, not an idealized picture, and that this is a deeper compliment than flattery. That idea, that being loved as you are is better than being praised as you are not, has become a common theme in modern romance, and Sonnet 130 is one of its oldest statements.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love Sonnet 130 first of all because it is funny, and funny in a way that has not aged. A four-hundred-year-old joke about hair like wires still makes people smile. That alone is rare. Most old humor needs a footnote, and this needs only a little.

They also love its honesty. There is a particular relief in reading a love poem that does not lie to you. Many people have felt the strain of the idealized romance, in films, in songs, in cards, and have wondered whether their own plainer feelings count. This poem says they do. It tells a person that you can see the flaws and still find the beloved precious, and that this may be the real thing.

The poem's voice is another pleasure. The speaker sounds like a real man, a bit blunt, a bit sly, speaking with a straight face while he is plainly having fun. You can imagine the grin behind the words. That personality is one of the reasons the poem lingers, because it feels like a person is talking, not a statue reciting.

Readers admire the final couplet most of all. After twelve lines of teasing, the sudden, heartfelt "And yet, by heaven" arrives like a hand on the shoulder. The shift from mockery to tenderness is one of the great moments in the sonnets, and many readers describe it as the point where they realize the poem is not what they thought. It rewards a second reading, because once you know the ending, the earlier insults begin to sound affectionate, like the teasing of someone who loves you.

Poets and teachers appreciate it too. For writers, it is a lesson in how to use a tradition against itself. It shows that parody and sincerity are not opposites, and that you can take apart a cliche and then use the pieces to build something truer. It is often one of the first poems that make a skeptical student say, I did not know poetry could be like this.

Finally, readers love that it feels modern. Its suspicion of advertising-style exaggeration, and its insistence on honest speech, strike many people as surprisingly current. In an age of filtered pictures and polished images, a poem that praises an unfiltered person feels almost radical. That is why it keeps being passed along: people recognize themselves, and the people they love, in its plain and loving gaze.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Even a charming poem like Sonnet 130 has had its detractors, and some of their points are worth taking seriously.

The first concerns the treatment of the woman. A number of modern readers have felt that the poem, whatever its intention, is quite unflattering to its subject. Lines about her skin being dun, her hair being wires, and her breath reeking are hard to hear as pure affection. Feminist critics in particular have noted that the poem still evaluates a woman by her body, merely using a different scale. The speaker's refusal to flatter is, in this view, still a way of judging. Other critics respond that this misreads the point, since the target is the flattering convention and not the woman, and that the final couplet clearly shows his devotion. Still, the discomfort is real and widely discussed.

The second is about the context of the Dark Lady sequence. The poems addressed to her are among the most troubled in the whole set. They suggest a speaker who is infatuated and ashamed, who finds her unfaithful and yet cannot leave. Read alongside those poems, 130 can look less like a warm, comic celebration and more like a rationalization, a man trying to convince himself that a difficult woman is worth it. Some critics see the humor as a mask for bitterness. That reading complicates the poem's sweetness, and not everyone is happy with it.

A third complaint is that the poem is derivative of the thing it mocks. To make the joke work, Shakespeare has to repeat the whole catalog of Petrarchan comparisons, and some readers argue that this keeps the old conventions alive as much as it kills them. The poem, in this view, depends on the very fashion it dismisses. Others point out that nearly all parody works this way.

There is also the problem of context for modern readers. Without knowing the background of the sonnet craze, much of the joke is lost, and the poem can seem merely rude. Teachers sometimes find that students read it as a straightforward insult and miss the turn.

Lastly, some scholars have questioned whether the poem is as radical as its reputation. Shakespeare was not the only poet to mock the conventions, and the idea of the plain-speaking lover was already present in earlier verse. So while the poem is excellent, it may be less revolutionary than is sometimes claimed. It is better thought of as a particularly brilliant example of a known game than as the invention of a new one.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To see where Sonnet 130 belongs, start with the tradition it comes from. In the fourteenth century, the Italian poet Petrarch wrote hundreds of poems to a woman he called Laura, celebrating her in elaborate images and sighing over her distance. His style spread across Europe, and by the sixteenth century English poets were imitating it heavily. Writers such as Thomas Watson, Philip Sidney, Edmund Spenser, and others filled their sonnet sequences with golden hair, ivory skin, and eyes like stars. Spenser, for example, writes of lips like cherries and of a lady whose looks outshine the sun. This conventional praise is exactly what Shakespeare mocks.

Shakespeare's sonnets take up the form but twist it. In many of his poems the beloved is a young man, and in the last section the beloved is a dark-haired woman who is anything but an untouchable ideal. She is physical, flawed, and sometimes cruel. In that way, Sonnet 130 is part of a larger move away from worship and toward something messier and more human. It is a love poem for people who live on the ground.

The poem also belongs to the tradition of comic and satirical verse. English literature has a long history of poets deflating pretension, from Chaucer's gentle mockery of romance in The Canterbury Tales to the sharper wit of the Restoration and the eighteenth century. Sonnet 130 sits comfortably in that line, which later leads to writers like Alexander Pope, who loved mock-heroic exaggeration, and to modern poets who blend humor and tenderness.

Its descendants are easy to find. Anyone who has written an anti-love poem that turns out to be a love poem is working in its shadow. The parody tradition of comparing a partner to something silly or humble is a direct heir. In the twentieth century, poets who valued plain speech over ornament often felt a kinship with its attitude.

There is also an interesting comparison with Sonnet 116. That poem declares love to be an unchanging star, in the grandest language possible. Sonnet 130 insists that no one is a star, and that this is fine. Together they show Shakespeare arguing with himself about what love really is, and both positions feel true.

Why read it today? Because it is short, funny, and kind, and because it still speaks to a world drowning in exaggerated images. It offers a simple and lasting idea: that to love someone truthfully is to see them as they are, and to find them wonderful anyway. Few poems say that so well, so briefly, and with such a good joke on the way.
