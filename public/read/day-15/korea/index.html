Key Context & Why It Matters

In the twelfth lunar month of 1395, just three years after General Yi Seong-gye (King Taejo) founded the Joseon Dynasty and moved the capital to Hanyang (Seoul), court scholar Kwon Geun and eleven royal astronomers of the Seoungwan completed National Treasure No. 228: the Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido ('Chart of the Constellations and the Regions They Govern'). Carved into a polished slab of jet-black hornblende limestone measuring 122.8 by 211.0 centimeters, it is the second-oldest surviving monumental stone star chart in the world. Based on a treasured ink rubbing of a lost ancient Goguryeo stele from Pyongyang, the Joseon astronomers recalculated the precession of the equinoxes over seven centuries and engraved 1,467 stars across 283 constellations with variable dot diameters representing apparent visual magnitude.

Inside the dimly lit astronomy gallery of the National Palace Museum of Korea, at the southwestern corner of Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, stands a two-ton monolith of polished black hornblende-biotite stone measuring 211 centimeters tall, 122.8 centimeters wide, and twelve centimeters thick. Across the upper half of its dark, mirror-like face spins a 76-centimeter circular wheel of the northern night sky: 1,467 individual stars linked into 283 constellations, bisected by the bifurcated river of the Milky Way (*Eunhasu*, 은하수) and framed by twenty-eight radial spokes corresponding to the lunar mansions (*isip-pal-su*, 이십팔수). This is the *Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido* (천상열차분야지도, National Treasure No. 228), carved in December 1395. According to Kwon’s 1395 text, the ancient northern Korean kingdom of Goguryeo (37 BCE–668 CE) had possessed a sacred stone-carved planisphere in its capital of Pyongyang.
