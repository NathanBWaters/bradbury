Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (1395): The Black-Marble Star Chart That Legitimized a Dynasty

Korea — How Kwon Geun and eleven Joseon astronomers rescued a sunken Goguryeo stone rubbing and carved 1,467 stars with varying magnitudes for Hanyang's latitude (Founding of Joseon Dynasty (December 1395 / Goguryeo Origin)) • 10 min read

“In olden times, Pyongyang had a stone astronomical chart carved on monument stone, which sank into the Taedong River during the wars... Soon after His Majesty ascended the throne, a man presented an ink rubbing of that old stone.” — Kwon Geun, Engraved Inscription on Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (1395)

Key Context & Why It Matters

In the twelfth lunar month of 1395, just three years after General Yi Seong-gye (King Taejo) founded the Joseon Dynasty and moved the capital to Hanyang (Seoul), court scholar Kwon Geun and eleven royal astronomers of the Seoungwan completed National Treasure No. 228: the Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido ('Chart of the Constellations and the Regions They Govern'). Carved into a polished slab of jet-black hornblende limestone measuring 122.8 by 211.0 centimeters, it is the second-oldest surviving monumental stone star chart in the world. Based on a treasured ink rubbing of a lost ancient Goguryeo stele from Pyongyang, the Joseon astronomers recalculated the precession of the equinoxes over seven centuries and engraved 1,467 stars across 283 constellations with variable dot diameters representing apparent visual magnitude.

Inside the dimly lit astronomy gallery of the National Palace Museum of Korea, at the southwestern corner of Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, stands a two-ton monolith of polished black hornblende-biotite stone measuring 211 centimeters tall, 122.8 centimeters wide, and twelve centimeters thick. Across the upper half of its dark, mirror-like face spins a 76-centimeter circular wheel of the northern night sky: 1,467 individual stars linked into 283 constellations, bisected by the bifurcated river of the Milky Way (Eunhasu) and framed by twenty-eight radial spokes corresponding to the lunar mansions (isip-pal-su). This is the Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (National Treasure No. 228), carved in December 1395.

Why was the very first scientific monument commissioned by King Taejo—founder of the Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910), before the walls of Gyeongbokgung Palace were barely dry—an astronomical star chart? In East Asian political philosophy, derived from the Book of Documents (Shujing), a new dynasty that overthrew the previous royal house (Goryeo) had to prove it possessed the Cheonmyeong (Mandate of Heaven). Only the rightful sovereign could accurately read the movements of the sun, moon, five naked-eye planets, and twenty-eight lunar mansions to promulgate the agricultural calendar (yeokbeop) for the people. The story of how King Taejo acquired the celestial map is inscribed directly onto the bottom half of the stone by Prime Minister and Neo-Confucian scholar Kwon Geun (1352–1409). According to Kwon’s 1395 text, the ancient northern Korean kingdom of Goguryeo (37 BCE–668 CE) had possessed a sacred stone-carved planisphere in its capital of Pyongyang. When Goguryeo fell to the allied Tang-Silla invasion in 668 CE, defenders cast the stone star chart into the deep waters of the Taedong River (Daedonggang) rather than let foreign armies capture the kingdom’s cosmic seal.

Over seven centuries, the sunken stone was eroded or lost beneath the river silt, and the few surviving ink rubbings (takbon) taken before 668 CE vanished during war. Yet immediately after Yi Seong-gye ascended the throne in July 1392, an anonymous commoner walked into the royal court and presented a single preserved ink rubbing of the lost Goguryeo star chart. Ecstatic court ministers hailed the rubbing’s appearance as miraculous proof that Joseon was the legitimate heir to ancient Goguryeo’s northern sovereignty. When King Taejo handed the ancient Goguryeo rubbing to the royal astronomers of the Seoungwan (Royal Bureau of Astronomy)—led by Ryu Bang-taek, Seol Gyeo-du, and Kwon Jung-hwa—the scientists discovered a critical astronomical discrepancy. Because Earth’s rotational axis wobbles like a spinning gyroscope in a 25,772-year cycle known as the precession of the equinoxes (secha undong), the positions of the equinoxes and the north celestial pole on the Goguryeo rubbing had drifted by more than ten degrees over the intervening centuries.

Rather than blindly copying the ancient rubbing out of superstition, Ryu Bang-taek and ten fellow astronomers spent three years conducting fresh nocturnal meridian observations in Hanyang using bronze armillary spheres (honcheonui). In the upper middle panels of the stone, they recalculated the exact lunar mansions where dusk (hon) and dawn (hyo) culminated on the twenty-four solar terms (jeolgi) for the 1390s, creating a unique two-layered astronomical archive. When modern astrophysicists led by Professor Park Chang-bom (formerly at Seoul National University and KIAS) digitized the exact polar projection coordinates of all 1,467 stars on the Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido and ran computer regressions backward in time, they confirmed Kwon Geun’s historical account with stunning precision. While the outer constellations and the recalculated solar-term tables reflect fourteenth-century Joseon observations near 38° N, the inner circumpolar stars within forty degrees of the pole best fit observational coordinates from Pyongyang dating between the first century BCE and sixth century CE—preserving the lost sky of Goguryeo inside a Joseon monument!

Even more remarkable to modern astronomers is a cartographic detail invisible on Chinese star charts of the same era (such as the 1247 Suzhou Planisphere, where every star is carved as a uniform circle of identical size). On the 1395 Korean Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido, the stone carvers drilled each of the 1,467 stars with different diameters proportional to its apparent visual brightness (magnitude) in the night sky! First-magnitude stars such as Sirius (Cheonirang), Vega (Jingnyeo), and Arcturus (Gak-su) are carved as prominent hemispherical hollows up to five millimeters across; fourth- and fifth-magnitude stars fade down to tiny one-millimeter pinpricks. Two centuries before Johann Bayer and Galileo Galilei popularized magnitude-scaled star atlases in seventeenth-century Europe, Joseon stonecutters had already encoded stellar photometry into black stone.

Look closely at the geometry of the 76-centimeter star circle itself. At the exact center sits the North Ecliptic and Celestial Pole encircled by an inner ring of the inner boundary (naegyu) representing stars that never set below the northern horizon of Korea (circumpolar stars). Radiating outward are twenty-eight irregular pie-slices corresponding to the Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions (isip-pal-su), divided into four cosmic guardian quadrants: the Azure Dragon of the East (Cheongnyong), the Black Tortoise of the North (Hyeonmu), the White Tiger of the West (Baekho), and the Vermilion Bird of the South (Jujak). Two overlapping off-center rings trace the Jeokdo (Celestial Equator) and the Hwangdo (the Ecliptic path of the sun), intersecting at the vernal and autumnal equinoxes, while a sinuous, double-bordered band sweeps across the stars to depict the Eunhasu (Milky Way) splitting at the constellation Cygnus—exactly as the dark Great Rift of interstellar dust appears to the naked eye on a clear Korean autumn night.

In the 1430s, when King Sejong the Great launched Joseon’s golden age of astronomy with Jang Yeong-sil, Yi Cheon, and Jeong Cho, the 1395 Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido served as their foundational reference. However, three decades of exposing the 1395 black stone to weather and hundreds of ink-rubbing mallets inside Gyeongbokgung Palace had begun to wear down its delicate star dots. In 1433, King Sejong commissioned a newly calibrated stone star chart atop Mount Geumgang and Gyeongbokgung, and in 1687 (the thirteenth year of King Sukjong), when the original 1395 slab had become too fragile for ink rubbings, the Gwansanggam (Office of Astronomy) carved an exact replica on white marble (now Treasure No. 837) and stored King Taejo’s original 1395 black-stone stele inside a protective pavilion.

During the twentieth century, as palaces were dismantled under colonial rule and bombarded during the Korean War, the 1395 original slab survived tucked inside a storage shed at Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung before being properly identified by Korean science historians in the 1960s and designated National Treasure No. 228 in 1985. In 2007, when the Bank of Korea redesigned the 10,000-won banknote—the green bill carried in every South Korean wallet today—designers placed King Sejong on the front and turned the entire reverse side into an astronomical tribute: behind the bronze Honcheonui armillary sphere and the Bohyeonsan Optical Astronomy Observatory telescope shines the circular star map of the 1395 Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido.

Stand before the dark stone slab in the National Palace Museum today, and then pull a 10,000-won note from your pocket under the streetlights of Gwanghwamun. Across more than two thousand years—from a Goguryeo astronomer watching the stars over the Taedong River to Ryu Bang-taek’s calculations in 1395—Korea’s map of the heavens has never gone dark.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Archaeoastronomy: How Precession Dating Proved the Goguryeo Origin

For decades, some skeptics wondered whether Kwon Geun's 1395 inscription claiming a Goguryeo origin for the star chart was merely a political myth invented to bolster Joseon's legitimacy. In the 1990s, astrophysicist Park Chang-bom measured the precise radial polar distances and right ascensions of all 1,467 stars on the 1395 stone and accounted for axial precession (50.3 arcseconds per year). His statistical fit proved that the stars inside the circumpolar ring reflect observations taken near latitude 39° N (Pyongyang) during the Goguryeo era, while outer stars reflect fourteenth-century revisions.

2. Photometric Engraving: Why Variable Star Dot Sizes Matter in Science History

Compare the 1395 Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido with the 1247 Chinese Suzhou Stone Planisphere. Whereas the Suzhou chart renders all stars as uniform circles regardless of brightness, the 1395 Korean chart systematically varies the drilled diameter of each star dot between 1.0 millimeter and 5.2 millimeters in direct correlation with apparent visual magnitude (from V = 0.0 to V = 5.5). This makes National Treasure No. 228 the oldest surviving monumental stone star chart on Earth to encode quantitative stellar magnitude visually.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Remember three astronomical terms: first, Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido, meaning 'Chart of the Constellations and the Regions They Govern Arranged in Order'; second, Isip-pal-su, the Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions dividing the equatorial sky like cosmic longitude; and third, Seoungwan (later Gwansanggam), the Royal Bureau of Astronomy where Ryu Bang-taek and eleven colleagues recalculated the twenty-four solar terms in 1395.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit the B1 Royal Science Gallery of the National Palace Museum of Korea inside Gyeongbokgung Palace, where both the original 1395 black-stone Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (National Treasure No. 228) and the 1687 King Sukjong white-marble replica (Treasure No. 837) are displayed side by side under fiber-optic constellation projections. Afterward, walk north inside Gyeongbokgung to the stone Gwanchondae observatory platform near Gyeonghoeru Pavilion.
