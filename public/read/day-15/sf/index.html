San Francisco Deep-Dive Story • New Deal Public Art & Civic Eccentricity

Coit Tower's 1934 New Deal Frescoes, Lillie Hitchcock Coit, and Knickerbocker Engine Co. No. 5

San Francisco — How a cigar-smoking Victorian heiress who chased fire engines left $118,000 to beautify San Francisco—and how twenty-six Diego Rivera-inspired artists painted radical Depression murals inside her tower (Victorian Fire Companies (1858) to New Deal Public Works of Art Project (1933–1934)) • 8 min read

“We were twenty-six artists painting wet lime plaster shoulder to shoulder while tear gas drifted up the hill from the Embarcadero waterfront strike.” — Bernard Zakheim (1934)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Crowning the 284-foot summit of Telegraph Hill, the 210-foot fluted concrete shaft of Coit Tower merges two radically different chapters of San Francisco rebellion. Funded by a 1929 bequest from Lillie Hitchcock Coit (1843–1929)—the wealthy socialite who at age fifteen dropped her bridal bouquet to haul Knickerbocker Engine Co. No. 5 up Telegraph Hill and wore a gold diamond-studded '5' badge pinned to her dress for the rest of her life—Arthur Brown Jr.'s Art Deco tower became in January 1934 the nation's first Public Works of Art Project (PWAP) monument, where twenty-six artists led by Victor Arnautoff and Bernard Zakheim painted frescoes of Depression breadlines, Karl Marx's Capital, and the 1934 Waterfront Strike.

Before any concrete tower stood on the 284-foot crown of Telegraph Hill—originally called Loma Alta ('High Hill') by Spanish settlers—the summit carried a giant two-armed wooden semaphore windmill erected in September 1849 to signal merchants down in Portsmouth Square whether an incoming vessel through the Golden Gate was a sidewheel mail steamer, a clipper ship, or a brig. On a blustery afternoon in 1858, while attending a wedding in North Beach, fifteen-year-old Lillie Hitchcock—daughter of U.S. Army surgeon Dr. Charles Hitchcock—spotted the volunteer firefighters of Knickerbocker Engine Company No. 5 straining shorthanded on their hemp drag-ropes as they hauled their heavy hand-pumped fire engine up the muddy rutted slope of Telegraph Hill toward a blaze.

Tossing aside her silk parasol and wedding gloves, young Lillie seized the tow rope in both hands, braced her boots against the cobblestones, and shouted to bystanders to pull until Engine No. 5 beat every rival company to the summit—earning her election on October 3, 1863, as an honorary lifetime member of Knickerbocker No. 5. For the next sixty-six years, Lillie Hitchcock Coit defied every Victorian gender boundary: she smoked Manila cheroots, played high-stakes poker with lumber barons in men's boots disguised under a mustache, sewed the red-and-black silk numeral '5' into all her linen undergarments and Paris gowns, and survived a 1903 shooting inside her Palace Hotel suite when her cousin tried to murder a nobleman.

When Lillie Coit died on July 22, 1929, at the age of eighty-five, her will left one-third of her residual estate—amounting to $118,730—to the City and County of San Francisco 'to be expended in an appropriate manner for the purpose of adding to the beauty of the city which I have always loved,' alongside a separate fund for a bronze monument to her beloved volunteer firemen in Washington Square. The San Francisco Art Commission selected Beaux-Arts master Arthur Brown Jr.—who had already designed San Francisco City Hall and the War Memorial Opera House—to crown Pioneer Park atop Telegraph Hill with a 210-foot slender fluted shaft constructed of unpainted reinforced concrete poured in wooden slip-forms, contrary to urban legend not modeled on a fire hose nozzle, though its proportions delighted fire buffs.

Just two months after Coit Tower opened on October 8, 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal established the Public Works of Art Project (PWAP) under regional director Walter Heil (director of the de Young Museum), selecting the bare plaster walls of Coit Tower's ground-floor rotunda and second-floor corridors as the nationwide pilot project for federal mural patronage. Between January and June 1934, twenty-six Bay Area artists and nineteen assistants—paid between 25 and42.50 a week—climbed wooden scaffolding inside the unheated tower to paint nearly 3,600 square feet of murals in authentic Renaissance buon fresco (grinding mineral pigments directly into wet lime-and-sand plaster before it dried), egg tempera, and oil on canvas.

Because half of the Coit Tower muralists—including technical supervisor Victor Arnautoff, Bernard Zakheim, Clifford Wight, and John Langley Howard—had personally worked alongside Mexican master Diego Rivera during his 1930–1931 visits to San Francisco, they rejected decorative pastoral myths in favor of a sweeping social-realist panorama of California in the throes of the Great Depression. In his monumental fresco City Life on the west wall, Russian-born painter Victor Arnautoff placed himself and his son at the bustling intersection of Montgomery and Washington streets surrounded by bank guards, a pickpocket robbing a tuxedoed financier, and a wooden newsstand displaying the Daily Worker and the New Masses directly beside the mainstream San Francisco Chronicle.

Next door in the Library panel, Polish-born furniture maker and muralist Bernard Zakheim painted fellow artist John Langley Howard reaching for a volume of Karl Marx's Das Kapital amid shelves stacked with Upton Sinclair, Jack London, and Hebrew yeshiva texts while headlines on wooden newspaper racks chronicled New Deal strikes and artistic resistance. In California Industrial Scenes, John Langley Howard juxtaposed sleek limousines and fur-draped gold-mine shareholders peering into a panning sluice with a multi-racial crowd of hungry transient families, striking workers, and unemployed fathers reading the Western Worker along a gravelly riverbank. As the artists laid their final wet plaster strokes in May and June 1934, history erupted directly below their windows: 12,000 longshoremen led by Harry Bridges walked off the Embarcadero piers below Telegraph Hill in the 1934 West Coast Waterfront Strike, and on 'Bloody Thursday' (July 5, 1934), San Francisco police fired shotguns into the picket lines at Steuart and Mission streets, killing Howard Sperry and Nick Bordoise.

Terrified that Coit Tower's murals would radicalize the public during the ensuing four-day San Francisco General Strike, the Art Commission padlock-bolted the heavy bronze doors of Coit Tower in July 1934, ordered Clifford Wight's fresco portrait of a Communist hammer-and-sickle badge jackhammered off the plaster wall above an exit doorway, and threatened to whitewash Zakheim's and Howard's panels. Refusing to back down, the Artists' and Writers' Union mounted round-the-clock picket lines around the base of Coit Tower to prevent vigilantes from defacing the frescoes inside—just as Rockefeller Center had jackhammered Diego Rivera's mural in New York months earlier—until public pressure forced the Art Commission to open the tower doors on October 20, 1934, with twenty-five of the twenty-six murals intact. Following a meticulous $1.7 million conservation treatment completed in 2014 that removed eighty years of automobile exhaust, fog moisture, and calcium efflorescence from the lime plaster, Coit Tower stands today as a twin monument to Lillie Hitchcock Coit's fearless Victorian individuality and the unvarnished artistic solidarity of 1934 San Francisco.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Renaissance Buon Fresco Meets the 1934 Waterfront Strike

Why could the San Francisco Art Commission not simply paint over or peel off the controversial details in Coit Tower's main rotunda in July 1934? Trained by Diego Rivera during his 1930–1931 commissions at the Pacific Stock Exchange and the San Francisco Art Institute, Victor Arnautoff and his colleagues executed the ground-floor walls in true buon fresco: pure earth pigments ground in water were painted onto fresh lime plaster, chemically bonding into calcium carbonate stone as the wall cured so that only a chisel or jackhammer could remove the imagery.

2. The Myth of the Fire-Hose Nozzle vs. Arthur Brown Jr.'s Classical Fluting

Generations of tour guides have repeated the colorful story that Lillie Hitchcock Coit demanded Coit Tower be shaped like the brass nozzle of a Knickerbocker No. 5 fire hose. In reality, Lillie Coit died four years before the tower was designed, leaving no architectural instructions, and Beaux-Arts architect Arthur Brown Jr. explicitly denied the nozzle story, explaining that the 210-foot shaft with its recessed vertical flutes and belvedere arcade was an Art Deco abstraction of a classical Greek Doric column.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three essential details in view: first, Lillie Hitchcock Coit's gold badge '5,' marking her 1863 induction into volunteer Knickerbocker Engine Company No. 5; second, Victor Arnautoff's fresco City Life and Bernard Zakheim's Library, where radical 1934 newspaper headlines and Marx's Capital were painted directly into the wet plaster; and third, the sole censored detail—Clifford Wight's Soviet emblem above the west doorway, chiseled out before the October 1934 reopening.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Start at Washington Square Park in North Beach (Columbus & Union streets) to view Haig Patigian's 1933 bronze statue of three volunteer firefighters carrying a rescued woman—the other monument funded by Lillie Coit's bequest—and then climb the Greenwich Steps to Pioneer Park at 1 Telegraph Hill Boulevard to walk clockwise around Coit Tower's ground-floor fresco rotunda before taking the elevator to the 360-degree observation belvedere.
