Remember

Christina Rossetti (1862) • 4 min read

A speaker contemplates her own death and reverses her initial plea for remembrance, choosing the beloved's happiness over her own memorial.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Written in 1849 when Christina Rossetti was just eighteen and published in her landmark 1862 Pre-Raphaelite collection Goblin Market and Other Poems, 'Remember' is a Petrarchan sonnet of extraordinary emotional poise and psychological generosity. Addressed from the threshold of the 'silent land' of death during an era when tuberculosis and strict religious conscience shadowed Rossetti’s life and courtships, the octave urges the beloved to remember her after she can no longer hold his hand, before the ninth-line volta executes a breathtaking reversal: preferring that the beloved forget her and smile rather than remember and be sad. Placed on Night 11 of the Bradbury 1000 curriculum, Christina Rossetti’s 'Remember' (1862) exemplifies how compressed poetic form preserves human perception across centuries.

Remember me when I am gone away,

Gone far away into the silent land;

When you can no more hold me by the hand,

Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.

Remember me when no more day by day

You tell me of our future that you plann’d:

Only remember me; you understand

It will be late to counsel then or pray.

Yet if you should forget me for a while

And afterwards remember, do not grieve:

For if the darkness and corruption leave

A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,

Better by far you should forget and smile

Than that you should remember and be sad.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

I. Historical & Biographical Context

Written in 1849 when Christina Rossetti was just eighteen years old and published thirteen years later in her acclaimed 1862 collection Goblin Market and Other Poems, 'Remember' emerged amidst chronic ill health and her broken engagement to the Pre-Raphaelite painter James Collinson over religious differences. Victorian mourning culture demanded elaborate, lifelong rituals of grief—jet jewelry, black crape, and tearful memorial albums. Against that possessive orthodoxy of mourning, young Rossetti crafted a sonnet of astonishing psychological generosity. By choosing quiet emotional realism over the theatrical conventions of Victorian bereavement, she turned a teenaged meditation into an enduring monument of lyric empathy and restraint. II. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

The poem dramatizes a profound evolution in the meaning of love across fourteen lines. In the octave (lines 1–8), the speaker clings to the natural human fear of erasure, pleading three times—'Remember me... Remember me... Only remember me'—as she imagines crossing into 'the silent land' where tactile intimacy ('hold me by the hand') and shared future plans cease. Yet at the volta in line 9 ('Yet if you should forget me for a while'), genuine love triumphs over ego. Realizing that obligatory grief would burden the survivor, she releases her beloved: 'Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should remember and be sad.' III. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Rossetti uses the strict Petrarchan sonnet form (ABBAABBA CDDECE) as a dramatic hinge for emotional transformation. Notice the exquisite physical realism of line four—'Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay'—where the syntax and repetition of 'turn / turning' enact the hesitant body language of lovers parting at a doorway. In the sestet, Rossetti swaps the euphemistic 'silent land' of line two for unflinching material reality ('the darkness and corruption' of the grave), making the closing antithesis ('forget and smile' vs. 'remember and be sad') all the more earned and unsentimental. Every word is predominantly monosyllabic and Anglo-Saxon in origin, giving the closing couplet the quiet, unarguable weight of a whispered bedside blessing.

IV. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Praised by Virginia Woolf as one of the supreme lyricists in English—Woolf declared that Rossetti's best poems stay in the mind like flawless musical notes—'Remember' has become a staple of anthologies and memorials worldwide. Critics frequently pair it with Shakespeare's Sonnet 71 ('No longer mourn for me when I am dead'), noting how Rossetti softens Shakespeare's bitter irony into quiet, selfless tenderness. Its enduring power lies in its refusal to weaponize memory or demand performative sorrow from those who remain behind in the land of the living. Across two centuries, readers facing bereavement have turned to Rossetti's sestet as a model of how love releases rather than binds. V. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

Swapped into any night of your Bradbury triad, Rossetti's 'Remember' offers an intimate counterpoint to stories of pride, possession, and mortality. Whether juxtaposed against the grandiose granite boast of 'Ozymandias' or the self-lacerating grief of Poe's narrator in 'The Raven,' Rossetti reminds us that the highest form of devotion is never a monument that demands despair, but a blessing that sets the living free to smile. When we read deeply across centuries, we discover that the truest immortality an artist or loved one can desire is to leave behind a spark of joy and clarity in another human mind. Ask yourself tonight how your words and actions can lighten, rather than burden, those you love.
