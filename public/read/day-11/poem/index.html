Nightly Poem • Romantic Ode & Lyric

Remember

Christina Rossetti (1862) • 4 min read

A speaker contemplates her own death and reverses her initial plea for remembrance, choosing the beloved's happiness over her own memorial.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Christina Rossetti wrote this sonnet in 1849, when she was just eighteen, and it appeared in her 1862 book Goblin Market and Other Poems, the volume that made her famous. She grew up in a gifted London family of artists and writers, and her brother Dante Gabriel helped found the Pre-Raphaelites, a circle that prized sincerity and close attention to real feeling. She was also deeply devout, and she lived in a century when early death hung over young people as an ordinary fact. The poem is spoken by someone imagining her own death and asking to be remembered. Then it does something surprising and tender with that request. A sonnet is a fourteen-line poem, and this one changes direction at its ninth line, so listen for the turn. In plain terms, it is about a love that cares more for the survivor's peace than for its own memory. It is short, quiet, and has comforted people at funerals for a century and a half.

Remember me when I am gone away,

Gone far away into the silent land;

When you can no more hold me by the hand,

Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.

Remember me when no more day by day

You tell me of our future that you plann’d:

Only remember me; you understand

It will be late to counsel then or pray.

Yet if you should forget me for a while

And afterwards remember, do not grieve:

For if the darkness and corruption leave

A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,

Better by far you should forget and smile

Than that you should remember and be sad.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

This is a poem spoken by someone who is imagining her own death, addressed to the person she loves. The first eight lines make a simple request: remember me when I have gone away into what she calls "the silent land," an old and gentle name for death. But the request is made through small physical details rather than grand statements. Look at the fourth line: "Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay." Anyone who has said goodbye to someone they love will recognize this. You start to leave, you half turn, and then you linger for one more moment. The poem is saying that death will end even that. There will be no more hands to hold and no more reluctant half-turns at the door.

The next four lines widen the loss from touch to time. "No more day by day / You tell me of our future that you plann'd." The couple used to talk about what they would do together, and that habit of planning is what a shared life feels like from the inside. Death removes the future tense. Then comes a line readers still argue about: "It will be late to counsel then or pray." To counsel means to advise or to talk things over. To pray may mean to pray for her, or to plead for her. In Rossetti's devout Christian world, the line suggests that once she is dead, the living can no longer change anything for her. All that is left is memory. So the first half is a plea made with some urgency: do it now, while it still matters, and above all remember.

Then, at line nine, the poem turns, and this is the heart of it. "Yet if you should forget me for a while / And afterwards remember, do not grieve." The speaker has suddenly stopped pleading. She now accepts that her beloved will forget her, probably because he is alive and life is demanding, and she forgives him in advance. She even imagines her own thoughts surviving in the grave, if "the darkness and corruption leave / A vestige of the thoughts that once I had." That phrase is blunt about decay, which keeps the poem from becoming prettily sentimental. The last two lines deliver the reversal: "Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should remember and be sad."

The layer a first-time reader might miss is how the whole poem is built on two words, remember and forget, and how their meanings trade places. The poem opens by treating remembering as love. It ends by treating forgetting as love, because it lets the beloved be happy. What it is really about is the difference between wanting to be loved and wanting someone you love to be well. The speaker moves from the first to the second inside fourteen lines.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The craft here is quiet but very sure. Start with the form. This is a Petrarchan sonnet, named for the Italian poet Petrarch. It has an eight-line first part, the octave, and a six-line second part, the sestet, and the thought pivots between them. The octave rhymes abba abba, which means only two rhyme sounds across eight lines. That is hard to do in English, which has far fewer rhymes than Italian, yet nothing here feels strained. "Away," "day," "stay," and "pray" sit together as if they had always belonged. The pivot at line nine is exactly where the form wants it, and the meaning turns with it. That marriage of structure and feeling is the poem's biggest success.

The language is almost all short, plain words. There is hardly a decoration in it. This gives the poem a spoken sincerity, as though the speaker is saying it across a table, and it also makes the few vivid phrases land hard. "Half turn to go yet turning stay" is the best line in the poem, and the reason is that it describes a gesture rather than an idea. The ending works the same way. The balance of "forget and smile" against "remember and be sad" is as neat as a scale tipping. It is memorable, quotable, and it feels earned because the whole poem has been walking toward it.

Now the honest complaints. Some lines are doing duty for the rhyme rather than the meaning. "Only remember me; you understand" feels a little like a placeholder that gets the poem to its next rhyme word, and "Gone far away into the silent land" leans on a stock phrase. The line "It will be late to counsel then or pray" is so compressed that it becomes ambiguous in a way that seems more accidental than intended. The sentence that runs across lines ten to thirteen, with its "if" and its "vestige of the thoughts that once I had," asks the reader to hold a tangled bit of grammar and a rather abstract idea at the same time. It is the one place where the poem's clarity wobbles.

There is also a question about emotional temperature. The speaker is so poised, so generous, so ready to release her beloved, that a modern reader may find her almost too saintly. We never learn who the beloved is, what they shared, or what she is afraid of. The poem gives us a graceful gesture rather than a person under pressure. That is partly the point, and partly the limit of the thing: it is a miniature, not a drama. It is also the work of an eighteen-year-old imagining death rather than facing it, and the elegance may owe something to that distance. It is beautifully made, but it is a modest poem, and its power comes from its restraint rather than its range.

III. The Impact It Had

For Rossetti herself, this poem was an early marker of what she did best. Written in 1849, it came from the same youthful burst that produced some of her finest short lyrics. She was publishing in the Pre-Raphaelite magazine The Germ in 1850, under a pen name, and by 1862, when Goblin Market and Other Poems appeared, she was recognized as a serious voice. The title poem, a strange fable about two sisters and goblin fruit, got most of the attention. But quieter pieces like "Remember" established her reputation as a poet of renunciation, longing, and death, and they shaped how Victorian readers imagined her. After Elizabeth Barrett Browning died in 1861, Rossetti became widely regarded as the leading woman poet in England, and later critics have often called her one of the great poets of her century.

Beyond her own career, "Remember" became one of the most anthologized and most read sonnets in English. It is a standard choice at funerals and memorial services, and it turns up again and again in books of consolation poetry. Part of that is the last line. Few people can say to the bereaved that it is fine to move on, but this poem says it, in the voice of the one who has died. Its cadence is easy to memorize, and people have carried it with them for that reason.

In the classroom, it has long been used as a model of the Petrarchan sonnet, because the volta, the turn, is so clean. Students can see exactly where the argument reverses and why. It also became a central text when scholars began, especially from the 1970s onward, to restore women writers to the Victorian canon. Rossetti was among the first to be rediscovered, and her work helped shape the new field of feminist literary criticism. Biographers such as Jan Marsh, whose life of Rossetti appeared in 1994, built a fuller picture of a woman whose poems of self-denial now read as more complicated than they first seemed.

Among other poets, the influence is more a matter of tone than imitation. The idea of a speaker who loves enough to let go, in plain words and tight form, runs through much later poetry about grief. Virginia Woolf, in her 1932 essay on Rossetti, wrote with real admiration for her compression and music. Many later sonnet writers, women especially, have worked in the space Rossetti opened, a space where private feeling gets formal dignity without raising its voice.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved this poem mainly because it tells the truth about something very hard, and tells it kindly. Most of us, if we imagine our own death, fear being forgotten. Most of us, if we imagine the death of someone close, fear that our grief will never end. The poem addresses both fears at once. It admits that the dead want to be remembered, and then it gives the living permission to forget. That permission is a form of tenderness, and people who have lost someone often say it is the thing they most needed to hear.

They also love how little the poem asks of them. There is no theology to decode, no obscure reference, no clever puzzle. A reader in the 1860s and a reader today can both feel the same pull in "half turn to go yet turning stay." Because the sentiment is so plainly human, the poem moves easily from the page into ordinary life: it is read aloud at memorial services, copied into condolence cards, and learned by heart.

There is a craft pleasure too, which readers often describe as a kind of surprise that feels inevitable. You expect the poem to keep pleading, and it turns on you, and then you see the turn was always coming. That is a hard effect to pull off. People who read sonnets for their formal skill admire how the poem keeps its voice steady and its feelings private while the argument flips completely.

Contemporaries in the Victorian press tended to praise Rossetti's purity, simplicity, and musical gift, and although there is little documented commentary on this particular sonnet in the 1860s, her small lyrics of this kind were central to her admirers' sense of her. Later writers and critics have returned to it as an example of how much a short poem can hold. In the twentieth century, when Woolf imagined herself speaking to Rossetti in an essay, she praised exactly this kind of compact, unforced music. Readers continue to feel that the poem is the work of someone who knew, even at eighteen, how to say a difficult thing without wasting a word. Another reason for the poem's hold is its length. Fourteen lines is short enough to carry in the head, and the poem rewards that: each time you recall it, a different line surfaces, sometimes the half turn at the door, sometimes the forgetting and the smile. Readers say it grows with them, meaning one thing at twenty and quite another after a loss.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Because this sonnet is short and was never a lightning rod, it has attracted less direct controversy than many famous poems, and it would be dishonest to pretend that there is a long record of reviewers attacking it. The criticism that exists is mostly about Rossetti's work as a whole, and about the kind of speaker she creates in poems like this one.

Some Victorian critics found her range narrow. She wrote repeatedly about death, loss, and renunciation, and a few reviewers thought this made her work morbid or monotonous, as if she kept returning to the same dim room. Her devotion to religion also made some readers feel that her poems were more about resignation than about living. Against that background, "Remember" can look like one more poem about giving things up.

Modern criticism, especially feminist criticism, has taken this further. Scholars such as Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar, in their well-known study of nineteenth-century women writers, discussed how often Rossetti's speakers deny themselves, shrink their desires, and accept silence. In that light, a woman who forgives her beloved in advance for forgetting her can seem almost too selfless, a picture of a woman taught that her proper place is to put others first. Some readers feel the poem's gentleness covers a kind of erasure, and they ask why it is always the speaker who must do the releasing.

A different objection is the charge of sentimentality. The tidy last line, with its beautiful balance, can seem a little too soothing. Real grief, some say, does not resolve in a neat antithesis, and the poem lets the beloved off too easily. There is also a sceptical biographical worry. Readers often want to attach the poem to Rossetti's later broken engagements, her health, or her religious scruples, but she wrote it at eighteen, before most of those events, so such readings are guesswork. And finally, some readers find the poem's theology slightly odd, since it imagines the dead retaining "thoughts" in the grave, which sits uneasily with the way a devout Christian poet might be expected to describe the afterlife. None of this has reduced the poem's standing, but it shows that its calm surface has a debate underneath it. It is also worth saying that the poem has never been treated as a masterpiece of difficulty, and perhaps that is part of why it is overlooked in scholarly debate. Its fans rarely need critics to defend it, while its detractors rarely bother to write about it, and the result is a poem whose reputation rests almost entirely on the loyalty of ordinary readers.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The poem belongs to the long tradition of the sonnet, a form that began in medieval Italy, was perfected by Petrarch, and traveled to England in the sixteenth century. Rossetti had a special link to that world. Her father, Gabriele, was an Italian exile and a scholar of Dante, and she grew up in a household soaked in Italian poetry. It is fitting that she chose the strict Italian form, with its two-part structure, rather than the looser English form Shakespeare used. Poets from Wordsworth to Elizabeth Barrett Browning had revived the sonnet in the nineteenth century, and Rossetti belongs among them, though her voice is more reserved than most.

If you want a comparison, the nearest thing in English is Shakespeare's Sonnet 71, which begins "No longer mourn for me when I am dead." There, too, the speaker says that he would rather be forgotten than have the beloved suffer by remembering. Rossetti's poem feels like an answer to it, though she begins by asking to be remembered, and that makes her turn even more moving. Rossetti also wrote a related poem a year earlier, "When I am dead, my dearest," in which the speaker says, "if thou wilt, remember, / And if thou wilt, forget." It is as if she tried the idea once lightly, then built it into a tight sonnet.

Other poets have written about the dead speaking to the living. John Donne's "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" asks lovers not to make a fuss over parting. Thomas Hardy wrote poems from beyond the grave, and Emily Dickinson wrote often in the voice of the dead. W. H. Auden's "Funeral Blues," with its demand that the world stop, makes an interesting opposite: it insists on grief at full volume, where Rossetti asks for none. Reading the two together is a good way to feel how many different things love can ask of us.

As for why it still matters, the answer is simple. Everyone eventually has to say goodbye to someone, and most of us do not know what to say. This poem offers a way. It treats memory as something to be given, not demanded, and it holds out a picture of love that is fierce enough to want the other person happy even if it costs us being forgotten. It takes about a minute to read, and it can last a lifetime.
