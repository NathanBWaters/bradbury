Bernard Maybeck's 1915 Palace of Fine Arts and the Panama-Pacific International Exposition

San Francisco — How a Berkeley bohemian architect built a temporary plaster-and-burlap Roman ruin on Harbor View tidal mudflats to mourn beauty's fragility—and why San Francisco rebuilt it in poured concrete (Post-Earthquake Rebirth & WWI Era (1912–1967)) • 20 min read

“An old Roman ruin, away from civilization, which two thousand years after its construction was discovered by a modern traveler—that is the mood of sadness modified by beauty that prepares the mind for art.” — Bernard Maybeck (1915)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Nine years after the April 1906 earthquake and fire leveled four-fifths of San Francisco, the city staged the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition across 635 acres of dredged tidal marsh at Harbor View (today's Marina District) to celebrate both the opening of the Panama Canal and San Francisco's phoenix-like resurrection. Assigned to design the temporary Palace of Fine Arts out of fibrous plaster, hemp burlap, and redwood framing, fifty-three-year-old Arts and Crafts architect Bernard Maybeck rejected triumphalism in favor of romantic melancholy—erecting a 162-foot octagonal Roman rotunda, a 1,100-foot Corinthian colonnade topped by Ulric Ellerhusen's 'Weeping Women' atop planter urns, and a still reflective lagoon. So beloved was Maybeck's deliberately crumbling ruin that it was the sole Exposition palace spared demolition in 1916 and was painstakingly recast in reinforced lightweight concrete between 1964 and 1967.

Standing today at 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon in the heart of the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh) (37.8029° N, 122.4484° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Post-Earthquake Rebirth & WWI Era (1912–1967). Nine years earlier, in April 1906, three days of firestorms had turned four square miles of San Francisco into smoking brick rubble; yet by February 20, 1915, Opening Day of the Panama-Pacific International Exposition (PPIE), 250,000 citizens marched from Van Ness Avenue onto 635 acres of newly dredged sand and bay mud between Fort Mason and the Presidio. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how architect Bernard Ralph Maybeck and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson and a generation of San Franciscans confronted creating an ephemeral plaster temple of melancholic grandeur on dredged bay mudflats and later rescuing it from structural decay, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh). Before the Exposition's hydraulic suction dredges pumped millions of cubic yards of Golden Gate sand behind a timber seawall in 1912, the site at Lyon and Baker streets was a salt-marsh lagoon known as Harbor View, where Italian fishermen beached feluccas and oyster beds thrived in shallow tidal sloughs. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during February 20, 1915 to December 4, 1915 (and the 1964–1967 concrete reconstruction). Officially hired merely as an unpaid drafting assistant in the office of architect Willis Polk, fifty-year-old Bernard Maybeck—wearing his trademark custom-woven wool sash and bohemian beret—produced a charcoal-and-pastel rendering of a classical ruin reflected in dark water that stunned the Exposition's Architectural Commission into handing him the entire Palace of Fine Arts. Contemporary observers who walked past 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood architect Bernard Ralph Maybeck and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Maybeck reasoned through what he called 'emotional functionalism': if a museum goer walked directly from noisy roller coasters and agricultural tractor displays into a gallery of Impressionist canvases, the mind would feel jarring exhaustion; instead, a colonnade evoking Arnold Böcklin's painting 'Isle of the Dead' and Giovanni Battista Piranesi's Roman engravings would quiet the pulse. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade. Because the eleven monumental palaces of the 'Jewel City' were designed to last only ten months, builders framed the 162-foot-high octagonal dome and the 1,100-foot curved pergola in Douglas fir and clothed them in 'staff'—a composite of Paris plaster, Manila hemp fiber, and burlap tinted by color director Jules Guérin in warm travertine ochre, Venetian rose, and oxidized verdigris. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through February 20, 1915 to December 4, 1915 (and the 1964–1967 concrete reconstruction), the surrounding streets of the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh) became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. Atop the square attic shrines of the Corinthian colonnade, sculptor Ulric Ellerhusen carved colossal female figures leaning face-down into hollow stone-like boxes—the famous 'Weeping Women,' who turned their backs to the crowd to gaze inward into empty planter urns where Maybeck originally intended trailing Monterey vines to spill like living tears. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Behind the romantic classical rotunda stood a fireproof, steel-framed curved exhibition gallery housing over eleven thousand paintings and sculptures—including works by John Singer Sargent, Childe Hassam, and Edvard Munch—while general electric searchlights of the 'Scintillator' projected thirty-six colored steam plumes into the night fog above the Golden Gate. Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade toward private speculation—forcing architect Bernard Ralph Maybeck and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon. When the Panama-Pacific Exposition closed on December 4, 1915, after welcoming 18,876,438 visitors, demolition crews dynamited the 435-foot Tower of Jewels and leveled the Court of the Universe to clear real estate for the new Marina District residential subdivision, leaving only Maybeck's Palace of Fine Arts standing after the Palace Preservation League collected tens of thousands of petition signatures. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade, the day-to-day craft of fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh). Ironically, Maybeck had explicitly designed the plaster rotunda to weather, crack, and surrender to nature as vines climbed its peeling columns; yet throughout the 1920s and 1930s, San Franciscans used the vast curved exhibition hall behind the lagoon for eighteen indoor tennis courts, Works Progress Administration (WPA) mural studios, and civic floral shows. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon. During World War II, the United States Army requisitioned the Palace of Fine Arts as a quartermaster motor pool and storage depot for military jeeps and trucks bound for the Pacific Theater, while outside on the lagoon, eucalyptus trees grew sixty feet tall and winter storms stripped chunks of plaster off Ellerhusen's weeping figures. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in February 20, 1915 to December 4, 1915 (and the 1964–1967 concrete reconstruction), the entire city turned its eyes toward the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade in the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh). By 1957, when Bernard Maybeck died at age ninety-five, falling plaster ornaments, dry-rotted timber trusses, and corroded iron tie-rods had forced the City of San Francisco to fence off the colonnade as a public safety hazard, sparking bitter debates over whether to bulldoze the decaying fantasy or spend millions casting it in permanent stone. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed February 20, 1915 to December 4, 1915 (and the 1964–1967 concrete reconstruction), shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade. In 1959, San Francisco wooden-box magnate and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson donated $2 million—matching a $2 million state appropriation and a $1.8 million municipal bond approved by voters—creating the funds necessary to demolish the rotting 1915 wood-and-burlap shell while taking precision silicone and plaster molds of every original relief panel. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting. Between 1964 and 1967, structural engineers Hans G. Treffinger and architects Welton Becket and Associates took down the rotunda piece by piece, drove deep foundation piles through the liquefiable Marina bay fill, and recast the 162-foot dome, Corinthian capitals, Bruno Louis Zimm's frieze panels ('The Struggle for the Beautiful'), and the Weeping Women in lightweight reinforced structural concrete. Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of architect Bernard Ralph Maybeck and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson, discovering anew how deeply its design was tuned to the unique rhythms of the San Francisco peninsula.

By the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, a new generation of preservationists, scientists, and community organizers recognized that the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade was more than a relic of Post-Earthquake Rebirth & WWI Era (1912–1967); it was a living ecological and cultural keystone. Two years after the concrete reconstruction reopened in 1967, physicist Frank Oppenheimer—brother of J. Robert Oppenheimer—walked into the cavernous 100,000-square-foot curved steel exhibition hall behind the rotunda and founded the Exploratorium in 1969, filling Maybeck's temple of romantic contemplation with hands-on exhibits of light, wave optics, and human perception for forty-four years. Through painstaking archival recovery, structural stabilization, and public stewardship, the site at 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon was safeguarded so that future generations could experience its lessons firsthand.

Walking the perimeter of the Palace of Fine Arts Rotunda and Corinthian Colonnade today at 37.8029° N, 122.4484° W, one can observe the exact interplay of architecture, geology, and memory that makes the Marina District (former Harbor View tidal marsh) inexhaustible to the attentive observer. In the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake, when the surrounding unconsolidated hydraulic sand fill of the Marina District suffered severe soil liquefaction and gas-main fires just blocks away on Divisadero and Beach streets, the deep-pile reinforced concrete foundations of the 1967 Palace of Fine Arts held firm above the rippling lagoon. From the weathered textures of fibrous plaster-and-burlap 'staff' molding and reinforced seismic concrete casting to the sightlines stretching toward the bay or the surrounding hills, the physical landscape reads like an open manuscript in which every chapter of Post-Earthquake Rebirth & WWI Era (1912–1967) remains legible.

Ultimately, the story of Bernard Maybeck's 1915 Palace of Fine Arts and the Panama-Pacific International Exposition endures because it captures the quintessential San Francisco paradox: a city perpetually perched on the edge of natural and social upheaval that responds by building works of extraordinary beauty, audacity, and solidarity. A subsequent $21 million seismic and lagoon restoration completed in 2010 cleaned the travertine-tinted concrete reliefs, rebuilt the promenade edge where swans and black-crowned night herons nest, and ensured that Maybeck's vision of 'sadness modified by beauty' remains one of the most photographed architectural silhouettes in the American West. Long after the last footsteps fade along 3301 Lyon Street along the edge of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon at dusk, the legacy of architect Bernard Ralph Maybeck and philanthropist Walter S. 1. 'Emotional Functionalism': Why Maybeck Built a Ruin for a World's Fair

While Beaux-Arts architects of the 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition designed triumphal arches to celebrate industrial conquest and the opening of the Panama Canal, Bernard Maybeck asked how architecture could alter human consciousness. Drawing on his training at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and his love of German Romantic painting, he designed the Palace of Fine Arts as an artificial ruin reflected in a quiet lagoon. By evoking the passage of two thousand years and the mortality of human ambition, the colonnade slowed visitors' footsteps and softened their mood before they entered the art galleries.
