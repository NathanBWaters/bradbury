Bernard Maybeck's 1915 Palace of Fine Arts and the Panama-Pacific International Exposition

San Francisco — How a Berkeley bohemian architect built a temporary plaster-and-burlap Roman ruin on Harbor View tidal mudflats to mourn beauty's fragility—and why San Francisco rebuilt it in poured concrete (Post-Earthquake Rebirth & WWI Era (1912–1967)) • 8 min read

“An old Roman ruin, away from civilization, which two thousand years after its construction was discovered by a modern traveler—that is the mood of sadness modified by beauty that prepares the mind for art.” — Bernard Maybeck (1915)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Nine years after the April 1906 earthquake and fire leveled four-fifths of San Francisco, the city staged the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition across 635 acres of dredged tidal marsh at Harbor View (today's Marina District) to celebrate both the opening of the Panama Canal and San Francisco's phoenix-like resurrection. Assigned to design the temporary Palace of Fine Arts out of fibrous plaster, hemp burlap, and redwood framing, fifty-three-year-old Arts and Crafts architect Bernard Maybeck rejected triumphalism in favor of romantic melancholy—erecting a 162-foot octagonal Roman rotunda, a 1,100-foot Corinthian colonnade topped by Ulric Ellerhusen's 'Weeping Women' atop planter urns, and a still reflective lagoon. So beloved was Maybeck's deliberately crumbling ruin that it was the sole Exposition palace spared demolition in 1916 and was painstakingly recast in reinforced lightweight concrete between 1964 and 1967.

Nine years earlier, in April 1906, three days of firestorms had turned four square miles of San Francisco into smoking brick rubble; yet by February 20, 1915, Opening Day of the Panama-Pacific International Exposition (PPIE), 250,000 citizens marched from Van Ness Avenue onto 635 acres of newly dredged sand and bay mud between Fort Mason and the Presidio. Before the Exposition's hydraulic suction dredges pumped millions of cubic yards of Golden Gate sand behind a timber seawall in 1912, the site at Lyon and Baker streets was a salt-marsh lagoon known as Harbor View, where Italian fishermen beached feluccas and oyster beds thrived in shallow tidal sloughs.

Officially hired merely as an unpaid drafting assistant in the office of architect Willis Polk, fifty-year-old Bernard Maybeck—wearing his trademark custom-woven wool sash and bohemian beret—produced a charcoal-and-pastel rendering of a classical ruin reflected in dark water that stunned the Exposition's Architectural Commission into handing him the entire Palace of Fine Arts. Maybeck reasoned through what he called 'emotional functionalism': if a museum goer walked directly from noisy roller coasters and agricultural tractor displays into a gallery of Impressionist canvases, the mind would feel jarring exhaustion; instead, a colonnade evoking Arnold Böcklin's painting 'Isle of the Dead' and Giovanni Battista Piranesi's Roman engravings would quiet the pulse.

Because the eleven monumental palaces of the 'Jewel City' were designed to last only ten months, builders framed the 162-foot-high octagonal dome and the 1,100-foot curved pergola in Douglas fir and clothed them in 'staff'—a composite of Paris plaster, Manila hemp fiber, and burlap tinted by color director Jules Guérin in warm travertine ochre, Venetian rose, and oxidized verdigris. Atop the square attic shrines of the Corinthian colonnade, sculptor Ulric Ellerhusen carved colossal female figures leaning face-down into hollow stone-like boxes—the famous 'Weeping Women,' who turned their backs to the crowd to gaze inward into empty planter urns where Maybeck originally intended trailing Monterey vines to spill like living tears.

Behind the romantic classical rotunda stood a fireproof, steel-framed curved exhibition gallery housing over eleven thousand paintings and sculptures—including works by John Singer Sargent, Childe Hassam, and Edvard Munch—while general electric searchlights of the 'Scintillator' projected thirty-six colored steam plumes into the night fog above the Golden Gate. When the Panama-Pacific Exposition closed on December 4, 1915, after welcoming 18,876,438 visitors, demolition crews dynamited the 435-foot Tower of Jewels and leveled the Court of the Universe to clear real estate for the new Marina District residential subdivision, leaving only Maybeck's Palace of Fine Arts standing after the Palace Preservation League collected tens of thousands of petition signatures.

Ironically, Maybeck had explicitly designed the plaster rotunda to weather, crack, and surrender to nature as vines climbed its peeling columns; yet throughout the 1920s and 1930s, San Franciscans used the vast curved exhibition hall behind the lagoon for eighteen indoor tennis courts, Works Progress Administration (WPA) mural studios, and civic floral shows. During World War II, the United States Army requisitioned the Palace of Fine Arts as a quartermaster motor pool and storage depot for military jeeps and trucks bound for the Pacific Theater, while outside on the lagoon, eucalyptus trees grew sixty feet tall and winter storms stripped chunks of plaster off Ellerhusen's weeping figures.

By 1957, when Bernard Maybeck died at age ninety-five, falling plaster ornaments, dry-rotted timber trusses, and corroded iron tie-rods had forced the City of San Francisco to fence off the colonnade as a public safety hazard, sparking bitter debates over whether to bulldoze the decaying fantasy or spend millions casting it in permanent stone. In 1959, San Francisco wooden-box magnate and philanthropist Walter S. Johnson donated 2 million—matching a2 million state appropriation and a $1.8 million municipal bond approved by voters—creating the funds necessary to demolish the rotting 1915 wood-and-burlap shell while taking precision silicone and plaster molds of every original relief panel.

Between 1964 and 1967, structural engineers Hans G. Treffinger and architects Welton Becket and Associates took down the rotunda piece by piece, drove deep foundation piles through the liquefiable Marina bay fill, and recast the 162-foot dome, Corinthian capitals, Bruno Louis Zimm's frieze panels ('The Struggle for the Beautiful'), and the Weeping Women in lightweight reinforced structural concrete. Two years after the concrete reconstruction reopened in 1967, physicist Frank Oppenheimer—brother of J. Robert Oppenheimer—walked into the cavernous 100,000-square-foot curved steel exhibition hall behind the rotunda and founded the Exploratorium in 1969, filling Maybeck's temple of romantic contemplation with hands-on exhibits of light, wave optics, and human perception for forty-four years.

In the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake, when the surrounding unconsolidated hydraulic sand fill of the Marina District suffered severe soil liquefaction and gas-main fires just blocks away on Divisadero and Beach streets, the deep-pile reinforced concrete foundations of the 1967 Palace of Fine Arts held firm above the rippling lagoon. A subsequent $21 million seismic and lagoon restoration completed in 2010 cleaned the travertine-tinted concrete reliefs, rebuilt the promenade edge where swans and black-crowned night herons nest, and ensured that Maybeck's vision of 'sadness modified by beauty' remains one of the most photographed architectural silhouettes in the American West.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. 'Emotional Functionalism': Why Maybeck Built a Ruin for a World's Fair

While Beaux-Arts architects of the 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition designed triumphal arches to celebrate industrial conquest and the opening of the Panama Canal, Bernard Maybeck asked how architecture could alter human consciousness. Drawing on his training at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and his love of German Romantic painting, he designed the Palace of Fine Arts as an artificial ruin reflected in a quiet lagoon. By evoking the passage of two thousand years and the mortality of human ambition, the colonnade slowed visitors' footsteps and softened their mood before they entered the art galleries.

2. From Ephemeral 'Staff' Plaster (1915) to Seismic Concrete (1964–1967)

The original 1915 Palace of Fine Arts was never meant to survive a single decade: its columns and friezes were molded from 'staff'—a mixture of plaster of Paris, cement, glycerine, and coarse hemp burlap nailed over green timber framing. When rotting wood and falling plaster threatened total collapse by the late 1950s, artisans under Walter S. Johnson's 1964–1967 reconstruction pulled master molds directly from Ulric Ellerhusen's and Leo Lentelli's surviving sculptures and recast the entire rotunda and colonnade in low-density, steel-reinforced concrete tinted to Jules Guérin's 1915 travertine palette.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three details: first, the 'Weeping Women' atop the colonnade's attic planter boxes, sculpted by Ulric Ellerhusen to lean inward in grief after the end of beauty; second, 'Harbor View,' the 635-acre tidal marshland filled with dredged bay sand in 1912–1914 that became the Marina District after the fair's other ten palaces were demolished; and third, Bruno Louis Zimm's three high-relief panels around the rotunda entablature depicting 'The Struggle for the Beautiful,' where centaurs of materialism battle idealized artistic truth.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Begin at the eastern bank of the Palace of Fine Arts Lagoon along Palace Drive at dawn or twilight when the wind drops and the 162-foot octagonal dome mirrors perfectly in the water. Walk beneath the 1,100-foot curved colonnade along Lyon Street, look straight up at the coffered ceiling of the Rotunda and Ellerhusen's Weeping Women atop the peristyle piers, and then walk two blocks north to Marina Green to trace where the Exposition's Court of the Universe and Tower of Jewels once stood against the bay.
