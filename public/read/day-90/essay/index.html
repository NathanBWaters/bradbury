Anti-Monarchal Essay

Thomas Paine (1792) • 14 min read

Why is Royalty an absurd and detestable government? Why is the Republic a government accordant with nature and reason?

Key Context & Why It Matters

This short, fiery essay argues that kings are not just bad but absurd, and that France was right to get rid of its own. It dates from 1792, just after the French Convention abolished the monarchy and proclaimed a republic. Collections of political writing have long printed it under the name of Thomas Paine, the English-born pamphleteer of the American and French revolutions. A careful reader should know, however, that the essay refers to Paine in the third person, so its authorship is uncertain and it may have come from someone in his French circle. Whoever wrote it, the aim is clear. The author wants new republicans to understand why they should celebrate, not just that they should. The essay mocks the idea of inherited rule, answers the standard defenses of monarchy one by one, and argues that royalty and priestcraft rest on the same trick. It is a vivid example of revolutionary rhetoric at full volume.

When we reach some great good, long desired, we begin by felicitating ourselves. We triumph, we give ourselves up to this joy without rendering to our minds any full account of our reasons for it. Then comes reflection: we pass in review all the circumstances of our new happiness; we compare it in detail with our former condition; and each of these thoughts becomes a fresh enjoyment. This satisfaction, elucidated and well-considered, we now desire to procure for our readers.

In seeing Royalty abolished and the Republic established, all France has resounded with unanimous plaudits. Yet some who clap their hands do not sufficiently understand the condition they are leaving or that which they are assuming.

The perjuries of Louis, the conspiracies of his court, the wildness of his worthy brothers, have filled every Frenchman with horror, and this race was dethroned in their hearts before its fall by legal decree. But it is little to throw down an idol; it is the pedestal that above all must be broken down; it is the regal office rather than the incumbent that is murderous. All do not realize this.

Why is Royalty an absurd and detestable government? Why is the Republic a government accordant with nature and reason? At the present time a Frenchman should put himself in a position to answer these two questions clearly. For, in fine, if you are free and contented it is yet needful that you should know why.

Let us first discuss Royalty or Monarchy. Although one often wishes to distinguish between these names, common usage gives them the same sense.

Bands of brigands unite to subvert a country, place it under tribute, seize its lands, enslave its inhabitants. The expedition completed, the chieftain of the robbers adopts the title of monarch or king. Such is the origin of Royalty among all tribes⁠—huntsmen, agriculturists, shepherds.

A second brigand arrives who finds it equitable to take away by force what was conquered by violence: he dispossesses the first; he chains him, kills him, reigns in his place. Ere long time effaces the memory of this origin; the successors rule under a new form; they do a little good, from policy; they corrupt all who surround them; they invent fictitious genealogies to make their families sacred; the knavery of priests comes to their aid; they take Religion for a lifeguard: thenceforth tyranny becomes immortal, the usurped power becomes an hereditary right.

The effects of Royalty have been entirely harmonious with its origin. What scenes of horror, what refinements of iniquity, do the annals of monarchies present! If we should paint human nature with a baseness of heart, an hypocrisy, from which all must recoil and humanity disavow, it would be the portraiture of kings, their ministers and courtiers.

And why should it not be so? What should such a monstrosity produce but miseries and crimes? What is monarchy? It has been finely disguised, and the people familiarized with the odious title: in its real sense the word signifies the absolute power of one single individual, who may with impunity be stupid, treacherous, tyrannical, etc. Is it not an insult to nations to wish them so governed?

Government by a single individual is vicious in itself, independently of the individual’s vices. For however little a State, the prince is nearly always too small: where is the proportion between one man and the affairs of a whole nation?

True, some men of genius have been seen under the diadem; but the evil is then even greater: the ambition of such a man impels him to conquest and despotism, his subjects soon have to lament his glory, and sing their Te-Deums while perishing with hunger. Such is the history of Louis XIV and so many others.

But if ordinary men in power repay you with incapacity or with princely vices? But those who come to the front in monarchies are frequently mere mean mischief-makers, commonplace knaves, petty intriguers, whose small wits, which in courts reach large places, serve only to display their ineptitude in public, as soon as they appear. In short, monarchs do nothing, and their ministers do evil: this is the history of all monarchies.

But if Royalty as such is baneful, as hereditary succession it is equally revolting and ridiculous. What! there exists among my kind a man who pretends that he is born to govern me? Whence derived he such right? From his and my ancestors, says he. But how could they transmit to him a right they did not possess? Man has no authority over generations unborn. I cannot be the slave of the dead, more than of the living. Suppose that instead of our posterity, it was we who should succeed ourselves: we should not today be able to despoil ourselves of the rights which would belong to us in our second life: for a stronger reason we cannot so despoil others.

An hereditary crown! A transmissible throne! What a notion! With even a little reflection, can anyone tolerate it? Should human beings then be the property of certain individuals, born or to be born? Are we then to treat our descendants in advance as cattle, who shall have neither will nor rights of their own? To inherit government is to inherit peoples, as if they were herds. It is the basest, the most shameful fantasy that ever degraded mankind.

It is wrong to reproach kings with their ferocity, their brutal indifference, the oppressions of the people, and molestations of citizens: it is hereditary succession that makes them what they are: this breeds monsters as a marsh breeds vipers.

The logic on which the hereditary prince rests is in effect this: I derive my power from my birth; I derive my birth from God; therefore I owe nothing to men. It is little that he has at hand a complacent minister, he continues to indulge, conscientiously, in all the crimes of tyranny. This has been seen in all times and countries.

Tell me, then, what is there in common between him who is master of a people, and the people of whom he is master? Are these masters really of their kind? It is by sympathy that we are good and human: with whom does a monarch sympathize? When my neighbor suffers I pity, because I put myself in his place: a monarch pities none, because he has never been, can never be, in any other place than his own.

A monarch is an egoist by nature, the egoist par excellence. A thousand traits show that this kind of men have no point of contact with the rest of humanity. There was demanded of Charles II the punishment of Lauderdale, his favorite, who had infamously oppressed the Scotch. “Yes,” said Charles coolly, “this man has done much against the Scotch, but I cannot see that he has done anything against my interests.” Louis XIV often said: “If I follow the wishes of the people, I cannot act the king.” Even such phrases as “misfortunes of the State,” “safety of the State,” filled Louis XIV with wrath.

Could nature make a law which should assure virtue and wisdom invariably in these privileged castes that perpetuate themselves on thrones, there would be no objection to their hereditary succession. But let us pass Europe in review: all of its monarchs are the meanest of men. This one a tyrant, that one an imbecile, another a traitor, the next a debauchee, while some muster all the vices. It looks as if fate and nature had aimed to show our epoch, and all nations, the absurdity and enormity of Royalty.

But I mistake: this epoch has nothing peculiar. For, such is the essential vice of this royal succession by animal filiation, the peoples have not even the chances of nature⁠—they cannot even hope for a good prince as an alternative. All things conspire to deprive of reason and justice an individual reared to command others. The word of young Dionysius was very sensible: his father, reproaching him for a shameful action, said, “Have I given thee such example?” “Ah,” answered the youth, “thy father was not a king!”

In truth, were laughter on such a subject permissible, nothing would suggest ideas more burlesque than this fantastic institution of hereditary kings. Would it not be believed, to look at them, that there really exist particular lineages possessing certain qualities which enter the blood of the embryo prince, and adapt him physically for royalty, as a horse for the racecourse? But then, in this wild supposition, it yet becomes necessary to assure the genuine family descent of the heir presumptive. To perpetuate the noble race of Andalusian chargers, the circumstances pass before witnesses, and similar precautions seem necessary, however indecent, to make sure that the trickeries of queens shall not supply thrones with bastards, and that the kings, like the horses, shall always be thoroughbreds.

Whether one jests or reasons, there is found in this idea of hereditary royalty only folly and shame. What then is this office, which may be filled by infants or idiots? Some talent is required to be a simple workman; to be a king there is need to have only the human shape, to be a living automaton. We are astonished when reading that the Egyptians placed on the throne a flint, and called it their king. We smile at the dog Barkouf, sent by an Asiatic despot to govern one of his provinces. But monarchs of this kind are less mischievous and less absurd than those before whom whole peoples prostrate themselves. The flint and the dog at least imposed on nobody. None ascribed to them qualities or characters they did not possess. They were not styled “Father of the People,”⁠—though this were hardly more ridiculous than to give that title to a rattle-head whom inheritance crowns at eighteen. Better a mute than an animate idol. Why, there can hardly be cited an instance of a great man having children worthy of him, yet you will have the royal function pass from father to son! As well declare that a wise man’s son will be wise. A king is an administrator, and an hereditary administrator is as absurd as an author by birthright.

Royalty is thus as contrary to common sense as to common right. But it would be a plague even if no more than an absurdity; for a people who can bow down in honor of a silly thing is a debased people. Can they be fit for great affairs who render equal homage to vice and virtue, and yield the same submission to ignorance and wisdom? Of all institutions, none has caused more intellectual degeneracy. This explains the often-remarked abjectness of character under monarchies.

Such is also the effect of this contagious institution that it renders equality impossible, and draws in its train the presumption and the evils of “Nobility.” If you admit inheritance of an office, why not that of a distinction? The Nobility’s heritage asks only homage, that of the Crown commands submission. When a man says to me, “I am born illustrious,” I merely smile; when he says “I am born your master,” I set my foot on him.

When the Convention pronounced the abolition of Royalty none rose for the defence that was expected. On this subject a philosopher, who thought discussion should always precede enactment, proposed a singular thing; he desired that the Convention should nominate an orator commissioned to plead before it the cause of Royalty, so that the pitiful arguments by which it has in all ages been justified might appear in broad daylight. Judges give one accused, however certain his guilt, an official defender. In the ancient Senate of Venice there existed a public officer whose function was to contest all propositions, however incontestible, or however perfect their evidence. For the rest, pleaders for Royalty are not rare: let us open them, and see what the most specious of royalist reasoners have said.

A king is necessary to preserve a people from the tyranny of powerful men.

Establish the Rights of Man; enthrone Equality; form a good Constitution; divide well its powers; let there be no privileges, no distinctions of birth, no monopolies; make safe the liberty of industry and of trade, the equal distribution of [family] inheritances, publicity of administration, freedom of the press: these things all established, you will be assured of good laws, and need not fear the powerful men. Willingly or unwillingly, all citizens will be under the Law.

The Legislature might usurp authority, and a king is needed to restrain it.

With representatives, frequently renewed, who neither administer nor judge, whose functions are determined by the laws; with national conventions, with primary assemblies, which can be convoked any moment; with a people knowing how to read, and how to defend itself; with good journals, guns, and pikes; a Legislature would have a good deal of trouble in enjoying any months of tyranny. Let us not suppose an evil for the sake of its remedy.

A king is needed to give force to executive power.

This might be said while there existed nobles, a priesthood, parliaments, the privileged of every kind. But at present who can resist the Law, which is the will of all, whose execution is the interest of all? On the contrary the existence of an hereditary prince inspires perpetual distrust among the friends of liberty; his authority is odious to them; in checking despotism they constantly obstruct the action of government. Observe how feeble the executive power was found, after our recent pretence of marrying Royalty with Liberty.

Take note, for the rest, that those who talk in this way are men who believe that the King and the Executive Power are only one and the same thing: readers of La Feuille Villageoise are more advanced.

Others use this bad reasoning: “Were there no hereditary chief there would be an elective chief: the citizens would side with this man or that, and there would be a civil war at every election.” In the first place, it is certain that hereditary succession alone has produced the civil wars of France and England; and that beyond this are the pretended rights, of royal families which have twenty times drawn on these nations the scourge of foreign wars. It is, in fine, the heredity of crowns that has caused the troubles of Regency, which Thomas Paine calls Monarchy at nurse.

But above all it must be said, that if there be an elective chief, that chief will not be a king surrounded by courtiers, burdened with pomp, inflated by idolatries, and endowed with thirty millions of money; also, that no citizen will be tempted to injure himself by placing another citizen, his equal, for some years in an office without limited income and circumscribed power.

In a word, whoever demands a king demands an aristocracy, and thirty millions of taxes. See why Franklin described Royalism as a crime like poisoning.

Royalty, its fanatical éclat, its superstitious idolatry, the delusive assumption of its necessity, all these fictions have been invented only to obtain from men excessive taxes and voluntary servitude. Royalty and Popery have had the same aim, have sustained themselves by the same artifices, and crumble under the same Light.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The essay begins with a clever psychological observation. When we win some long-desired good, we celebrate first and think later. Then reflection arrives, and by comparing our new happiness with our old condition, we double our pleasure. The writer wants to give his readers that second, reasoned joy. France has just abolished royalty and set up a republic, and everyone is applauding. But some of those applauding do not fully understand what they have left behind or what they have taken on.

The first big idea follows. It is little to throw down an idol; it is the pedestal that must be broken. In other words, the fault is not Louis XVI the man, whose perjuries and conspiracies had made him hated, but the office itself. It is the regal office rather than the incumbent that is murderous. The writer wants Frenchmen to be able to answer two questions clearly: why is royalty absurd and detestable, and why is a republic in accord with nature and reason?

To answer the first, he attacks monarchy from several angles. One is sympathy. We are good because we can put ourselves in another's place, and a king, he says, never has been and never can be in any place but his own, so he is an egoist by nature. He illustrates with a story of Charles II, who said that a favorite who had oppressed the Scots had done nothing against his interests, and with a remark attributed to Louis XIV. Another is character. All the monarchs of Europe, he claims, are the meanest of men, and even if one were good, the system gives the people no chance of choosing one.

The sharpest ridicule is directed at heredity. The writer asks us to imagine that royal blood really produced a special ability to rule, the way a racehorse is bred for speed. In that case, he jokes, one would have to verify the lineage as carefully as breeders do. The comic point is that an office which may be filled by an infant or an idiot cannot require talent. He compares it to the Egyptians placing a flint on the throne, or an Asian despot sending a dog to govern a province, and says those at least deceived nobody. A king is an administrator, and an hereditary administrator is as absurd as an author by birthright.

The final part takes up the best arguments for monarchy and answers them. A king is said to protect people from powerful men, to restrain a legislature, to give force to the executive, and to prevent civil war at elections. To each the writer replies that a good constitution, equal rights, frequent elections, a literate people, and a free press do those jobs better, and that it is hereditary succession that has caused wars. He closes with a bold equation. Royalty and popery have had the same aim, have sustained themselves by the same artifices, and will crumble under the same light.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As rhetoric, the essay is vigorous and often very funny. The opening is a real strength: starting with the psychology of celebration is a disarming way into a polemic, and it signals that the writer is addressing readers' minds as well as their feelings. The line that it is the pedestal that must be broken, and not just the idol, is memorable and exactly right, because it makes the argument structural rather than personal. It tells new republicans that dethroning one king is not enough.

The structure of the second half is also effective. By stating the royalist arguments in plain, short headings and answering each in a paragraph, the writer gives the piece the feel of a catechism for new citizens. This is a smart design for a text meant, in the subtitle that some editions give it, for the use of new republicans. It teaches by repeated drill, and it is easy to remember. The proposal that the Convention appoint an official defender of royalty, like the devil's advocate or the officer in the old Venetian senate who contested every motion, is a neat way of showing confidence in the argument.

The jokes are the highlight. The flint, the dog, and the thoroughbred are vivid pictures, and the remark that when a man says he is born illustrious one merely smiles, but when he says he is born your master one sets a foot on him, has real punch. These lines make the argument vivid and portable.

But there are weaknesses. The breeding joke, though funny, rests on an insinuation about queens that is coarse and lazily sexist, and it relies on a stereotype rather than on evidence. The claim that all the monarchs of Europe are the meanest of men is a sweeping insult that no historian could support, and the argument from the bad character of kings is the weakest in the piece, since a defender could simply point to a good one. The writer half-sees this and pivots to the structural argument, but the rhetoric lingers.

More seriously, the answers to the royalist objections are quick, and sometimes too quick. The claim that a good constitution and a free press will make powerful men harmless is optimistic, and the remark that an elected chief would never be surrounded by courtiers or burdened with pomp was soon tested. Within a dozen years, the French Republic produced Napoleon, who had himself crowned emperor. It is a bitter irony for an essay so confident. Readers today admire the vigor but notice that the author is more interested in winning than in weighing the possible dangers of what he proposes.

III. The Impact It Had

Here honesty requires a caveat: the documented reception of this particular piece is thin. It is not a famous, widely reviewed work like Common Sense or Rights of Man, and its specific reviewers and sales are not on record. What can be said is something about its setting and its afterlife.

Its setting is the autumn of 1792. On the twenty-first of September the Convention abolished the monarchy, and the next day the French counted the beginning of a republican era. Thomas Paine, who had been elected to the Convention, was at the center of the debate, and the essay's references to his name show how closely the new republicans associated his ideas with their cause. The piece belongs to a flood of pamphlets and newspaper articles that tried to explain the new order to ordinary people, including peasants, whose papers like the Feuille Villageoise, mentioned in the essay, aimed to teach them politics in plain language.

Its afterlife has been largely through anthologies. Collections of Paine's writings have printed it, so students and readers interested in the revolution encounter it as a compact sample of republican argument. It is useful in teaching because it shows how revolutionaries tried to move from a feeling to a justification, and how they used humor and plain logic against an institution that had been protected by awe.

The essay also illustrates a theme that did shape later events: the idea that the monarchy itself, not just a bad monarch, was the problem. That view helped drive the push toward the trial and execution of Louis XVI in January 1793. Paine himself, famously, opposed the execution, arguing that the king should be exiled, a stand that contributed to his own imprisonment under the Terror. It is a reminder that the arguments of 1792 had consequences that even their advocates did not control.

Beyond that, the impact is the general one of republican polemic. Pieces like this helped make it normal to ask of any ruler by what right he rules, and to answer by appeal to reason rather than tradition. That habit of mind spread across Europe and the Americas through the nineteenth century, and this essay is one small, vivid piece of the evidence.

IV. What Readers Loved

Because this essay is obscure compared with its famous cousins, there is little recorded praise. What can be said is what readers tend to like in it today.

First, they like the energy. The writer is plainly delighted by the argument, and the delight is catching. Reading it, one feels the mood of 1792, when the abolition of a thousand-year institution seemed like the dawn of a new age. That sense of exhilaration is rare in political writing, which is usually cautious, and it gives the piece a youthful charm.

Second, they like the humor. For modern readers the jokes about the flint, the dog, and the thoroughbred are the high points. They are irreverent in a way that still feels fresh, and they show that mockery can be a serious political tool. The line about being born your master is the sort of sentence people copy into notebooks.

Third, the writer's confidence in ordinary people is appealing. The essay assumes that readers can follow an argument, that they can distinguish between a person and an office, and that a people who know how to read and defend themselves can govern without a monarch. It treats the audience as citizens rather than subjects, and many readers admire that respect.

Fourth, the essay gives a clear and compressed account of why hereditary rule is troubling, which is useful for students. The point that an office passing by birth is as absurd as an author by birthright can be grasped immediately, and it opens up larger questions about inherited privilege that are still alive today, in debates about wealth, titles, and political dynasties.

Finally, there is a historical pleasure in hearing a revolution explain itself. The reader gets a glimpse of the arguments that circulated in the streets and journals of Paris, with all their bravado and their blind spots. For a person trying to understand how people actually talked in 1792, the essay is a vivid document, and that is reason enough to read it.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The first problem is the one a careful reader meets immediately: who wrote it? The essay is widely printed among Paine's works, but its text speaks of Thomas Paine in the third person, saying that he calls monarchy at nurse the regency of a child king, and it refers to Franklin and to readers of a French journal as if from outside. It also talks about the French in the first person plural, as a Frenchman would. These details suggest that it was written by a French republican, or translated from one, and that the attribution to Paine may be loose. The question is unsettled, and readers should treat the authorship as uncertain rather than as established.

The more substantive criticism is aimed at the argument. Defenders of monarchy then and later have said that the essay confuses the worst kings with the institution, and that it ignores the stabilizing function of a hereditary head of state who stands above party. Modern constitutional monarchies, such as Britain, the Netherlands, and Sweden, are among the most stable democracies, which makes the claim that royalty is inherently an engine of servitude look overstated. The writer would answer that these are not true monarchies, but that is a debatable rejoinder.

A second criticism is about moral tone. The essay's ridicule can slide into contempt, and the line about setting one's foot on a man who says he is born your master is the language of a revolution that was becoming violent. Critics have pointed out that a movement which begins with jokes about an idol can end by treating the idol's people as enemies, and the next year did bring the Terror. The essay does not foresee that, and its cheerful confidence now reads with a shadow.

Third, there is the historical record. The writer predicts that an elected head of state would never be a king surrounded by courtiers, but within years the French republic ended in an empire. Historians note that revolutions often produce new forms of personal rule, and that dismissing the risk, as the writer does, was naive.

Fourth, the essay's anti-clerical ending has drawn objections. The equation of royalty and popery treats religion as a mere trick of tax collectors, which many believers and many historians find reductive. The same impulse, applied in the Revolution's dechristianization campaigns, produced real persecution. So the essay is read both as a spirited defense of republican principle and as a case study in the blind spots of revolutionary certainty.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

This essay belongs to the great tradition of anti-monarchical writing that stretches across the early modern period. Its ancestors include John Milton's Tenure of Kings and Magistrates, written to justify the trial of Charles I, and John Locke's attack on the divine right theory of Robert Filmer, which argued that kings inherit their power from Adam. Voltaire and the French philosophes mocked kings and priests for decades before the Revolution, and Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels turned the courts of Europe into objects of satire. The essay's jokes about absurd rulers belong to this line of satire.

The closest relative is Paine's own Common Sense of 1776, which also ridiculed hereditary monarchy and declared that one honest man is worth more to society than all the crowned ruffians that ever lived. Paine's Rights of Man followed in 1791 and 1792, and the essay we have read applies the same logic to the French case. If the work is indeed by another hand, it shows how widely that style had spread and how readily the new republicans borrowed Paine's plain manner.

Looking forward, the essay descends into the nineteenth-century republican movements of France, Spain, Italy, and Latin America, which kept arguing that an inherited head of state is incompatible with equality. It also anticipates later arguments about inherited privilege in general, including the case against hereditary titles and dynasties, and the modern movements in countries such as Australia and Britain that debate whether to keep a monarch at all.

For comparison, one can set it against Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France, which defended inherited institutions as the accumulated wisdom of ages and warned that tearing down the old order would unleash chaos. Burke's prediction proved partly right, and this essay's optimism partly wrong. Reading them together gives a fuller picture of the argument that shaped modern politics. It is the argument between those who think institutions should justify themselves to reason and those who think reason should be humble before inheritance.

Why read it today? Not because it is the best argument against monarchy, but because it shows the moment when such an argument became a popular passion. It captures the excitement, the humor, the overconfidence, and the moral energy of a new republic in its first weeks. It is a short, lively document, and it reminds us that political change begins with someone persuading an audience that a thing they always accepted is, when you look at it, a little ridiculous.
