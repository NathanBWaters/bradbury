Admiral Yi Sun-sin, the Turtle Ship, and the Whirlpools of Myeongnyang (1597)

Korea — How a disgraced, tortured commander used tidal hydrodynamics and thirteen warships to shatter an invading armada of 133 vessels (Imjin War / Joseon Dynasty (1592–1598)) • 11 min read

“Those who seek death shall live, and those who seek life shall die.” (필사즉생 필생즉사) — Admiral Yi Sun-sin, War Diary (Nanjung Ilgi), September 15, 1597

At dawn on October 26, 1597 (the sixteenth day of the ninth lunar month), thick autumn mist clung to the jagged cliffs of Uldolmok—literally 'The Roaring Throat'—the narrowest point of the Myeongnyang Strait between Jindo Island and the southwestern tip of the Korean peninsula. On the deck of his flagship, fifty-two-year-old Admiral Yi Sun-sin stood in brigandine armor, his body still scarred from the *hyeongjang* torture rods of the royal interrogation court in Seoul just months earlier. Behind him floated the entire surviving Joseon navy: twelve flat-bottomed *panokseon* warships, plus his own vessel—thirteen ships in total, facing an approaching Japanese armada of 133 warships and 200 support vessels.

Five years earlier, in April 1592, Japanese regent Toyotomi Hideyoshi had launched the Imjin War, landing 158,000 battle-hardened samurai at Busan equipped with Portuguese-style matchlock muskets (*teppo*). Within twenty days the Japanese army captured Hanyang (Seoul) and marched north toward Pyongyang. Yet while Joseon armies collapsed on land, Yi Sun-sin—then Commander of the Left Jeolla Naval District in Yeosu—systematically severed Hideyoshi’s maritime supply lines across twenty-three consecutive naval engagements without losing a single ship.

Yi’s early triumphs rested on two technological marvels: the heavy pine-timbered *panokseon* ('board-roofed ship') and his revived, iron-spiked assault vessel, the *Geobukseon* (Turtle Ship). Built by master shipwright Na Dae-yong months before the invasion broke out, the *Geobukseon* enclosed its upper deck beneath a curved wooden shell armored with hexagonal iron plates and razor-sharp iron spikes to foil Japanese boarding tactics—the primary method of samurai naval warfare. Through a fierce dragon head mounted at the bow, sulfur and saltpeter smoke belched across the water to mask the ship's movements, while up to twenty-six *Cheonja* ('Heaven') and *Jija* ('Earth') cannons fired iron shrapnel and six-foot winged wooden iron-tipped arrows (*daejeon*) through gunports along both broadsides.

Unable to defeat Admiral Yi at sea, Japanese intelligence agents exploited the bitter factionalism of the Joseon royal court. In early 1597, a double agent named Yoshira leaked false coordinates for general Kato Kiyomasa’s fleet. Suspecting a coastal ambush among treacherous shoals, Yi refused to sail into the trap. His jealous rival Won Gyun and court ministers convinced King Seonjo that Yi was treasonously cowardly. Stripped of his rank, chained in a wooden cangue, and marched to Seoul, Yi was beaten until his flesh tore, spared execution only because elder minister Jeong Tak pleaded that killing Joseon’s greatest commander would hand the war to Japan. Reduced to a common foot soldier (*baekui-jonggun*—'serving in white'), Yi walked south just as his replacement, Won Gyun, led the entire Joseon fleet of 160 ships into a catastrophic ambush at Chilcheonryang, where virtually every ship—including the Turtle Ships—was sunk and Won Gyun killed.

Panic gripped the royal court. King Seonjo hastily reinstated Yi as Commander of the Three Provinces and sent a royal dispatch advising him to abandon the sea entirely, burn the twelve remaining ships, and join the land forces. Yi dipped his brush in ink and wrote back the most famous memorial in Korean history: *'shin-e-ge-neun a-jik yeol-du cheok-ui 배가 있사옵니다'*—'Your Majesty, your servant still has twelve warships left. As long as I live, the enemy will never look down upon us across the Western Sea.'

Refusing to fight in open water where 133 Japanese vessels under Todo Takatora and Kurushima Michifusa could encircle his tiny squadron, Yi lured the Japanese fleet into the 294-meter-wide bottleneck of the Myeongnyang Strait. Yi had spent days studying the strait’s extreme tidal physics: every three hours, the vast volume of water between the Yellow Sea and the South Sea squeezes through Uldolmok at speeds up to 11.5 knots (over 21 kilometers per hour), creating boiling whirlpools and an underwater acoustic roar audible miles inland—and then abruptly reverses direction.

When the Japanese vanguard surged into the narrow channel on the morning north-flowing tide, terrified Joseon captains held back, paralyzed by the ten-to-one odds. For nearly an hour, Yi’s flagship fought entirely alone at the mouth of the choke point. Anchoring his flat-bottomed *panokseon* so it would not spin in the eddies, Yi unleashed concentrated broadsides of *hyeonja* cannon and fire arrows into the tightly packed, deep-keeled Japanese *sekibune* warships, which collided with one another in the narrow channel. When Captain An Wi finally edged forward, Yi roared across the water: 'An Wi! Do you wish to die under military law? If you think you can live by hiding, you are mistaken!'

Then came the moment Yi had calculated to the minute. Just past noon, the tide slackened for a brief breath and violently reversed, roaring southward directly into the bows of the Japanese fleet while carrying Yi’s thirteen heavy *panokseon* downstream like battering rams. Caught in the whirling currents, the light Japanese vessels spun out of control, smashing their hulls and oars against one another. Joseon fishermen and coastal villagers who had watched from the cliffs rowed out to haul a pre-laid iron chain cable taut across the current while others dragged disabled Japanese ships into the eddies. Japanese commander Kurushima Michifusa was pulled from the water and decapitated, his head hoisted to the top of Yi’s mainmast.

By dusk, thirty-one Japanese warships had been sunk at the bottom of the Roaring Throat and over ninety more were crippled, while Admiral Yi had not lost a single one of his thirteen vessels. The miracle of Myeongnyang saved the breadbasket of Jeolla Province, cut off Japanese armies advancing on Seoul, and forced the invaders into a full retreat that ended a year later at the Battle of Noryang—where Admiral Yi, struck in the chest by a stray matchlock bullet at the moment of final victory, whispered to his son and nephew: 'The battle is at its height; do not announce my death. Beat my war drums.'

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Naval Architecture: Why the Panokseon & Geobukseon Dominated

To understand Yi Sun-sin's 23-0 undefeated record, compare the physics of the two fleets. Japanese warships (atakebune and sekibune) were built with V-shaped cedar hulls for speed in the open sea, optimized for closing quickly and boarding with swords because recoil from heavy cannon would crack their thin hulls. Joseon panokseon and Geobukseon (Turtle Ships) were constructed from thick, dense Korean red pine joined with oak wooden pegs (jangsak) that expanded when wet, atop a flat U-shaped hull. This flat bottom allowed Joseon ships to pivot 360 degrees on a single point in shallow coastal currents and absorb the recoil of dozens of long-range cannons firing in rotating broadsides.

2. The War Diary (Nanjung Ilgi): Inside the Mind of a Stoic Commander

From January 1, 1592, until two days before his death in November 1598, Admiral Yi kept a private daily journal in cursive Classical Chinese known as the Nanjung Ilgi (now National Treasure No. 76 and a UNESCO Memory of the World artifact). Far from a boastful military chronicle, the diary reveals a deeply human, often anguished man: he records weeping through the night after learning his youngest son Myeon had been killed by Japanese troops just days after Yi's release from torture; he tracks the phases of the moon and tides; and he notes drinking rice wine under the moonlight while playing the flute to steady his officers' nerves.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three historic phrases in mind: first, Uldolmok (울돌목, 'The Roaring Throat'), the Korean vernacular name for the Myeongnyang Strait where the underwater rocks literally groan under the 11.5-knot current; second, Baekui-jonggun (백의종군, 'serving in white clothes'), describing how a stripped, humiliated general marched as a common private without complaint to save his country; and third, Pilsajeuksaeng Pilsaengjeuksa (필사즉생 필생즉사)—'Those who seek death shall live, and those who seek life shall die'—which Yi brushed into his diary the night before Myeongnyang.
