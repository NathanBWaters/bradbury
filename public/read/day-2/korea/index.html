Admiral Yi Sun-sin, the Turtle Ship, and the Whirlpools of Myeongnyang (1597)

Korea — How a disgraced, tortured commander used tidal hydrodynamics and thirteen warships to shatter an invading armada of 133 vessels () • 20 min read

“Those who seek death shall live, and those who seek life shall die.” (필사즉생 필생즉사) — Admiral Yi Sun-sin, War Diary (Nanjung Ilgi), September 15, 1597

Key Context & Why It Matters

On the morning of October 26, 1597, at the narrow tidal choke point of Uldolmok ('The Roaring Throat') between Jindo Island and Haenam in southwestern Korea, fifty-two-year-old Admiral Yi Sun-sin stood on the deck of his flagship with just twelve other surviving panokseon warships against an approaching Japanese invasion armada of 133 warships and 200 transport vessels. Only months earlier, court factionalism and Japanese counter-intelligence had seen Yi stripped of command, chained, and tortured nearly to death in Seoul while his replacement lost the entire Joseon fleet at Chilcheonryang. Reinstated in desperation, Yi refused royal orders to abandon the sea, instead turning the 11.5-knot reversing whirlpools of the Myeongnyang Strait and heavy Joseon broadside cannonry into a hydrodynamic trap that shattered the invading fleet without losing a single ship.

Before daybreak on October 26, 1597—the sixteenth day of the ninth lunar month—cold autumn mist hung low over the jagged granite cliffs of Uldolmok, the narrowest throat of the Myeongnyang Strait between the Haenam peninsula and Jindo Island at the southwestern tip of Korea. Even before the sun cleared the coastal ridges, the water beneath the mist was groaning like a living beast. Here, where the vast tides of the West Sea and the South Sea squeeze through a rocky bottleneck barely 294 meters wide, submerged reefs tear the current into boiling whirlpools whose subterranean roar gives Uldolmok its Korean name: 'The Roaring Throat.' Standing on the command platform (*jangdae*) of his flagship, fifty-two-year-old Admiral Yi Sun-sin gripped the hilt of his six-foot war sword. Beneath his brigandine coat of crimson wool, his kneecaps and ribs still throbbed from the *hyeongjang* beating rods and leg-twisting *juri* torture administered in Seoul six months earlier. Behind him floated the entire surviving Joseon navy: twelve flat-bottomed *panokseon* warships, facing an approaching Japanese armada of 133 warships backed by 200 support vessels.

To understand how Joseon's greatest commander came to stand in that mist with thirteen ships against an empire, one must rewind five years to the spring of 1592. Fresh from unifying Japan's warring provinces, regent Toyotomi Hideyoshi assembled an invasion force of 158,000 veteran samurai in Kyushu to march through Korea and conquer Ming China. On April 13, 1592 (lunar), the Japanese First Division under Konishi Yukinaga landed at Busan. Armed with Portuguese-derived *teppo* matchlock muskets and led by generals Kato Kiyomasa and Kuroda Nagamasa, the invaders overwhelmed Korea's long-neglected land fortresses. Within twenty days, after General Sin Rip's cavalry was slaughtered at Chungju, King Seonjo and his court fled Hanyang in the pouring night rain toward Uiju on the Chinese border, while furious bondservants burned the palace slave registries.

As Joseon's armies disintegrated on land, the survival of the kingdom fell upon a forty-seven-year-old naval commander in the southern port of Yeosu. Appointed Commander of the Left Jeolla Naval District (*Jeolla Jwasusa*) in 1591 through the recommendation of his childhood friend, Prime Minister Ryu Seong-ryong, Yi Sun-sin was no pampered courtier. Born in 1545 in Hanyang's Geoncheon-dong neighborhood, he had fallen from his horse and broken his leg during his first military examination in 1572, coolly splinted the fracture with willow branches using one hand, and climbed back into the saddle to finish the course. Later stationed on the northern Jurchen frontier, he refused to bribe corrupt War Ministry inspectors and endured false demotion to common soldier without complaint.

The moment Yi arrived at Yeosu in February 1591, fourteen months before Hideyoshi's invasion, he recognized that war with Japan was imminent. Inspecting every harbor under his command, he tightened discipline, stockpiled grain and gunpowder, stretched heavy iron chains across harbor mouths, and collaborated with master shipwright Na Dae-yong to resurrect and perfect a radical naval weapon: the *Geobukseon*, or Turtle Ship. While Japanese naval doctrine treated warships (*atakebune* and *sekibune*) as floating infantry transports—built with thin, V-shaped hulls of light Japanese cedar (*sugi*) to sprint across the water, grapple an enemy vessel, and loose waves of sword-wielding samurai onto the decks—Yi Sun-sin engineered his fleet as mobile, unboardable floating fortresses.

Both the standard two-decked Joseon warship, the *panokseon* ('board-roofed ship'), and the assault *Geobukseon* were constructed from massive timbers of dense Korean red pine (*sonamu*) and oak, joined not with iron nails that rusted and sheared in saltwater, but with seasoned oak dowels (*jangsak*) and mortise-and-tenon joints that swelled tight when wet. Their flat, U-shaped hulls had shallow drafts that allowed seventy oarsmen shielded on the lower deck to pivot the vessel 360 degrees in its own length amid shallow coastal shoals, while absorbing the tremendous recoil of heavy bronze artillery on the upper gun deck. On the *Geobukseon*, Na Dae-yong enclosed the entire upper deck beneath a curved wooden carapace covered with hexagonal iron plates and studded with razor-sharp iron spikes (*cheolchom*) concealed beneath straw mats to impale any samurai who leaped aboard. At the bow snarled a carved dragon's head capable of belching toxic clouds of burning sulfur and saltpeter smoke, while gunports along both broadsides bristled with up to twenty-six heavy cannons.

Those cannons were technological marvels of sixteenth-century East Asian metallurgy, named sequentially after the first four characters of the *Thousand Character Classic*: *Cheonja* ('Heaven'), *Jija* ('Earth'), *Hyeonja* ('Black'), and *Hwangja* ('Yellow'). Instead of relying solely on round stone or iron shot, Yi's gunners loaded the massive bronze *Cheonja* and *Jija* barrels with *daejeon* and *janggunjeon*—six-foot-long, iron-tipped oak beams stabilized by iron fins that screamed through the air like ballistic missiles, splintering Japanese cedar hulls at 500 meters—paired with double-fistfuls of cast-iron shrapnel (*cheolhwan*) and incendiary bombs (*jillyeopo*) that shredded sails and rigging.

Beginning at Okpo on May 7, 1592, Yi Sun-sin unleashed a naval campaign without parallel in maritime history. At Sacheon on May 29, he deployed the *Geobukseon* in combat for the first time, feigning retreat to draw twelve Japanese warships into open water before wheeling around on the rising tide and reducing every vessel to burning driftwood; struck through the left shoulder by a musket ball during the battle, Yi forbade his aides from bandaging the wound until nightfall so his sailors would not lose heart, digging the lead bullet out with a knife blade. At Dangpo and Danghangpo in June, and then at the Battle of Hansando on July 8, 1592, Yi lured Wakizaka Yasuharu's elite armada of seventy-three warships out of the narrow Gyeonnaeryang channel into the open bay off Hansan Island, then swung his fleet into the *Hakikjin*—the Crescent 'Crane-Wing Formation'—encircling the enemy in a three-sided crossfire that destroyed fifty-nine Japanese ships in a single afternoon.

By shattering Hideyoshi's western maritime corridor, Yi prevented Japanese naval forces from sailing up the Yellow Sea to resupply Konishi Yukinaga's army in Pyongyang, starving the land invasion in its tracks and safeguarding the fertile rice plains of Jeolla Province. Establishing his Allied Naval Headquarters of the Three Provinces (*Samdo Sugun Tongjeyong*) on Hansando Island in 1593, Yi transformed a barren island into a self-sufficient maritime commonwealth: welcoming tens of thousands of war refugees, opening salt evaporation pans, forging iron weapons, brewing medicinal herbs against typhus outbreaks, and writing nightly in his cursive Classical Chinese diary, the *Nanjung Ilgi*. Across twenty-three consecutive naval engagements between 1592 and 1596, Yi Sun-sin never lost a single warship.

Unable to defeat Admiral Yi on the water, Japanese commanders turned to psychological warfare inside the faction-ridden royal court in Hanyang. In early 1597, as peace talks with Ming China collapsed and Hideyoshi launched a second invasion (*Jeongyu Jaeran*), a cunning Japanese double agent from Tsushima named Yoshira leaked word to Joseon General Kim Eung-seo that Kato Kiyomasa's fleet would cross the Korea Strait on a specific date, urging King Seonjo to send Yi Sun-sin out to intercept him near Busan. Having scouted those waters for years, Yi recognized a deadly trap: the intelligence came from the enemy, the offshore islands were swarming with concealed Japanese squadrons, and winter gales would dash his heavy *panokseon* against uncharted reefs. He refused to sail blind.

In Hanyang, Yi's bitter rival Won Gyun joined forces with court factions to whisper in King Seonjo's ear that the victorious admiral had grown arrogant and treasonous. In March 1597, royal bailiffs arrived at Hansando, stripped Yi of his command seal, locked his neck in a heavy wooden cangue (*kal*), and marched him in an oxcart 400 kilometers north to Seoul while weeping villagers lined the roads. Thrown into the dungeons of the *Uigeumbu* on March 4 (lunar), Yi was subjected to brutal leg-breaking torture on April 4 to force a confession. Only the courageous memorial (*Sin-gu-cha*) submitted by eighty-two-year-old Right State Councillor Jeong Tak—who warned the king that executing the realm's sole undefeated naval genius would delight only the Japanese—spared Yi from the executioner's blade. On April 11, stripped of all rank, Yi was released under the sentence of *baekui-jonggun* ('serving in white hemp clothes' as a common unranked foot soldier). Limping south toward General Gwon Yul's camp, he received word at Asan that his eighty-three-year-old mother, Lady Byeon, had died aboard a boat while trying to sail north to see him one last time.

Three months later, the catastrophe Yi had foreseen struck with apocalyptic fury. Installed as Yi's replacement at Hansando, Won Gyun purged Yi's veteran staff, drank heavily, and—after being flogged by Supreme Commander Gwon Yul for inaction—blindly led the entire Joseon armada of over 160 warships toward Busan in July 1597 without scouting or supply barges. Repulsed off Busan by rough seas and shore batteries, his exhausted oarsmen retreated after dark on August 27 into the narrow channel of Chilcheonryang beside Geoje Island. Before dawn on August 28, Japanese fleets under Todo Takatora, Wakizaka Yasuharu, and Shimazu Yoshihiro sealed both ends of the strait and attacked from sea and land simultaneously. In a single night of fire and slaughter, roughly 150 *panokseon* and every precious *Geobukseon* were burned or sunk. Won Gyun, Commander Yi Eok-gi, and Choe Ho died in the rout; only Right Gyeongsang Commander Bae Seol escaped westward with twelve ships.

With the sea lanes wide open, Japanese armies stormed Namwon Fortress in August 1597 and marched toward Seoul, while the victorious Japanese fleet rounded the southwestern coast toward the Yellow Sea. In Hanyang, a terrified King Seonjo hastily issued an edict reinstating Yi Sun-sin as Naval Commander of the Three Provinces. Accompanied by just nine officers and six soldiers, Yi rode day and night across the mountain passes of South Jeolla, recruiting scattered refugees and reaching the harbor of Hoeryongpo in Boseong, where Bae Seol handed over the twelve surviving *panokseon*. Soon afterward, a royal courier arrived from Seoul bearing an edict from King Seonjo: since twelve ships were far too feeble to confront the Japanese armada, Yi was authorized to burn his ships, abolish the Joseon navy, and join General Gwon Yul's army ashore. Dipping his horsehair brush in black ink, Yi wrote back the most famous memorial in Korean history (*Sangnyunmun*): 'Your Majesty, your servant still possesses twelve warships (*Sin-e-ge-neun a-jik yeol-du cheok-ui jeonseon-i issaomnida*). So long as I live and fight to the death, the enemy will never dare look down upon us.'

Joined shortly thereafter by former magistrate Kim Eok-chu bringing one repaired warship—raising the fleet to thirteen *panokseon* without a single Turtle Ship—Yi shifted his base to Byeokpajin on Jindo Island. Even within his tiny squadron, terror ran deep: on the night of September 2, Bae Seol deserted in the dark and fled inland. On the night of September 14, after a scout revealed that over 200 Japanese ships had anchored at Oranpo, Yi heard the nocturnal roar of the Myeongnyang Strait echoing across the water. Realizing that Byeokpajin sat down-current where his thirteen ships could be surrounded in open water, Yi moved his fleet across the strait to Usuyeong in Haenam on September 15 and summoned his captains to his tent. Quoting the ancient strategist Wu Qi, he spoke the words he brushed into his *Nanjung Ilgi* that night: 'According to the principles of strategy, *he who seeks death shall live, and he who seeks life shall die* (*Pilsajeuksaeng Pilsaengjeuksa*). Furthermore, if one man stands guard at a narrow gateway, he can strike terror into a thousand enemies (*Ilbu-dang-gwan manbu-mak-gae*).'

When the sun rose on October 26, 1597, the Japanese vanguard of 133 warships—commanded by Todo Takatora, Kato Yoshiaki, Wakizaka Yasuharu, and Sea-Pirate Daimyo Kurushima Michifusa—rode the screaming northwest flood tide straight into the 294-meter funnel of Uldolmok. Yi weighed anchor and led his thirteen *panokseon* into the northern mouth of the strait. Yet as the wall of red-lacquered Japanese prows filled the horizon, his twelve subordinate captains froze in terror, backing water half a mile to the rear. Refusing to retreat a single oar-stroke—knowing that if he turned his bow, the 11.5-knot flood tide would sweep the Japanese fleet straight past the bottleneck into the open Yellow Sea—Admiral Yi dropped anchor right at the lip of the roaring current. For nearly a full hour, his single flagship fought 133 Japanese warships alone.

Because the narrow channel and boiling whirlpools of Uldolmok prevented more than a handful of Japanese *sekibune* from advancing abreast without smashing into one another or the granite reefs, Yi turned their own numbers into a death trap. Pivoting his flat-bottomed *panokseon* on its anchor cable, Yi's gunners poured rapid-fire broadsides of *Cheonja*, *Jija*, and *Hyeonja* cannon balls, iron shrapnel, and winged *daejeon* rocket-arrows into the densely packed Japanese vanguard. When Japanese musket balls rained like hail across his upper deck and his men ducked behind the gunwales, Yi stood upright in full armor on the forecastle, beating the war drum. Seeing his other ships still hanging back, Yi hoisted the *choyogi* command flag and blew the great conch shell (*nagak*), summoning Magistrate An Wi of Geojedo and Commander Kim Eung-ham. As An Wi's ship finally crept within hailing distance, Yi roared across the waves: 'An Wi! Do you wish to die under military law? Do you think you can live by hiding behind me?'

Stung by the rebuke, An Wi and Kim Eung-ham drove their *panokseon* straight into the Japanese line. Instantly, three Japanese warships commanded by Kurushima Michifusa grappled An Wi's hull, and scores of samurai swarmed over his bulwarks. While An Wi and his crew fought desperately on deck with pikes and oak staffs, Yi Sun-sin weighed anchor, swung his flagship around, and slammed into Kurushima's squadron at point-blank range, blasting them apart. In the churning foam below, a Japanese defector serving aboard Yi's flagship named Junsa pointed out a warrior floating in the wreckage wearing a brocaded red surcoat and horned helmet; Yi's sailors hauled the body aboard with iron grappling hooks, identified Daimyo Kurushima Michifusa, severed his head, and hoisted it to the top of the mainmast for the entire armada to see. At that exact moment—shortly past noon—the tidal miracle Yi had calculated to the minute arrived: the northwest flood current slackened for a breathless pause, and then the Myeongnyang Strait violently reversed direction, thundering southeastward at eleven knots. Pinned against the reversing torrent while local Joseon women danced in wide *Ganggangsullae* circles atop Mount Manggeumsan to mimic vast reinforcements and fishermen towed decoy boats behind the strait, the deep-keeled Japanese warships spun uncontrollably into one another. Riding the outbound current downstream like thirteen iron hammers, Yi's fleet sank thirty-one enemy warships and crippled over ninety more without losing a single vessel—saving Joseon and setting the stage for the war's final hour at Noryang Strait a year later, where Admiral Yi, struck in the chest by a stray matchlock ball at the moment of final victory, whispered to his son Hoe and nephew Wan: 'The battle is at its height; do not announce my death. Beat my war drums.'

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Naval Architecture: Why the Panokseon & Geobukseon Dominated

To understand Yi Sun-sin's 23-0 undefeated record, compare the physics of the two fleets. Japanese warships (atakebune and sekibune) were built with V-shaped cedar hulls for speed in the open sea, optimized for closing quickly and boarding with swords because recoil from heavy cannon would crack their thin hulls. Joseon panokseon and Geobukseon (Turtle Ships) were constructed from thick, dense Korean red pine joined with oak wooden pegs (jangsak) that expanded when wet, atop a flat U-shaped hull. This flat bottom allowed Joseon ships to pivot 360 degrees on a single point in shallow coastal currents and absorb the recoil of dozens of long-range cannons firing in rotating broadsides.

2. The War Diary (Nanjung Ilgi): Inside the Mind of a Stoic Commander

From January 1, 1592, until two days before his death in November 1598, Admiral Yi kept a private daily journal in cursive Classical Chinese known as the Nanjung Ilgi (now National Treasure No. 76 and a UNESCO Memory of the World artifact). Far from a boastful military chronicle, the diary reveals a deeply human, often anguished man: he records weeping through the night after learning his youngest son Myeon had been killed by Japanese troops just days after Yi's release from torture; he tracks the phases of the moon and tides; and he notes drinking rice wine under the moonlight while playing the flute to steady his officers' nerves.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three historic phrases in mind: first, Uldolmok (울돌목, 'The Roaring Throat'), the Korean vernacular name for the Myeongnyang Strait where the underwater rocks literally groan under the 11.5-knot current; second, Baekui-jonggun (백의종군, 'serving in white clothes'), describing how a stripped, humiliated general marched as a common private without complaint to save his country; and third, Pilsajeuksaeng Pilsaengjeuksa (필사즉생 필생즉사)—'Those who seek death shall live, and those who seek life shall die'—which Yi brushed into his diary the night before Myeongnyang. 4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Travel to the Myeongnyang Daecheop Memorial Park at Haenam and Jindo (34.5736° N, 126.3061° E), where a skywalk extends directly over the churning whirlpools of Uldolmok beneath the twin-pylon Jindo Bridge; at peak tide, you can still hear the water roaring against the submerged reefs. In central Seoul, Admiral Yi's bronze statue stands guard just south of King Sejong in Gwanghwamun Square with a replica Geobukseon in the underground exhibition hall, while Hyeonchungsa Shrine in Asan preserves Yi's original handwritten Nanjung Ilgi and his twin six-foot war swords.
