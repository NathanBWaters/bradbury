Emperor Norton I: How a Bankrupted Rice Merchant Became San Francisco's Enlightened Monarch

San Francisco — The true story of Joshua Abraham Norton (1818–1880), who issued his own currency, halted anti-Chinese riots with a prayer, and decreed the Bay Bridge sixty years early (Post-Gold Rush Gilded Age (1859–1880)) • 20 min read

“At the peremptory request and desire of a large majority of the citizens of these United States, I, Joshua Norton, formerly of Algoa Bay, Cape of Good Hope, declare and proclaim myself Emperor of these United States.” — San Francisco Daily Evening Bulletin, September 17, 1859

Late on the afternoon of September 17, 1859, a stocky, dignified forty-one-year-old gentleman with a full dark beard and piercing blue eyes walked into the editorial office of the *San Francisco Daily Evening Bulletin* at the corner of Montgomery and Merchant streets. Approaching editor-in-chief George Fitch, he silently placed a single handwritten sheet of paper on the desk and bowed out the door. Rather than tossing the note into the wastebasket as the ravings of another Gold Rush eccentric, Fitch read it twice and printed it verbatim in that evening’s newspaper under the dry, amused headline *'Have We an Emperor Among Us?'* The notice announced that at the peremptory request and desire of a large majority of the citizens of the United States, Joshua Abraham Norton, formerly of Algoa Bay, Cape of Good Hope, formally declared and proclaimed himself *Emperor of these United States*—and commanded the representatives of the different states of the Union to assemble at Musical Hall in San Francisco on the first day of February next to make such alterations in the existing laws of the Union as might ameliorate the evils under which the country was laboring.

Ten years before he claimed the American throne, nobody on Montgomery Street would have mistaken Joshua Norton for a pauper or a dreamer. Born in Deptford on the outskirts of London in February 1818 to English-Jewish parents, John and Sarah Norton, young Joshua had sailed with his family at the age of two to South Africa’s Eastern Cape as part of the British government’s 1820 Settlers scheme. Raised in the frontier port of Algoa Bay (modern Port Elizabeth) and educated in Grahamstown and Cape Town, where his father built a prosperous ship-chandling and mercantile enterprise, Norton learned the mathematics of maritime trade, colonial real estate, and commodity arbitrage. After the deaths of his mother, brothers, and finally his father in 1848, the thirty-one-year-old Norton liquidated the family estate, tucked forty thousand dollars of inherited capital into his money belt, and sailed via Rio de Janeiro aboard the Hamburg vessel *Franziska*, stepping ashore at Yerba Buena Cove in November 1849.

While tens of thousands of reckless Argonauts lost their shirts knee-deep in the icy gravel of the Yuba River, Joshua Norton stayed dry in downtown San Francisco and multiplied his fortune with astonishing speed. Operating from a brisk countinghouse on Montgomery Street, he bought prime corner water lots at Jackson and Sansome streets, erected brick warehouses, operated one of the city’s first steam-powered rice mills, and ran a lucrative cigar factory. By the autumn of 1852, Norton’s net worth had soared to a quarter of a million dollars—equivalent to nearly nine million dollars today. He dined nightly on roasted canvasback duck and claret at the city’s premier French rotisseries, served alongside future civic leaders on the First Vigilance Committee of 1851 and the Hebrew Benevolent Society, and was universally regarded by bankers as one of the sharpest commodity men on the Pacific Coast.

Then came December 1852, and the single speculative gamble that shattered his life. Earlier that year, widespread famine in southern China had prompted the Qing imperial authorities to ban the export of Chinese rice, starving San Francisco’s rapidly growing Chinese and European populations of their dietary staple. Within weeks, the wholesale price of rice on the San Francisco waterfront rocketed ninefold, from four cents to thirty-six cents a pound. On December 22, 1852, commodity broker Asahel Mann approached Norton with what appeared to be the deal of a lifetime: the mercantile house of Ruiz Hermanos had just received the ship *Glyde* from Peru carrying two hundred thousand pounds of South American rice, and was willing to sell the entire cargo to Norton for twelve and a half cents a pound—twenty-five thousand dollars in total, with two thousand dollars paid down on the spot.

Norton signed the contract, dreaming of cornering the entire Pacific Coast rice market. Less than a week later, the winter fog parted off the Golden Gate and an entire flotilla of delayed merchant ships from Callao and Valparaíso sailed into the bay, every hold crammed to the hatches with cheap Peruvian rice. Overnight, the wholesale price collapsed to three cents a pound. Ruined if he honored the contract, Norton refused to take delivery of the remaining sacks from the *Glyde*, claiming that the merchants had misrepresented the quality and grade of the grain. For the next two and a half years, from 1853 to 1855, Norton fought a desperate, exhausting legal war through the courts. Although he initially won a jury verdict in the district court, the California Supreme Court reversed the judgment in *Ruiz v. Norton* in October 1854, ruling that a written commercial contract could not be voided by oral assurances.

Financial vultures immediately circled. To pay his mounting court costs and legal judgments, Norton was forced to default on his real-estate mortgages held by the banking house of Lucas, Turner & Co., whose San Francisco branch manager—a stern former West Point officer named William Tecumseh Sherman—foreclosed mercilessly on Norton’s North Beach and Jackson Square properties. In August 1856, Joshua Norton filed for insolvency in the courts, listing debts of more than fifty-six thousand dollars against zero liquid assets. Evicted from his offices and shunned by the fair-weather speculators who had once toasted him over champagne, Norton vanished into the working-class alleys of the waterfront, renting a windowless, nine-by-six-foot cubicle for fifty cents a night at the Eureka Lodgings boardinghouse at 624 Commercial Street.

For three lonely years, between 1856 and 1859, the broken merchant walked the fog-shrouded boardwalks of San Francisco, reading every newspaper in the Mechanics’ Institute reading room as the United States spiraled toward civil war over slavery, the Dred Scott decision, and corruption in Washington and Sacramento. Somewhere inside that cramped boardinghouse room on Commercial Street, Norton underwent a profound psychological metamorphosis. If a republic torn apart by venal politicians and ruthless speculators had failed him and failed the nation, then only a benevolent, incorruptible monarch—an enlightened philosopher-emperor above party and greed—could restore justice. When he walked into the *Evening Bulletin* office on September 17, 1859, and subsequent proclamations dissolved Congress and added *Protector of Mexico* to his title after Napoleon III invaded Veracruz, Joshua Norton had left his bankruptcy behind forever.

What happened next says as much about the soul of nineteenth-century San Francisco as it does about Joshua Norton. In London, New York, or Boston, a destitute forty-one-year-old man wandering the financial district in a homemade military uniform and ordering the dissolution of the United States Congress would have been clapped into a straitjacket and locked inside a lunatic asylum within a week. San Francisco—a city where virtually every citizen had known the whiplash of sudden wealth and overnight ruin—looked at Emperor Norton, recognized its own reflection, and bowed. When his clothes grew threadbare, the U.S. Army officers stationed at the Presidio presented him with a cast-off navy-blue cavalry coat adorned with tarnished gold-braided epaulets and brass buttons, which he paired with a tall beaver top hat encircled by a green silk rosette and a sweeping ostrich plume, a ceremonial brass-hilted saber at his hip, a fresh carnation in his buttonhole, and heavy boots with neat slits cut into the leather sides to relieve his aching corns.

Throughout the 1860s, as he patrolled the wooden sidewalks of Montgomery, Clay, and Kearny streets with a heavy knotted hickory cane, the Emperor was constantly seen in the company of San Francisco’s two most famous street dogs: *Bummer* and *Lazarus*. Bummer, a stocky black-and-white Newfoundland cross who haunted the free-lunch saloons along Montgomery Street, had famously rescued a mangy, half-dead yellow cur from the jaws of a larger dog, bringing him scraps of meat and nursing him back to health until townspeople named the survivor Lazarus. Though technically independent stray dogs of the saloon district, Bummer and Lazarus trailed Emperor Norton on his daily rounds so faithfully that printmakers sketched the trio together in popular lithographs, and when Lazarus died in October 1863—followed by Bummer in November 1865—a young San Francisco journalist named Samuel Clemens (Mark Twain) penned celebrated front-page obituaries mourning their passing.

Only once did anyone dare challenge the Emperor’s liberty, and the city’s reaction sealed his untouchable status forever. On the afternoon of January 21, 1867, a newly hired private Auxiliary policeman named Armand Barbier arrested Emperor Norton on Kearny Street on a charge of 'lunacy' and locked him inside a cold holding cell at the City Hall station so he could be committed to the state asylum at Stockton. The next morning, San Francisco’s newspapers exploded in unanimous civic fury. In a scorching editorial that still rings down the decades, the *Daily Alta California* thundered: *'Since he has worn the imperial purple, he has shed no blood, robbed no one, and despoiled no country; which is more than can be said of any of his fellows in that line.'* Embarrassed Police Chief Patrick Crowley rushed to the jail himself, unlocked the cell door, offered a formal state apology to His Majesty, and issued a standing order to the San Francisco Police Department: henceforth, every officer on the beat was to render a crisp military salute whenever Emperor Norton walked past.

To finance his twenty-one-year reign without levying oppressive taxes on his subjects, Norton established his own Imperial Treasury. Printed free of charge by his affectionate friends at the job-printing shop of Cuddy & Hughes at 511 Clay Street (and later by C. A. Murdock & Co.), handsomely engraved *Imperial Treasury Promissory Notes* in denominations of fifty cents, two dollars, and five dollars bore a woodcut portrait of Norton I alongside the royal seal of California and promised repayment in the year 1880 with interest at seven percent per annum—a rate the fiscally conscientious Emperor later lowered to five and then four percent so as not to overburden the imperial exchequer. Across San Francisco, saloonkeepers, booksellers, ferry captains, and restaurant waiters accepted Norton’s fifty-cent bonds without hesitation; when he entered the California Theatre on Bush Street on opening night, the entire audience and symphony orchestra rose to their feet and bowed until the Emperor took his complimentary front-row armchair.

His daily schedule was as rigorous as any head of state’s. Rising early in his tiny room at the Eureka Lodgings—where his fifty-cent nightly tariff was quietly guaranteed behind the scenes by his fellow Freemasons of Occidental Lodge No. 22 and wealthy old acquaintances at the Pacific Club—Norton inspected the condition of the city’s cobblestone streets, cast-iron storm drains, and newly laid cable-car slots. At noon he dined royally at the free-lunch buffets of Barry & Patten’s saloon or Martin & Horton’s on Montgomery Street; in the afternoons he studied international dispatches and played fierce games of chess at the Mechanics’ Institute Library on Post Street. A devout universalist who believed that a true monarch belonged to every faith in his realm, he attended Jewish Sabbath services on Saturdays and occupied a front pew at Old St. Mary’s Catholic Cathedral or Trinity Episcopal Church on Sundays.

Beneath the pageantry, many of Emperor Norton’s hundreds of handwritten *Imperial Proclamations*—published faithfully in the black-owned abolitionist weekly *The Pacific Appeal* as well as the mainstream dailies—displayed a moral courage and political foresight that shamed the elected officials of the Gilded Age. In an era of virulent anti-Chinese demagoguery, he issued decrees commanding that Chinese immigrants be permitted to give legal testimony in California courts against white citizens and ordering the police to suppress anti-Chinese rioters. During one particularly tense evening in the late 1870s, when a whipped-up crowd of Denis Kearney’s Workingmen’s Party agitators gathered on Kearny Street threatening to march into Chinatown with torches, Emperor Norton walked calmly into the narrow space between the angry mob and the Chinese storefronts, took off his ostrich-plumed hat, closed his eyes, and recited the Lord’s Prayer in a quiet, resonant voice over and over until the shamed rioters lowered their clubs and dispersed into the fog.

Even more astonishing were his visionary engineering decrees of 1872. At a time when practical engineers dismissed the idea of spanning San Francisco Bay as an impossible fantasy, Emperor Norton issued three separate proclamations in *The Pacific Appeal*—on January 6, March 23, and August 12, 1872—*commanding* that a suspension bridge be constructed forthwith from Oakland Point across Goat Island (modern Yerba Buena Island) to Telegraph Hill in San Francisco, followed by a september decree ordering a transbay railroad tunnel beneath the water. Sixty-four years later, in November 1936, the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge opened along the exact island-hopping trajectory Emperor Norton had mapped out on paper, followed thirty-eight years after that by the underwater BART Transbay Tube.

The reign of Norton I came to a sudden, dramatic end on the stormy evening of Thursday, January 8, 1880. Shortly after eight o’clock, braving a driving winter downpour, the sixty-one-year-old Emperor was climbing the steep, rain-slicked cobblestones of California Street just below Dupont Street (now Grant Avenue), directly opposite the red-brick gothic tower of Old St. Mary’s Cathedral, on his way to attend the weekly scientific debate at the California Academy of Sciences. Partway up the hill, he faltered beneath his umbrella, sank onto the wet planks of the sidewalk, and lost consciousness. A passing policeman standing under a streetlamp rushed to his side and summoned a horse-drawn carriage to rush him to the City Receiving Hospital, but before the wheels reached Kearny Street, Joshua Abraham Norton had died peacefully of apoplexy.

When hospital attendants emptied the pockets of his threadbare blue cavalry coat, they found no hidden fortune: only five dollars and change in silver coins, a single British gold sovereign, a two-franc French note, a bundle of unissued fifty-cent Imperial bonds, and a worn packet of telegrams fabricated over the years by affectionate local newspaper reporters—one purporting to be from Tsar Alexander II of Russia congratulating Norton on his impending marriage to Queen Victoria, and another signed by President Ulysses S. Grant urging him to complete the match in the interest of world peace. The next morning, January 9, 1880, the *San Francisco Chronicle* ran a three-word French headline that brought tears to thousands of eyes across the city: **'Le Roi Est Mort'**—*The King Is Dead*. Refusing to let their beloved monarch be buried in a pauper’s pine box, the millionaires of the Pacific Club raised a handsome purse for a velvet-lined rosewood casket, and on Sunday, January 11, 1880, as a total solar eclipse darkened the sky over northern California, a two-mile-long cortege of more than ten thousand mourners—from barefoot Chinese laborers and newsboys to railroad barons—followed *Norton I, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico*, to his resting place.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Why San Francisco Crowned a Bankrupt Forty-Niner

Historians of the American West point out that Emperor Norton could only have existed in post-Gold Rush San Francisco because almost every citizen of the city in the 1850s and 1860s had experienced sudden fortune followed by catastrophic overnight ruin—whether in the fires of 1851, the collapse of placer gold mining, or the Comstock silver panics. Looking at Joshua Norton in his frayed blue coat and ostrich-plumed beaver hat, San Franciscans saw a living saint of resilience: a man who had lost everything on a single shipment of Peruvian rice and responded by promoting himself to Emperor of the United States.

2. Literary Immortality: Mark Twain, Robert Louis Stevenson, and Neil Gaiman

Two of the greatest writers of the nineteenth century lived in San Francisco during Norton's reign and immortalized him in fiction. Mark Twain, who worked as a reporter at the Morning Call in the same building where Norton had once kept his real-estate office, modeled the 'King' in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn directly on Emperor Norton. Robert Louis Stevenson made Norton a central character in his 1892 novel The Wrecker, marveling askance: 'In what other city would a harmless madman have been so tenderly humored, his bills paid, his very cheques cashed?' A century later, Neil Gaiman devoted one of the most celebrated issues of The Sandman ('Three Septembers and a January') to Norton's dignity.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three icons of Norton's San Francisco in mind: first, the 1872 Bay Bridge Proclamations, which envisioned the Oakland–Yerba Buena Island–San Francisco bridge route sixty-four years before it opened; second, Bummer and Lazarus, the two celebrated rat-terrier stray dogs of Montgomery Street often associated with Norton in local lore and mourned with front-page eulogies by Mark Twain when they died in the 1860s; and third, the Imperial Treasury Notes, surviving examples of which now sell at numismatic auctions for over $10,000 apiece. Exploring these historical layers side by side with classic world fiction deepens the Bradbury nightly habit, turning concrete dates, local vocabulary, and living neighborhoods into permanent mental landmarks that enrich how we read both cities and human history.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Begin at 624–650 Commercial Street (between Montgomery and Kearny, 37.7942° N, 122.4048° E) beside the Empire Park Redwood pocket park, where Norton lived for seventeen years in a nine-by-six-foot room at the Eureka Lodgings. Walk up California Street to the corner of Grant Avenue beneath Old St. Mary's Cathedral—the exact spot where Norton collapsed in the rain in 1880—and visit the Transbay Transit Center plaque honoring his 1872 Bay Bridge decree, or stop by Comstock Saloon on Columbus Avenue where an original portrait and artifacts of the Emperor watch over the bar.
