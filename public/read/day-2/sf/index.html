Emperor Norton I: How a Bankrupted Rice Merchant Became San Francisco's Enlightened Monarch

San Francisco — The true story of Joshua Abraham Norton (1818–1880), who issued his own currency, halted anti-Chinese riots with a prayer, and decreed the Bay Bridge sixty years early (Post-Gold Rush Gilded Age (1859–1880)) • 10 min read

“At the peremptory request and desire of a large majority of the citizens of these United States, I, Joshua Norton, formerly of Algoa Bay, Cape of Good Hope, declare and proclaim myself Emperor of these United States.” — San Francisco Daily Evening Bulletin, September 17, 1859

Late on the afternoon of September 17, 1859, a stocky, bearded forty-one-year-old gentleman walked into the editorial office of the *San Francisco Daily Evening Bulletin* at Montgomery and Merchant streets and handed editor George Fitch a single sheet of paper. Rather than tossing it into the wastebasket, Fitch printed it verbatim in that evening’s edition under the headline 'Have We an Emperor Among Us?' The notice declared that Joshua Abraham Norton—having witnessed the corruption, sectional strife, and fraud tearing the American republic apart on the eve of the Civil War—had graciously acceded to the wishes of the citizenry and assumed the throne as *Norton I, Emperor of the United States* (to which he soon added *Protector of Mexico* when Napoleon III invaded Veracruz).

Ten years earlier, Joshua Norton had been one of the sharpest, wealthiest commodity brokers in Gold Rush San Francisco. Born in England in 1818 and raised in South Africa’s Algoa Bay by English-Jewish parents, Norton sailed into Yerba Buena Cove in November 1849 with $40,000 in inherited capital. Buying corner lots at Montgomery and Jackson streets, building a rice mill, and operating a cigar factory, he amassed a fortune of $250,000—equivalent to nearly $9 million today—dining at the city’s finest French restaurants and trading jokes with bankers and judges.

Then, in December 1852, famine struck China and banned rice exports, causing the price of rice in San Francisco to skyrocket from four cents to thirty-six cents a pound. Tempted by the chance to corner the entire Pacific Coast market, Norton paid $25,000 down for the entire 200,000-pound Peruvian rice cargo of the ship *Glyde* at twelve-and-a-half cents a pound. Days later, an entire flotilla of delayed ships loaded to the gunwales with cheap Peruvian rice sailed through the Golden Gate. The market collapsed overnight. After three years of brutal lawsuits before the California Supreme Court, foreclosures by the Lucas, Turner & Co. bank (managed by future Union General William Tecumseh Sherman), and bankruptcy in 1856, Norton vanished into a cheap fifty-cent-a-night boardinghouse at 624 Commercial Street.

When he emerged in 1859 as Emperor Norton I, San Francisco did something that no other major nineteenth-century city on Earth would have done: instead of locking him in an asylum, the entire city saluted and played along. Dressed in a worn blue Union Army officer’s coat with tarnished brass buttons and gold-braided epaulets bestowed by the soldiers of the Presidio, a beaver top hat adorned with a green cockade and ostrich plume, a brass-mounted saber at his hip, and boots with slits cut in the sides to relieve his corns, Emperor Norton patrolled his capital daily accompanied by two stray dogs—*Bummer* and *Lazarus*—calculating the state of the pavements, inspecting cable-car construction, and attending every session of the California Legislature.

When a rookie private security guard named Armand Barbier arrested Norton for 'lunacy' in January 1867 so he could be committed, San Francisco’s newspapers erupted in unanimous civic outrage. The *Daily Alta California* thundered that Norton had 'shed no blood; robbed no one; and despoiled no country, which is more than can be said of any of his fellows in that line.' Police Chief Patrick Crowley personally ordered Norton’s immediate release with a formal apology, and from that day forward every San Francisco police officer on the beat saluted the Emperor as he passed.

Consider how Norton funded his twenty-one-year reign. Printed gratis by his friends at the job-printing shop of Cuddy & Hughes on Clay Street, Imperial Treasury Certificates in denominations of fifty cents, two dollars, and five dollars promised redemption in the year 1880 with interest at four to seven percent, signed 'Norton I, Emperor.' These notes were universally accepted as legal tender at taverns, restaurants, ferries, and theaters across San Francisco; when Norton entered a theater on opening night, the entire orchestra pit and audience rose to their feet and bowed until the Emperor took his complimentary front-row seat.

Far from mere eccentric nonsense, many of Emperor Norton’s hundreds of Imperial Proclamations proved uncannily prophetic and morally courageous. During the vicious anti-Chinese riots of the 1870s sparked by demagogue Denis Kearney’s Workingmen’s Party, Norton stepped squarely between a racist mob and Chinese residents on the street, bowed his head, and recited the Lord’s Prayer repeatedly in a calm, booming voice until the ashamed rioters dispersed without throwing a stone. In another proclamation, he ordered that Chinese residents be allowed to testify in California courts against white citizens; in others, he decreed the formation of a global League of Nations and fined anyone five dollars who dared utter the vulgar word 'Frisco.'

Most famously of all, across three separate proclamations written in January, March, and August of 1872, Emperor Norton *commanded* that a suspension bridge be constructed immediately from Oakland Point across Goat Island (now Yerba Buena Island) to Telegraph Hill in San Francisco—specifying the exact island-hopping trajectory that engineers finally used six decades later when they opened the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge in November 1936.

On the rainy evening of January 8, 1880, while walking up California Street toward Grant Avenue (then Dupont Street) on his way to a scientific debate at the California Academy of Sciences, Emperor Norton collapsed on the wet cobblestones and died of apoplexy before the hospital carriage arrived. In his pockets police found five dollars in small change, a gold sovereign, and telegrams he had received (forged affectionately by local journalists) congratulating him on his engagement to Queen Victoria and praising his friendship with Tsar Alexander II. The next morning, the *San Francisco Chronicle* printed a headline that still stops the heart: **'Le Roi Est Mort.'** Bought a rosewood coffin by the Pacific Club of millionaires he had once dined with, Joshua Abraham Norton was followed to his grave at the Masonic Cemetery by a two-mile-long funeral cortege of over 10,000 mourners—from bootblacks and Chinese laborers to railroad barons—just as a total solar eclipse darkened the sky over northern California.

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Why San Francisco Crowned a Bankrupt Forty-Niner

Historians of the American West point out that Emperor Norton could only have existed in post-Gold Rush San Francisco because almost every citizen of the city in the 1850s and 1860s had experienced sudden fortune followed by catastrophic overnight ruin—whether in the fires of 1851, the collapse of placer gold mining, or the Comstock silver panics. Looking at Joshua Norton in his frayed blue coat and ostrich-plumed beaver hat, San Franciscans saw a living saint of resilience: a man who had lost everything on a single shipment of Peruvian rice and responded by promoting himself to Emperor of the United States.

2. Literary Immortality: Mark Twain, Robert Louis Stevenson, and Neil Gaiman

Two of the greatest writers of the nineteenth century lived in San Francisco during Norton's reign and immortalized him in fiction. Mark Twain, who worked as a reporter at the Morning Call in the same building where Norton had once kept his real-estate office, modeled the 'King' in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn directly on Emperor Norton. Robert Louis Stevenson made Norton a central character in his 1892 novel The Wrecker, marveling askance: 'In what other city would a harmless madman have been so tenderly humored, his bills paid, his very cheques cashed?' A century later, Neil Gaiman devoted one of the most celebrated issues of The Sandman ('Three Septembers and a January') to Norton's dignity.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three icons of Norton's San Francisco in mind: first, the 1872 Bay Bridge Proclamations, which envisioned the Oakland–Yerba Buena Island–San Francisco bridge route sixty-four years before it opened; second, Bummer and Lazarus, the two celebrated rat-terrier stray dogs of Montgomery Street often associated with Norton in local lore and mourned with front-page eulogies by Mark Twain when they died in the 1860s; and third, the Imperial Treasury Notes, surviving examples of which now sell at numismatic auctions for over $10,000 apiece.
