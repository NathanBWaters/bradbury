Nightly Essay • Transcendentalist Philosophical Essay

Nature

Ralph Waldo Emerson (1836) • 9 min read

Our age is retrospective; it builds the sepulchres of the fathers—Emerson's revolutionary call to step out under the stars and behold the universe firsthand.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Ralph Waldo Emerson's "Nature" is the founding text of American Transcendentalism, a movement that believed people can know truth directly, through intuition and experience, rather than through inherited authority. It appeared anonymously in Boston in 1836, a short book written by a man in his early thirties who had resigned from the Unitarian ministry and lost his first wife, his brother, and his old sense of purpose. After a trip to Europe, where he met Wordsworth, Coleridge and Carlyle, he settled in Concord, Massachusetts, and began to ask what a truly American thought might look like. What you are about to hear is the book's opening, the declaration of independence and the first chapter. It asks why every generation should live on secondhand religion, secondhand poetry and secondhand wisdom when the sun still shines and the woods are still there. Emerson's answer is that nature, truly seen, restores us to ourselves, and that this renewal is available to anyone who learns to look.

Our age is retrospective. It builds the sepulchres of the fathers. It writes biographies, histories, and criticism. The foregoing generations beheld God and nature face to face; we, through their eyes. Why should not we also enjoy an original relation to the universe? Why should not we have a poetry and philosophy of insight and not of tradition, and a religion by revelation to us, and not the history of theirs? Embosomed for a season in nature, whose floods of life stream around and through us, and invite us by the powers they supply, to action proportioned to nature, why should we grope among the dry bones of the past, or put the living generation into masquerade out of its faded wardrobe? The sun shines to-day also. There is more wool and flax in the fields. There are new lands, new men, new thoughts. Let us demand our own works and laws and worship.

To go into solitude, a man needs to retire as much from his chamber as from society. I am not solitary whilst I read and write, though nobody is with me. But if a man would be alone, let him look at the stars. The rays that come from those heavenly worlds, will separate between him and what he touches. One might think the atmosphere was made transparent with this design, to give man, in the heavenly bodies, the perpetual presence of the sublime. Seen in the streets of cities, how great they are! If the stars should appear one night in a thousand years, how would men believe and adore; and preserve for many generations the remembrance of the city of God which had been shown! But every night come out these envoys of beauty, and light the universe with their admonishing smile.

The stars awaken a certain reverence, because though always present, they are inaccessible; but all natural objects make a kindred impression, when the mind is open to their influence. Nature never wears a mean appearance. Neither does the wisest man extort her secret, and lose his curiosity by finding out all her perfection. Nature never became a toy to a wise spirit. The flowers, the animals, the mountains, reflected the wisdom of his best hour, as much as they had delighted the simplicity of his childhood.

When we speak of nature in this manner, we have a distinct but most poetical sense in the mind. We mean the integrity of impression made by manifold natural objects. It is this which distinguishes the stick of timber of the wood-cutter, from the tree of the poet. The charming landscape which I saw this morning, is indubitably made up of some twenty or thirty farms. Miller owns this field, Locke that, and Manning the woodland beyond. But none of them owns the landscape. There is a property in the horizon which no man has but he whose eye can integrate all the parts, that is, the poet. This is the best part of these men’s farms, yet to this their warranty-deeds give no title.

To speak truly, few adult persons can see nature. Most persons do not see the sun. At least they have a very superficial seeing. The sun illuminates only the eye of the man, but shines into the eye and the heart of the child. The lover of nature is he whose inward and outward senses are still truly adjusted to each other; who has retained the spirit of infancy even into the era of manhood. His intercourse with heaven and earth, becomes part of his daily food. In the presence of nature, a wild delight runs through the man, in spite of real sorrows. Nature says,—he is my creature, and maugre all his impertinent griefs, he shall be glad with me.

Not the sun or the summer alone, but every hour and season yields its tribute of delight; for every hour and change corresponds to and authorizes a different state of the mind, from breathless noon to grimmest midnight. Nature is a setting that fits equally well a comic or a mourning piece. In good health, the air is a cordial of incredible virtue. Crossing a bare common, in snow puddles, at twilight, under a clouded sky, without having in my thoughts any occurrence of special good fortune, I have enjoyed a perfect exhilaration. I am glad to the brink of fear. In the woods too, a man casts off his years, as the snake his slough, and at what period soever of life, is always a child. In the woods, is perpetual youth. Within these plantations of God, a decorum and sanctity reign, a perennial festival is dressed, and the guest sees not how he should tire of them in a thousand years.

In the woods, we return to reason and faith. There I feel that nothing can befal me in life,—no disgrace, no calamity, (leaving me my eyes,) which nature cannot repair. Standing on the bare ground,—my head bathed by the blithe air, and uplifted into infinite space,—all mean egotism vanishes. I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all; the currents of the Universal Being circulate through me; I am part or parcel of God. The name of the nearest friend sounds then foreign and accidental: to be brothers, to be acquaintances,—master or servant, is then a trifle and a disturbance. I am the lover of uncontained and immortal beauty. In the wilderness, I find something more dear and connate than in streets or villages. In the tranquil landscape, and especially in the distant line of the horizon, man beholds somewhat as beautiful as his own nature.

The greatest delight which the fields and woods minister, is the suggestion of an occult relation between man and the vegetable. I am not alone and unacknowledged. They nod to me, and I to them. The waving of the boughs in the storm, is new to me and old. It takes me by surprise, and yet is not unknown. Its effect is like that of a higher thought or a better emotion coming over me, when I deemed I was thinking justly or doing right.

Yet it is certain that the power to produce this delight, does not reside in nature, but in man, or in a harmony of both. It is necessary to use these pleasures with great temperance. For, nature is not always tricked in holiday attire, but the same scene which yesterday breathed perfume and glittered as for the frolic of the nymphs, is overspread with melancholy today. Nature always wears the colors of the spirit. To a man laboring under calamity, the heat of his own fire hath sadness in it. Then, there is a kind of contempt of the landscape felt by him who has just lost by death a dear friend. The sky is less grand as it shuts down over less worth in the population.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The piece you have just heard is really two things welded together: a manifesto and a meditation. The manifesto comes first. "Our age is retrospective. It builds the sepulchres of the fathers." Emerson is saying that his generation spends its energy on biographies, histories and criticism, looking at God and nature through other people's eyes. His question is simple and radical: "Why should not we also enjoy an original relation to the universe?" Notice the phrase "dry bones of the past" and the image of putting the living generation into "masquerade out of its faded wardrobe." The past, in this picture, is a costume closet. You can dress in it, but you are then an actor, not yourself. The paragraph ends in a rally: "The sun shines to-day also."

The meditation begins with solitude. To be alone, he says, you must leave your room as well as other people, because even reading and writing keep company. So "let him look at the stars." Then the great thought experiment: if the stars appeared only one night in a thousand years, people would adore them and remember the vision for generations. But because they come out every night, we hardly look up. The point is about habit, the way constant gifts become invisible. The stars here are "envoys of beauty" with an "admonishing smile," a gentle rebuke to our inattention.

Next he distinguishes between the ordinary and the poetic way of seeing. The woodcutter sees a stick of timber, the poet sees a tree. And in his small parable of the landscape, several farmers own the fields, but none owns the view. "There is a property in the horizon which no man has but he whose eye can integrate all the parts." That "integration" is key. Real seeing is not counting objects but taking in the whole, and it is a kind of ownership no deed can record. "Few adult persons can see nature," he says, because the sun shines only on the eye of the adult but "into the eye and the heart of the child."

Then comes the most famous moment, usually remembered as "the transparent eye-ball." Standing on bare ground, with his head "bathed by the blithe air," his "mean egotism vanishes." "I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all; the currents of the Universal Being circulate through me." It sounds odd, and it was mocked for it, but he means something fairly precise: the ordinary self, with its worries about reputation, shrinks away, and what is left is pure perception, a feeling of belonging to something larger.

The last paragraph is the one many readers miss. Emerson pulls back and admits that the delight "does not reside in nature, but in man, or in a harmony of both." Nature is not always cheerful. To someone who has just lost a friend, "the sky is less grand." That sentence comes from a man who knew grief intimately, and it quietly corrects the sunny tone of everything before it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The strongest quality of this passage is its sentences. Emerson writes in short, strong, quotable units, and the first paragraph alone contains several that have lasted: the sepulchres of the fathers, the dry bones of the past, the masquerade of the faded wardrobe. The rhythm of the opening questions, each beginning "Why should not we..." builds like a drumbeat, and the turn to plain fact, "There is more wool and flax in the fields," is a masterstroke. After lofty abstractions, he reminds us of wool and flax. It is the voice of a preacher who has left the pulpit but kept the music.

The stars passage is probably the best-built thought in the whole excerpt. It uses a hypothetical, makes a surprising reversal, and lands on an image, the "admonishing smile," that is both beautiful and slightly stern. The parable of the farms and the horizon is similarly efficient and a little witty: the owners Miller, Locke and Manning are named like characters in a miniature fable. And the closing turn to grief, where he lets the weather of the soul override the weather of the sky, gives the piece a depth that pure nature-worship lacks.

The weaknesses are real, though. Emerson does not argue so much as assert. He moves from claim to claim by association, and the connection between them often rests on the reader's willingness to be carried along. The "transparent eye-ball" passage is the clearest example. It is thrilling when you are in sympathy with it, and faintly ridiculous when you are not. Even the author seems to have known this; the image became a favorite target for caricature almost at once. A reader who wants to know how one gets from the woods to the "Universal Being" will find an experience described rather than a path laid out.

There is also a certain privilege in the vision. The solitary walker with a free afternoon and a head full of Latin and Plato does not have much to say to someone working a field, or to anyone for whom nature is a source of labor and danger, not of epiphany. Emerson's farmers do not own the landscape, but they also do not seem to see it, and the essay is a little condescending about them. The line that "most persons do not see the sun" is flattering to the person who thinks they do.

Finally, the prose can be gaseous. Phrases like "integrity of impression" and "inward and outward senses... truly adjusted" gesture toward ideas that are never defined. This is the price of the style: the aphorism lights up and then leaves you to work out its meaning. Many readers find this exhilarating. Others finish a page of Emerson feeling uplifted and unable to say what they have been told. Both reactions are fair, and the essay is more honest about the second than its reputation suggests, since it ends by admitting that nature can fail us when we are sad.

III. The Impact It Had

The immediate reception of "Nature" was modest. It was published without Emerson's name in September 1836, in a small printing, and it took years to sell out. But among a small group of readers in and around Boston and Concord it landed like a flare. Within weeks of its appearance, the group that came to be called the Transcendental Club was gathering, and the book gave that circle a vocabulary and a confidence. Margaret Fuller, Bronson Alcott, and the younger Henry David Thoreau were all within its orbit.

Emerson's own career grew directly from it. Two years later he gave the Divinity School Address at Harvard, which scandalized the Unitarian establishment by urging young ministers to trust their own intuition over church doctrine, and which got him effectively barred from speaking at Harvard for decades. "The American Scholar," delivered in 1837 and often called America's intellectual declaration of independence, picks up the opening cry of "Nature" and widens it. "Self-Reliance," from 1841, carries the same idea into private conduct. Everything he wrote afterward can be read as an expansion of the first paragraph you heard.

The influence on other writers is hard to overstate. Thoreau went to live at Walden Pond on land Emerson owned, and Walden is in many ways an extended experiment in the proposition that one can have an original relation to the universe. Walt Whitman said that Emerson's essays had brought him to a boil, and the first edition of Leaves of Grass drew a famous letter of praise from Emerson in return. Emily Dickinson, though she rarely left her home, absorbed his ideas. Later, the naturalist John Muir admired Emerson, who visited Yosemite in 1871, and the American conservation movement inherits some of its language of wilderness as spiritual renewal from this tradition. Overseas, readers such as Nietzsche found a kindred voice in his hymns to self-overcoming.

In wider culture, the book helped define an American attitude: suspicion of inherited authority, trust in direct experience, belief that landscape can heal. You can hear it in the nature writing of the twentieth century, in the outdoors movement, in the spiritual-but-not-religious outlook that so many people now share, and in countless graduation speeches. When someone says they feel closer to God in the woods than in church, they are echoing the "wilderness" passage you just heard.

In classrooms, "Nature" is a standard text in American literature courses, usually taught as the founding document of Transcendentalism and often read alongside "Self-Reliance." It has also been used by scholars of environmental history, who see in it the beginnings of both American ecological thought and its tendency to treat wilderness as a place for human self-discovery.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved most is the sense of permission. Emerson's opening says, in effect, that you do not need a degree, a priesthood, or a tradition's blessing to stand in front of the world and feel its meaning. For a young country in the 1830s that was intoxicating, and for an individual reader at any date it is still a relief. People tend to remember where they were when they first encountered "an original relation to the universe." It sounds like an invitation, and it is meant to be one.

Another source of affection is the sheer beauty of particular images. The stars that come out every night and light the universe with their "admonishing smile" has lodged in many memories, as has the idea that one can walk past a field owned by three men and own the best part of it by looking. Readers who like the essay often say it taught them to look up, literally. It is rare for a philosophical text to change the way a person spends five minutes on a walk, but this one does so reliably.

Writers and thinkers have loved its confidence. Thoreau, Whitman and many others took from Emerson the conviction that an American voice was possible. Whitman's remark that Emerson started him boiling is the best-known tribute, but the line of admirers runs through the whole nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Readers who find themselves stuck in rules and expectations often discover in this passage a kind of second wind.

The "transparent eye-ball" passage has its own devotees. Those who have ever stood in a forest, on a mountain or beside the sea and felt their ordinary worries thin out will recognize what he is describing. For them, the oddness of the phrase is a sign of honesty: he was trying to put into words a state that does not fit ordinary language. Even skeptics often admit that he captured something genuinely felt.

Finally, there is a loyalty to the essay's emotional honesty at the end. For all his serenity, Emerson allows the plain fact that delight is not guaranteed, that grief can dim the sky. Readers who come to the piece in hard times often say that this last paragraph is what keeps the optimism from feeling cheap. The passage promises that nature "can repair" any disgrace or calamity, and then admits that this depends on the state of the person looking. That mixture of hope and realism is what lets people go on returning to it long after they have outgrown the youthful thrill of the opening.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

From the start, "Nature" drew mockery as well as admiration. The most famous target was the "transparent eye-ball." Within a few years the image was being parodied, and the painter and Transcendentalist Christopher Cranch drew a well-known cartoon of a giant walking eyeball on legs. Critics outside the movement used the phrase as shorthand for everything they considered fuzzy about the group. Edgar Allan Poe, for one, was openly scornful of the Transcendentalists, and Nathaniel Hawthorne, who knew many of them, treated their utopian enthusiasms with gentle skepticism in his fiction.

More seriously, religious conservatives objected to what the book implied. If God can be met directly in the woods, what is the church for? If each person has "revelation to us, and not the history of theirs," what becomes of scripture? The same alarm greeted Emerson's Divinity School Address two years later, and it is part of why some of the Unitarian establishment treated him as a danger. The charge, essentially, was pantheism: that he had blurred God into nature and the self.

Philosophers and scientists have had different complaints. Some say his thinking is not rigorous: it is poetry dressed as argument, moving by flashes of insight with no demonstration. Others note that his picture of nature is highly selective. The nature of this passage is serene, nourishing and always at the service of the human mind, with little room for storms, predators, disease or indifference. Later readers, especially after Darwin, found this view of a benevolent, mind-friendly natural world hard to sustain. Even within the excerpt, a careful reader will notice that Emerson's claim that "nature never wears a mean appearance" is tested by his own admission at the end.

There are also social and political criticisms. Modern readers have asked who gets to stand alone in the woods with a bare head and a clear mind. Emerson wrote as a comfortable New England man at a time when millions of enslaved people could not walk anywhere freely, and critics have pointed out that his early work, with its focus on private spiritual independence, says little about the structural injustices around him. To be fair, Emerson became a significant antislavery voice in the following decades, but the silence of this early text is noticeable. Likewise, the celebration of landscape as an emptiness for the self to fill has been questioned by historians who note that the "wilderness" was already inhabited, and that the cult of it often overlooked the people who lived there.

Last, some have simply found the idealism tiresome: the claim that "the power to produce this delight does not reside in nature, but in man" can read as a retreat into the mind, a way of making the world a mirror. For readers who prefer the world to be stubbornly itself, that is a flaw, not a feature.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Emerson's "Nature" belongs to the great tradition of Romanticism, the movement that swept Europe and America around 1800 to 1850 and insisted on feeling, imagination and the individual against the cool rationalism of the Enlightenment. Its direct ancestors are the English Romantics Wordsworth and Coleridge, whom Emerson visited, and the German idealists who shaped Coleridge's thinking, along with Kant, whose notion that the mind actively shapes what it perceives gave Transcendentalism its name. There is also an older strand of Neoplatonism and of Swedenborg, the Swedish mystic whom Emerson admired, running through the book's belief that natural things are signs of spiritual ones.

What descends from it is enormous. In American literature, the line runs from Thoreau and Whitman through Dickinson, and into the modern traditions of nature writing, from John Muir to Annie Dillard and beyond. The idea that wild places are a source of spiritual health underlies the national parks and the conservation movement. In philosophy, Emerson fed into American pragmatism: William James, the author of the essay on habit, was his godson, and his view that truth is something lived and tested has Emersonian roots. In Europe, Nietzsche kept a copy of Emerson close at hand.

It is useful to compare "Nature" with other pieces on reading and attention. Bacon's "Of Studies" says wisdom is won by observation and tempered by experience, and Emerson takes that proposition and pushes it into a vision, trusting the individual much more than Bacon would. Montaigne also withdrew to solitude, but he found the mind a noisy place that needed discipline; Emerson finds in solitude something like grace. Thoreau, who learned from Emerson, did the experiment that the older man proposed. Woolf's call for readers to trust their own judgment in the library is, in a sense, Emerson's call in a different room.

One thing to remember is that this is only the opening of a larger book, in which Emerson goes on to discuss commodity, beauty, language, discipline, idealism and spirit. What you have here is the doorway, and its job is to unsettle the reader's habits of looking.

Why read it now? Because the problem it names, living through other people's eyes, has become sharper. We experience much of life through screens, reviews and summaries, in an age more retrospective than he could have imagined. His invitation to stand outside, look up, and see the stars as if they appeared once in a thousand years may be the most practical advice in the essay. It costs nothing, takes five minutes, and, as Emerson promises, it does not wear out.
