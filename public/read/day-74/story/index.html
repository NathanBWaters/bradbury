Nightly Short Story • Modernist Fiction

The Killers

Ernest Hemingway (1927) • 15 min read

“The door of Henry’s lunchroom opened and two men came in. They sat down at the counter.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Killers, published in 1927 and collected in Ernest Hemingway's book Men Without Women, is one of the most famous crime stories ever written, though it contains almost no crime. Two hired gunmen walk into a small-town lunchroom in Illinois, tie up the cook and a teenage boy named Nick Adams, and wait for a retired boxer who never shows up. Hemingway was in his twenties, living in Europe, and by then known as the leader of a new, spare way of writing in which feeling is carried by what people do and say rather than by what the narrator explains. The setting is Prohibition-era America, when gangsters from Chicago were becoming a national legend and a national fear. The story's surprise is that the real subject is not violence but what it does to a young person who sees it up close. Listen for the way the dialogue loops and repeats, and for how the story ends not with a bang but with a boy making a quiet decision.

The door of Henry’s lunchroom opened and two men came in. They sat down at the counter.

“What’s yours?” George asked them.

“I don’t know,” one of the men said. “What do you want to eat, Al?”

“I don’t know,” said Al. “I don’t know what I want to eat.”

Outside it was getting dark. The streetlight came on outside the window. The two men at the counter read the menu. From the other end of the counter Nick Adams watched them. He had been talking to George when they came in.

“I’ll have a roast pork tenderloin with apple sauce and mashed potatoes,” the first man said.

“It isn’t ready yet.”

“What the hell do you put it on the card for?”

“That’s the dinner,” George explained. “You can get that at six o’clock.”

George looked at the clock on the wall behind the counter.

“It’s five o’clock.”

“The clock says twenty minutes past five,” the second man said.

“It’s twenty minutes fast.”

“Oh, to hell with the clock,” the first man said. “What have you got to eat?”

“I can give you any kind of sandwiches,” George said. “You can have ham and eggs, bacon and eggs, liver and bacon, or a steak.”

“Give me chicken croquettes with green peas and cream sauce and mashed potatoes.”

“That’s the dinner.”

“Everything we want’s the dinner, eh? That’s the way you work it.”

“I can give you ham and eggs, bacon and eggs, liver⁠—”

“I’ll take ham and eggs,” the man called Al said. He wore a derby hat and a black overcoat buttoned across the chest. His face was small and white and he had tight lips. He wore a silk muffler and gloves.

“Give me bacon and eggs,” said the other man. He was about the same size as Al. Their faces were different, but they were dressed like twins. Both wore overcoats too tight for them. They sat leaning forward, their elbows on the counter.

“Got anything to drink?” Al asked.

“Silver beer, bevo, ginger-ale,” George said.

“I mean you got anything to drink?”

“Just those I said.”

“This is a hot town,” said the other. “What do they call it?”

“Summit.”

“Ever hear of it?” Al asked his friend.

“No,” said the friend.

“What do you do here nights?” Al asked.

“They eat the dinner,” his friend said. “They all come here and eat the big dinner.”

“That’s right,” George said.

“So you think that’s right?” Al asked George.

“Sure.”

“You’re a pretty bright boy, aren’t you?”

“Sure,” said George.

“Well, you’re not,” said the other little man. “Is he, Al?”

“He’s dumb,” said Al. He turned to Nick. “What’s your name?”

“Adams.”

“Another bright boy,” Al said. “Ain’t he a bright boy, Max?”

“The town’s full of bright boys,” Max said.

George put the two platters, one of ham and eggs, the other of bacon and eggs, on the counter. He set down two side-dishes of fried potatoes and closed the wicket into the kitchen.

“Which is yours?” he asked Al.

“Don’t you remember?”

“Ham and eggs.”

“Just a bright boy,” Max said. He leaned forward and took the ham and eggs. Both men ate with their gloves on. George watched them eat.

“What are you looking at?” Max looked at George.

“Nothing.”

“The hell you were. You were looking at me.”

“Maybe the boy meant it for a joke, Max,” Al said.

George laughed.

“You don’t have to laugh,” Max said to him. “You don’t have to laugh at all, see?”

“All right,” said George.

“So he thinks it’s all right.” Max turned to Al. “He thinks it’s all right. That’s a good one.”

“Oh, he’s a thinker,” Al said. They went on eating.

“What’s the bright boy’s name down the counter?” Al asked Max.

“Hey, bright boy,” Max said to Nick. “You go around on the other side of the counter with your boy friend.”

“What’s the idea?” Nick asked.

“There isn’t any idea.”

“You better go around, bright boy,” Al said. Nick went around behind the counter.

“What’s the idea?” George asked.

“None of your damn business,” Al said. “Who’s out in the kitchen?”

“The nigger.”

“What do you mean the nigger?”

“The nigger that cooks.”

“Tell him to come in.”

“What’s the idea?”

“Tell him to come in.”

“Where do you think you are?”

“We know damn well where we are,” the man called Max said. “Do we look silly?”

“You talk silly,” Al said to him. “What the hell do you argue with this kid for? Listen,” he said to George, “tell the nigger to come out here.”

“What are you going to do to him?”

“Nothing. Use your head, bright boy. What would we do to a nigger?”

George opened the slit that opened back into the kitchen. “Sam,” he called. “Come in here a minute.”

The door to the kitchen opened and the nigger came in. “What was it?” he asked. The two men at the counter took a look at him.

“All right, nigger. You stand right there,” Al said.

Sam, the nigger, standing in his apron, looked at the two men sitting at the counter. “Yes, sir,” he said. Al got down from his stool.

“I’m going back to the kitchen with the nigger and bright boy,” he said. “Go on back to the kitchen, nigger. You go with him, bright boy.” The little man walked after Nick and Sam, the cook, back into the kitchen. The door shut after them. The man called Max sat at the counter opposite George. He didn’t look at George but looked in the mirror that ran along back of the counter. Henry’s had been made over from a saloon into a lunch-counter.

“Well, bright boy,” Max said, looking into the mirror, “why don’t you say something?”

“What’s it all about?”

“Hey, Al,” Max called, “bright boy wants to know what it’s all about.”

“Why don’t you tell him?” Al’s voice came from the kitchen.

“What do you think it’s all about?”

“I don’t know.”

“What do you think?”

Max looked into the mirror all the time he was talking.

“I wouldn’t say.”

“Hey, Al, bright boy says he wouldn’t say what he thinks it’s all about.”

“I can hear you, all right,” Al said from the kitchen. He had propped open the slit that dishes passed through into the kitchen with a catsup bottle. “Listen, bright boy,” he said from the kitchen to George. “Stand a little further along the bar. You move a little to the left, Max.” He was like a photographer arranging for a group picture.

“Talk to me, bright boy,” Max said. “What do you think’s going to happen?”

George did not say anything.

“I’ll tell you,” Max said. “We’re going to kill a Swede. Do you know a big Swede named Ole Andreson?”

“Yes.”

“He comes here to eat every night, don’t he?”

“Sometimes he comes here.”

“He comes here at six o’clock, don’t he?”

“If he comes.”

“We know all that, bright boy,” Max said. “Talk about something else. Ever go to the movies?”

“Once in a while.”

“You ought to go to the movies more. The movies are fine for a bright boy like you.”

“What are you going to kill Ole Andreson for? What did he ever do to you?”

“He never had a chance to do anything to us. He never even seen us.”

“And he’s only going to see us once,” Al said from the kitchen.

“What are you going to kill him for, then?” George asked.

“We’re killing him for a friend. Just to oblige a friend, bright boy.”

“Shut up,” said Al from the kitchen. “You talk too goddam much.”

“Well, I got to keep bright boy amused. Don’t I, bright boy?”

“You talk too damn much,” Al said. “The nigger and my bright boy are amused by themselves. I got them tied up like a couple of girl friends in the convent.”

“I suppose you were in a convent.”

“You never know.”

“You were in a kosher convent. That’s where you were.”

George looked up at the clock.

“If anybody comes in you tell them the cook is off, and if they keep after it, you tell them you’ll go back and cook yourself. Do you get that, bright boy?”

“All right,” George said. “What you going to do with us afterward?”

“That’ll depend,” Max said. “That’s one of those things you never know at the time.”

George looked up at the clock. It was a quarter past six. The door from the street opened. A streetcar motorman came in.

“Hello, George,” he said. “Can I get supper?”

“Sam’s gone out,” George said. “He’ll be back in about half an hour.”

“I’d better go up the street,” the motorman said. George looked at the clock. It was twenty minutes past six.

“That was nice, bright boy,” Max said. “You’re a regular little gentleman.”

“He knew I’d blow his head off,” Al said from the kitchen.

“No,” said Max. “It ain’t that. Bright boy is nice. He’s a nice boy. I like him.”

At six-fifty-five George said: “He’s not coming.”

Two other people had been in the lunchroom. Once George had gone out to the kitchen and made a ham-and-egg sandwich “to go” that a man wanted to take with him. Inside the kitchen he saw Al, his derby hat tipped back, sitting on a stool beside the wicket with the muzzle of a sawed-off shotgun resting on the ledge. Nick and the cook were back to back in the corner, a towel tied in each of their mouths. George had cooked the sandwich, wrapped it up in oiled paper, put it in a bag, brought it in, and the man had paid for it and gone out.

“Bright boy can do everything,” Max said. “He can cook and everything. You’d make some girl a nice wife, bright boy.”

“Yes?” George said. “Your friend, Ole Andreson, isn’t going to come.”

“We’ll give him ten minutes,” Max said.

Max watched the mirror and the clock. The hands of the clock marked seven o’clock, and then five minutes past seven.

“Come on, Al,” said Max. “We better go. He’s not coming.”

“Better give him five minutes,” Al said from the kitchen.

In the five minutes a man came in, and George explained that the cook was sick.

“Why the hell don’t you get another cook?” the man asked. “Aren’t you running a lunch-counter?” He went out.

“Come on, Al,” Max said.

“What about the two bright boys and the nigger?”

“They’re all right.”

“You think so?”

“Sure. We’re through with it.”

“I don’t like it,” said Al. “It’s sloppy. You talk too much.”

“Oh, what the hell,” said Max. “We got to keep amused, haven’t we?”

“You talk too much, all the same,” Al said. He came out from the kitchen. The cutoff barrels of the shotgun made a slight bulge under the waist of his too tight-fitting overcoat. He straightened his coat with his gloved hands.

“So long, bright boy,” he said to George. “You got a lot of luck.”

“That’s the truth,” Max said. “You ought to play the races, bright boy.”

The two of them went out the door. George watched them, through the window, pass under the arc-light and cross the street. In their tight overcoats and derby hats they looked like a vaudeville team. George went back through the swinging-door into the kitchen and untied Nick and the cook.

“I don’t want any more of that,” said Sam, the cook. “I don’t want any more of that.”

Nick stood up. He had never had a towel in his mouth before.

“Say,” he said. “What the hell?” He was trying to swagger it off.

“They were going to kill Ole Andreson,” George said. “They were going to shoot him when he came in to eat.”

“Ole Andreson?”

“Sure.”

The cook felt the corners of his mouth with his thumbs.

“They all gone?” he asked.

“Yeah,” said George. “They’re gone now.”

“I don’t like it,” said the cook. “I don’t like any of it at all.”

“Listen,” George said to Nick. “You better go see Ole Andreson.”

“All right.”

“You better not have anything to do with it at all,” Sam, the cook, said. “You better stay way out of it.”

“Don’t go if you don’t want to,” George said.

“Mixing up in this ain’t going to get you anywhere,” the cook said. “You stay out of it.”

“I’ll go see him,” Nick said to George. “Where does he live?”

The cook turned away.

“Little boys always know what they want to do,” he said.

“He lives up at Hirsch’s rooming-house,” George said to Nick.

“I’ll go up there.”

Outside the arc-light shone through the bare branches of a tree. Nick walked up the street beside the car-tracks and turned at the next arc-light down a side-street. Three houses up the street was Hirsch’s rooming-house. Nick walked up the two steps and pushed the bell. A woman came to the door.

“Is Ole Andreson here?”

“Do you want to see him?”

“Yes, if he’s in.”

Nick followed the woman up a flight of stairs and back to the end of a corridor. She knocked on the door.

“Who is it?”

“It’s somebody to see you, Mr. Andreson,” the woman said.

“It’s Nick Adams.”

“Come in.”

Nick opened the door and went into the room. Ole Andreson was lying on the bed with all his clothes on. He had been a heavyweight prizefighter and he was too long for the bed. He lay with his head on two pillows. He did not look at Nick.

“What was it?” he asked.

“I was up at Henry’s,” Nick said, “and two fellows came in and tied up me and the cook, and they said they were going to kill you.”

It sounded silly when he said it. Ole Andreson said nothing.

“They put us out in the kitchen,” Nick went on. “They were going to shoot you when you came in to supper.”

Ole Andreson looked at the wall and did not say anything.

“George thought I better come and tell you about it.”

“There isn’t anything I can do about it,” Ole Andreson said.

“I’ll tell you what they were like.”

“I don’t want to know what they were like,” Ole Andreson said. He looked at the wall. “Thanks for coming to tell me about it.”

“That’s all right.”

Nick looked at the big man lying on the bed.

“Don’t you want me to go and see the police?”

“No,” Ole Andreson said. “That wouldn’t do any good.”

“Isn’t there something I could do?”

“No. There ain’t anything to do.”

“Maybe it was just a bluff.”

“No. It ain’t just a bluff.”

Ole Andreson rolled over toward the wall.

“The only thing is,” he said, talking toward the wall, “I just can’t make up my mind to go out. I been in here all day.”

“Couldn’t you get out of town?”

“No,” Ole Andreson said. “I’m through with all that running around.”

He looked at the wall.

“There ain’t anything to do now.”

“Couldn’t you fix it up some way?”

“No. I got in wrong.” He talked in the same flat voice. “There ain’t anything to do. After a while I’ll make up my mind to go out.”

“I better go back and see George,” Nick said.

“So long,” said Ole Andreson. He did not look toward Nick. “Thanks for coming around.”

Nick went out. As he shut the door he saw Ole Andreson with all his clothes on, lying on the bed looking at the wall.

“He’s been in his room all day,” the landlady said downstairs. “I guess he don’t feel well. I said to him: ‘Mr. Andreson, you ought to go out and take a walk on a nice fall day like this,’ but he didn’t feel like it.”

“He doesn’t want to go out.”

“I’m sorry he don’t feel well,” the woman said. “He’s an awfully nice man. He was in the ring, you know.”

“I know it.”

“You’d never know it except from the way his face is,” the woman said. They stood talking just inside the street door. “He’s just as gentle.”

“Well, good night, Mrs. Hirsch,” Nick said.

“I’m not Mrs. Hirsch,” the woman said. “She owns the place. I just look after it for her. I’m Mrs. Bell.”

“Well, good night, Mrs. Bell,” Nick said.

“Good night,” the woman said.

Nick walked up the dark street to the corner under the arc-light, and then along the car-tracks to Henry’s eating-house. George was inside, back of the counter.

“Did you see Ole?”

“Yes,” said Nick. “He’s in his room and he won’t go out.”

The cook opened the door from the kitchen when he heard Nick’s voice.

“I don’t even listen to it,” he said and shut the door.

“Did you tell him about it?” George asked.

“Sure. I told him but he knows what it’s all about.”

“What’s he going to do?”

“Nothing.”

“They’ll kill him.”

“I guess they will.”

“He must have got mixed up in something in Chicago.”

“I guess so,” said Nick.

“It’s a hell of a thing.”

“It’s an awful thing,” Nick said.

They did not say anything. George reached down for a towel and wiped the counter.

“I wonder what he did?” Nick said.

“Double-crossed somebody. That’s what they kill them for.”

“I’m going to get out of this town,” Nick said.

“Yes,” said George. “That’s a good thing to do.”

“I can’t stand to think about him waiting in the room and knowing he’s going to get it. It’s too damned awful.”

“Well,” said George, “you better not think about it.”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The Killers is built in three movements. First comes the scene in Henry's lunchroom, the longest part by far, in which two men in tight black overcoats and derby hats, Al and Max, order dinner, make fun of the staff, and finally reveal that they have come to kill a man named Ole Andreson, a big Swede who usually eats there at six. They tie up Nick and the cook, Sam, in the kitchen, hold George at the counter, and wait. Ole does not come. At last they give up, walk out, and leave the others alive. In the second movement, Nick runs to the rooming house to warn Ole. He finds him lying on his bed, fully dressed, facing the wall, and Ole refuses to do anything. In the third, Nick returns to the lunchroom and says he is going to leave town.

What is it about? The most obvious answer is fear, but a more careful reading says it is about a collision between innocence and a world that does not care. The killers are almost comic at first. They bicker about the menu, call George and Nick "bright boys," and say they are killing Ole "just to oblige a friend." That phrase is a chilling joke: to them murder is a favor, a piece of business. They have nothing against him. He never did them any harm. The violence is utterly impersonal, which is what makes it terrifying. Nothing in the universe of the story gives it a reason.

Then there is Ole Andreson. Once a heavyweight prizefighter, he lies on the bed and says, "There ain't anything to do." He has stopped running. He says he got "in wrong," and he is waiting for the thing to happen. This surrender is what truly shakes Nick. A big man, a man who once fought for a living, has been reduced to a body turned toward a wall. The story suggests that some doom cannot be argued with, and that some people, when it finds them, simply stop.

Nick is the real center. At the start he is a boy who watches. At the end he says he cannot stand to think about Ole waiting in his room, "knowing he's going to get it. It's too damned awful." George, older and more settled, gives the answer of the world: "Well, you better not think about it." That is the story's final line, and it is the cold truth of how adults cope. Nick has crossed a line from a boy who didn't know to a young man who does. The story is an initiation, a loss of innocence told without a single speech about it. It also carries, in the background, the presence of casual racism in the era's language, which the story records without comment and which modern readers will notice.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The craft of The Killers is spectacular, and its first great strength is tension built from almost nothing. For nearly half the story, the menace comes entirely from dialogue and delay. The men's refusal to order, their mocking repetition, their small cruelties, and the patience of their waiting produce a quiet dread that action rarely achieves. The famous opening exchange, with Al and Max each saying "I don't know what I want to eat," sets the pattern: speech that circles and circles, and under it a threat.

Hemingway's ear for dialogue is the engine. The killers talk in a stylized, almost vaudeville way. They echo each other and slide from comedy into threat. "The town's full of bright boys," says Max. Their repetition is reminiscent of the routine of a comedy double act, which is part of the horror, since menace delivered as a routine seems to have no human feeling at all. George's cautious replies and Nick's silence form a counterpoint. Nobody says what he feels, and so everything is felt. The scene in the kitchen, where Nick and Sam sit tied while Al sits with a sawed-off shotgun on the ledge of the serving hatch, is a model of controlled physical description: simple facts that make the reader's pulse rise.

The structure is also admirable. The story spends a great deal of time on the scene where nothing is accomplished and then moves quickly through the aftermath. That imbalance is deliberate. The waiting is the story, and the killers' failure to find their victim denies the reader the satisfaction of a crime plot, replacing it with something harder: knowledge.

There are real weaknesses to consider. The killers are drawn so stylishly that some readers feel Hemingway admires them, or at least that they become more vivid than the people they threaten. The dialogue can tip into mannerism, with its relentless repetition, and in the hands of imitators it became a parody. Ole's passivity is a mystery the story deliberately refuses to solve, but some readers find his fatalism too convenient, a device to produce the final shock rather than a convincing human response. The ending, with Nick's decision, is slightly more explicit about its meaning than the best of Hemingway's work, since the boy practically states the theme.

Finally, the casual racist language applied to the cook, and the way Sam is treated as a minor figure who simply wants to avoid trouble, are features a present-day reader cannot ignore. The story is recording the attitudes of its time and its characters, but it records them without any visible critical perspective, and that deserves honest notice.

Even so, the story's mastery is plain. It is rare to find a piece of fiction that creates so much fear from so little, and does it with such discipline.

III. The Impact It Had

When The Killers appeared in Scribner's Magazine in March 1927, and then in Men Without Women in October, it was greeted as a demonstration of what American short fiction could now do. It helped cement Hemingway's reputation as the leading stylist of his generation, and it became one of the most widely reprinted short stories in the language. It is also the story where Nick Adams, the semi-autobiographical young man who runs through Hemingway's early work, appears in one of his most dramatic roles.

Its influence on crime writing was immediate and lasting. The hard-boiled school of American detective fiction, led by writers like Dashiell Hammett and later Raymond Chandler, shared Hemingway's taste for plain, vernacular speech and his refusal to sentimentalize violence. James M. Cain, Jim Thompson, and a whole tradition of noir fiction follow in the wake. More specifically, the image of two professional killers who chat idly before work has been borrowed again and again. It is hard to watch a later scene of hit men talking about nothing, whether in the films of Quentin Tarantino or the Coen brothers, without thinking of Al and Max.

The story has been adapted for the screen repeatedly. The best known is the 1946 film directed by Robert Siodmak, which expands the story into a full film noir: the opening twenty minutes follow Hemingway's story closely, and then the film spins a long investigation of why Ole, here played by Burt Lancaster in his debut, was marked for death. A second adaptation, directed by Don Siegel in 1964, reshaped the material again, and it has the distinction of featuring Lee Marvin as a hit man. A short student film of the story was also made in 1956 by the young Andrei Tarkovsky, before he became one of the great directors of world cinema.

Within the history of the short story, The Killers became a staple of teaching. It is a classic example of dramatic, objective narration, in which the author remains outside the characters' minds and the reader is forced to infer. It is used to illustrate subtext, repetition, tone, and the structure of an initiation story.

For Hemingway's career, it was a high point of his early period, showing that his method worked not just on quiet domestic dilemmas but on high-tension material. It is part of the body of work that supported his later Nobel Prize and the extraordinary force of his example on twentieth-century prose.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have always responded to the sheer suspense of The Killers. Even people who know nothing about Hemingway or modernism find the lunchroom scene gripping. The menace is physical and immediate, and the humor of the gunmen, their weird hospitality and their taunting, makes the reader both laugh and shiver. There is an old observation that the most frightening villains are the ones who talk like comedians, and Al and Max are the proof.

Readers also love the story's economy. In a few thousand words it creates a town, a decor, a set of people, and a crisis. Henry's lunchroom, made over from a saloon, with the mirror along the back and the arc light outside, is a complete place. The reader feels the clock on the wall, which George says runs twenty minutes fast, and the streetlight coming on at dusk. The details are chosen so tightly that readers who return to the story often discover a new one each time.

Many admire the ending. Instead of a burst of violence, the story gives a moral pivot: a young man deciding to leave a town where such a thing can happen. People find it resonant because it suggests how most of us meet cruelty, not by fighting it but by trying to look away, and by being unable to stop thinking about it. George's advice not to think about it is, for many readers, the saddest line, because it is what practical adults really say, and it shows how a community absorbs horror by refusing to look.

Writers in particular admire how well the story demonstrates what Hemingway could do with speech. Teachers of fiction often use it to show how dialogue can characterize, advance a plot, and build tension all at once. Crime writers revere it. Film directors have loved it for its visual, theatrical staging: a single room, a few actors, and a ticking threat. It plays almost like a one-act play.

And then there is Ole Andreson, who has been a source of fascination ever since. The scene in the rooming house, with the huge, gentle man facing the wall, is regarded by many as the emotional peak of the story. His flat refusal to run, and the quiet way the landlady says "he's just as gentle," humanize a figure who might have been a mere target. Readers are moved by that dignity and that despair, and they often find the unexplained mystery of what he did to be more haunting than any answer could be.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Killers has been largely admired, but a number of reservations have been voiced. One is the old complaint about the Hemingway mannerism. The clipped, repetitive speech of the killers and the plain declarative narration are so distinctive that, even at the time, some reviewers worried they were becoming a formula. Later critics have said that Hemingway's style, so fresh in its first appearances, could harden into a pose of toughness, and that the killers' routine is a little too stylized to be fully believable as speech. Real gangsters, the objection goes, do not talk like a vaudeville act. This is a fair point about realism, though defenders reply that the story is not aiming at documentary realism but at a heightened, almost ritual effect.

A second line of criticism concerns the story's unexplained center. We never learn what Ole did. Some readers find the lack of explanation frustrating, a mystery that is also an evasion. Others, as with the Siodmak film, have felt the need to supply a backstory, which is a kind of critical verdict in itself. Hemingway's view, consistent with his theory of leaving out what the reader can feel, was that the explanation would weaken the story. But the story's fatalism, in which Ole simply lies down to wait for death, has struck some critics as an unconvincing passivity.

A third area is the treatment of race and class. The cook, Sam, is described with a racial slur in the narration and in George's speech, and is a figure of fear and compliance. Modern readers rightly note that the story records this without any critical frame. Whether the author is merely reflecting the world he wrote about or sharing its blind spots is debated, and it is one of the reasons the story is taught today with some care.

There are also criticisms of the story's moral vision. Some have found its treatment of violence as an inexplicable fact, to be endured and avoided, too bleak or too resigned. The story offers no justice, no redemption, and no counterforce. Readers who want some sense of moral order may feel that the story, however brilliantly made, is a shrug. Others say that this is exactly the honesty that makes it great.

Finally, a few critics have observed that the story is more admired than any one of its parts. The famous opening dialogue is often cited as the best part, and the rooming house scene and the ending, while powerful, have drawn comparatively less attention and sometimes feel like a necessary epilogue to an extraordinary first movement.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Killers stands at a crossroads between literary fiction and genre fiction. Its materials come from the crime tale, with hit men, a doomed victim, and a Chicago background, but its method comes from modernist literature, with its objectivity, compression, and refusal to explain. This fusion is one of the most productive in twentieth-century writing. It helped open the way for the literary respectability of crime fiction and for the hard-boiled style of Hammett, Chandler, Cain, and later writers, while also showing literary writers that violence and menace could be handled with great subtlety.

In Hemingway's own work, it belongs with the early Nick Adams stories and with the collection In Our Time. Together they trace a young man's education in a violent world: the Indian camp where a baby is born and a father kills himself, the war stories, the fishing trips that are really recoveries. In those, as in this, Nick is the observer to whom something terrible happens, and the lesson is not articulated but absorbed. The story is also in conversation with a long American tradition of the innocent encountering evil, from Hawthorne and Melville to Twain.

Among comparisons, it resembles in some ways Shirley Jackson's The Lottery, in which a small town carries out a ritual violence in a flat, everyday tone, and Flannery O'Connor's A Good Man Is Hard to Find, in which a family meets a killer. Each uses plain, cheerful surface detail to contrast with horror. It also has kinship with Chekhov's method of ending on an open note and with Joyce's technique in Dubliners, where a small event produces a moment of realization.

Its descendants are everywhere: in the films of Tarantino, whose hit men chatter about hamburgers, in Harold Pinter's play The Dumb Waiter, in which two killers wait in a basement room and bicker, in the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men, and in the grim, understated tone of countless crime dramas on screen. Whenever violence is presented as routine, delivered by people who talk about lunch, the lineage leads back to Henry's lunchroom.

Why read it today? Because it is a model of suspense that does not depend on gimmicks, a lesson in how to write dialogue, and an enduring portrait of the moment when a young person realizes that the world contains forces that cannot be reasoned with and that most adults handle by looking away. It takes twenty minutes to read. It will teach you to hear what a conversation hides, and it will probably make you slightly uneasy the next time two strangers walk into a quiet diner.
