The Necklace

Guy de Maupassant (1884) • 14 min read

“How little a thing is needed for us to be lost or to be saved!”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Guy de Maupassant published The Necklace in a Paris newspaper in 1884, and it quickly became one of the most famous short stories ever written. Maupassant was a pupil and protege of Gustave Flaubert, the great French novelist, who taught him to look at the world with unsparing clarity and to find exactly the right word. In a short, astonishingly productive career, cut off by illness and early death, Maupassant wrote hundreds of stories, many of them about the French middle and lower classes, who were gripped by money, status and appearances. The Necklace belongs to that world. It follows a pretty woman married to a modest clerk who longs for a life of luxury she was not born to. Its surprise ending is so well known that it has made the phrase twist ending almost a literary category. As you listen, notice how small a single mistake can seem, and how large its consequences become.

She was one of those pretty and charming girls who are sometimes, as if by a mistake of destiny, born in a family of clerks. She had no dowry, no expectations, no means of being known, understood, loved, wedded, by any rich and distinguished man; and she let herself be married to a little clerk at the Ministry of Public Instruction. She dressed plainly because she could not dress well, but she was as unhappy as though she had really fallen from her proper station; since with women there is neither caste nor rank; and beauty, grace, and charm act instead of family and birth.

She suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born for all the delicacies and all the luxuries. She suffered from the poverty of her dwelling, from the wretched look of the walls, from the worn-out chairs, from the ugliness of the curtains. All those things, of which another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious, tortured her and made her angry. The sight of the little Breton peasant who did her humble housework aroused in her regrets which were despairing, and distracted dreams. She thought of silent antechambers hung with Oriental tapestry, lit by tall bronze candelabra, and of two great footmen in knee-breeches who sleep in the big armchairs, made drowsy by the heavy warmth of the hot-air stove. She thought of long salons fitted up with ancient silk, of delicate furniture carrying priceless curiosities, and of coquettish perfumed boudoirs made for talks at five o’clock with intimate friends, with men famous and sought after, whom all women envy and whose attention they all desire.

When she sat down to dinner, before the round table covered with a table-cloth three days old, opposite her husband, who uncovered the soup-tureen and declared with an enchanted air, “Ah, the good pot-au-feu! I don’t know anything better than that,” she thought of dainty dinners, of shining silverware, of tapestry which peopled the walls with ancient personages and with strange birds flying in the midst of a fairy forest; and she thought of delicious dishes served on marvellous plates, and of the whispered gallantries which you listen to with a sphinx-like smile, while you are eating the pink flesh of a trout or the wings of a quail. She had no dresses, no jewels, nothing. And she loved nothing but that; she felt made for that. She had a friend, a former schoolmate at the convent, who was rich, and whom she did not like to go to see any more, because she suffered so much when she came back.

But, one evening, her husband came home with a triumphant air, and holding a large envelope in his hand. “There,” said he, “here is something for you.” She tore the paper sharply, and drew out a printed card which bore these words: “The Minister of Public Instruction and Mme. Georges Ramponneau request the honor of M. and Mme. Loisel’s company at the palace of the Ministry on Monday evening, January 18th.” Instead of being delighted, as her husband hoped, she threw the invitation on the table with disdain, murmuring: “What do you want me to do with that?” “But, my dear, I thought you would be glad. You never go out, and this is an occasion, a fine one! I had awful trouble to get it. Every one wants to go; it is very select, and they are not giving many invitations to clerks. The whole official world will be there.”

She looked at him with an irritated eye, and she said, impatiently: “And what do you want me to put on my back?” He had not thought of that; he stammered: “Why, the dress you go to the theatre in. It looks very well, to me.” He stopped, distracted, seeing that his wife was crying. Two great tears descended slowly from the corners of her eyes toward the corners of her mouth. He stuttered: “What’s the matter? What’s the matter?” By a violent effort she conquered her grief, and replied in a calm voice, while she wiped her wet cheeks: “Nothing. Only I have no dress and therefore I can’t go to this ball. Give your card to some colleague whose wife is better equipped than I.” He was in despair. He resumed: “Come, let us see, Mathilde. How much would it cost, a suitable dress, which you could use on other occasions, something very simple?” She reflected several seconds, making her calculations and wondering also what sum she could ask without drawing on herself an immediate refusal and a frightened exclamation from the economical clerk. Finally, she replied, hesitatingly: “I don’t know exactly, but I think I could manage it with four hundred francs.” He had grown a little pale, because he was laying aside just that amount to buy a gun and treat himself to a little shooting next summer on the plain of Nanterre, with several friends who went to shoot larks there of a Sunday. But he said: “All right. I will give you four hundred francs. And try to have a pretty dress.”

The day of the ball drew near, and Mme. Loisel seemed sad, uneasy, anxious. Her dress was ready, however. Her husband said to her one evening: “What is the matter? Come, you’ve been so queer these last three days.” And she answered: “It annoys me not to have a single jewel, not a single stone, nothing to put on. I shall look like distress. I should almost rather not go at all.” He resumed: “You might wear natural flowers. It’s very stylish at this time of the year. For ten francs you can get two or three magnificent roses.” She was not convinced. “No; there’s nothing more humiliating than to look poor among other women who are rich.” Then her husband cried out: “How stupid we are! Go look up your friend Mme. Forestier, and ask her to lend you some jewels. You’re quite thick enough with her to do that.” She uttered a cry of joy: “It’s true. I never thought of it.”

The next day she went to her friend and told of her distress. Mme. Forestier went to a wardrobe with a glass door, took out a large jewel-box, brought it back, opened it, and said to Mme. Loisel: “Choose, my dear.” She saw first of all some bracelets, then a pearl necklace, then a Venetian cross, gold and precious stones of admirable workmanship. She tried on the ornaments before the glass, hesitated, could not make up her mind to part with them, to give them back. She kept asking: “Haven’t you any more?” “Why, yes. Look. I don’t know what you like.” All of a sudden she discovered, in a black satin box, a superb necklace of diamonds, and her heart began to beat with an immoderate desire. Her hands trembled as she took it. She fastened it around her throat, outside her high-necked dress, and remained lost in ecstasy at the sight of herself. Then she asked, haltingly, full of anxiety: “Could you lend me this, only this?” “Why, yes, certainly.” She sprang upon the neck of her friend, kissed her passionately, then fled with her treasure.

The day of the ball arrived. Mme. Loisel made a great success. She was prettier than them all, elegant, gracious, smiling, and crazy with joy. All the men looked at her, asked her name, endeavored to be introduced. All the attachés of the Cabinet wanted to waltz with her. She was remarked by the minister himself. She danced with intoxication, with passion, made drunk by pleasure, forgetting all, in the triumph of her beauty, in the glory of her success, in a sort of cloud of happiness composed of all this homage, of all this admiration, of all these awakened desires, and of that sense of a complete victory which is so sweet to step into a woman’s heart.

She went away about four o’clock in the morning. Her husband had been sleeping since midnight, in a little deserted anteroom, with three other gentlemen whose wives were having a very good time. He threw over her shoulders the wraps which he had brought, modest wraps of common life, whose poverty contrasted with the elegance of the ball dress. She felt this and wanted to escape so as not to be remarked by the other women, who were enveloping themselves in costly furs. Loisel held her back. “Wait a bit. You will catch cold outside. I will go and call a cab.” But she did not listen to him, and rapidly descended the stairs. When they were in the street they did not find a carriage; and they began to look for one, shouting after the cabmen whom they saw passing them at a distance. They went down toward the Seine, in despair, shivering with cold. At last they found on the quay one of those ancient noctambulant coupés which, exactly as if they were ashamed to show their misery during the day, are never seen round Paris until after nightfall. It took them to their door in the Rue des Martyrs, and once more, sadly, they climbed up homeward.

All was ended, for her. And as to him, he reflected that he must be at the Ministry at ten o’clock. She removed the wraps, which covered her shoulders, before the glass, so as once more to see herself in all her glory. But suddenly she uttered a cry. She had no longer the necklace around her neck! Her husband, already half-undressed, demanded: “What is the matter with you?” She turned madly toward him: “I have—I have—I’ve lost Mme. Forestier’s necklace.” He stood up, distracted: “What!—how?—Impossible!” And they looked in the folds of her dress, in the folds of her cloak, in her pockets, everywhere. They did not find it.

Loisel, who had already put on his clothes, said: “I am going back on foot, over the whole route which we have taken, to see if I can’t find it.” And he went out. She sat waiting on a chair in her ball dress, without strength to go to bed, overwhelmed, without fire, without a thought. Her husband came back about seven o’clock. He had found nothing. He went to Police Headquarters, to the newspaper offices, to offer a reward; he went to the cab companies—everywhere, in fact, whither he was urged by the least spark of hope. She waited all day, in the same condition of mad fear before this terrible calamity. Loisel returned at night with a hollow, pale face; he had discovered nothing. “You must write to your friend,” said he, “that you have broken the clasp of her necklace and that you are having it mended. That will give us time to turn round.” At the end of a week they had lost all hope. And Loisel, who had aged five years, declared: “We must consider how to replace that jewel.”

The next day they took the box which had contained it, and they went to the jeweler whose name was found within. He consulted his books. “It was not I, madame, who sold that necklace; I must simply have furnished the case.” Then they went from jeweler to jeweler, searching for a necklace like the other, consulting their memories, sick both of them with chagrin and with anguish. In a shop at the Palais Royal they found a string of diamonds which seemed to them exactly like the one they looked for. It was worth forty thousand francs. They could have it for thirty-six. So they begged the jeweler not to sell it for three days yet. And they made a bargain that he should buy it back for thirty-four thousand francs, in case they found the other one before the end of February. Loisel possessed eighteen thousand francs which his father had left him. He would borrow the rest. He did borrow, asking a thousand francs of one, five hundred of another, five louis here, three louis there. He gave notes, took up ruinous obligations, dealt with usurers, and all the race of lenders. He compromised all the rest of his life, risked his signature without even knowing if he could meet it; and, frightened by the pains yet to come, by the black misery which was about to fall upon him, by the prospect of all the physical privations and of all the moral tortures which he was to suffer, he went to get the new necklace, putting down upon the merchant’s counter thirty-six thousand francs.

Mme. Loisel came to know the horrible existence of the needy. She took her part, moreover, all on a sudden, with heroism. That dreadful debt must be paid. She would pay it. They dismissed their servant; they changed their lodgings; they rented a garret under the roof. She came to know what heavy housework meant and the odious cares of the kitchen. She washed the dishes, using her rosy nails on the greasy pots and the bottoms of the pans. She washed the dirty linen, the shirts, and the dish-cloths, which she dried upon a line; she carried the slops down to the street every morning, and carried up the water, stopping for breath at every landing. And, dressed like a woman of the people, she went to the fruiterer, the grocer, the butcher, her basket on her arm, bargaining, insulted, defending her miserable money sou by sou. Every month they had to meet some notes, renew others, obtain more time. Her husband worked in the evening making a fair copy of some tradesman’s accounts, and late at night he often copied manuscript for five sous a page. And this life lasted ten years. At the end of ten years they had paid everything, everything, with the rates of usurer, and the accumulations of the compound interest.

Mme. Loisel looked old now. She had become the woman of impoverished households—strong and hard and rough. With frowsy hair, skirts askew, and red hands, she talked loud while washing the floor with great swishes of water. But sometimes, when her husband was at the office, she sat down near the window, and she thought of that gay evening of long ago, of that ball where she had been so beautiful and so fêted. What would have happened if she had not lost that necklace? Who knows? who knows? How strange life is, how changeful! How little a thing is needed for us to be lost or to be saved!

But, one Sunday, having gone to take a walk in the Champs Élysées to refresh herself from the labors of the week, she suddenly perceived a woman who was leading a child. It was Mme. Forestier, still young, still beautiful, still charming. Mme. Loisel felt moved. Was she going to speak to her? Yes, certainly. And now that she had paid, she was going to tell her all about it. Why not? She went up. “Good-day, Jeanne.” The other, astonished to be familiarly addressed by this plain good-wife, did not recognize her at all, and stammered: “But—madame!—I do not know—You must have mistaken.” “No. I am Mathilde Loisel.” Her friend uttered a cry. “Oh, my poor Mathilde! How you are changed!” “Yes, I have had days hard enough, since I saw you, and miserable ones—and all because of you!” “Of me! How so?” “Do you remember that diamond necklace which you lent me to wear at the ministerial ball?” “Yes. Well?” “Well, I lost it.” “What do you mean? You brought it back.” “I brought you back another just like it. And for this we have been ten years paying. You can understand that it was not easy for us, us who had nothing. At last it is ended, and I am very glad.” Mme. Forestier had stopped. “You say that you bought a necklace of diamonds to replace mine?” “Yes. You never noticed it, then! They were very like.” And she smiled with a joy which was proud and naïve at once. Mme. Forestier, strongly moved, took her two hands. “Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste. It was worth at most five hundred francs!”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The story follows Mathilde Loisel, a pretty, charming woman born, in Maupassant's words, as if by a mistake of destiny into a family of clerks. She has no dowry and no prospects, so she marries a minor clerk in the Ministry of Public Instruction. She suffers constantly, feeling that she was made for all the delicacies and luxuries of life. She looks at her shabby apartment, her worn chairs and her husband's contentment with a plain stew, and dreams of silver, tapestries and exquisite dinners. She also has a rich friend, Madame Forestier, whom she can no longer bear to visit because it makes her unhappy.

One day her husband brings home an invitation to a ball at the Minister's house, expecting her to be delighted. She weeps instead, because she has nothing to wear. He gives her four hundred francs he had saved for a gun, and she buys a dress. Still she needs jewels, so she borrows a diamond necklace from Madame Forestier. At the ball she is a sensation. She dances, she is admired, she is intoxicated by her success. In the early hours, they rush out to avoid being seen in her humble wrap, and at home she finds the necklace gone.

They search everywhere. They find a nearly identical necklace at a jeweler's, for thirty-six thousand francs. Loisel spends his father's inheritance of eighteen thousand and borrows the rest at ruinous rates. They return the replacement, and Madame Forestier does not look inside the case. Then comes the long punishment. The Loisels dismiss their maid, move to a garret, and work endlessly. She does the hardest housework, and he takes on extra jobs at night. For ten years they struggle until the debt is paid. Mathilde becomes old, coarse and rough-handed.

The final scene occurs on a Sunday walk in the Champs-Elysees. She meets Madame Forestier, still young and lovely. She tells her the truth about the lost necklace and the years of toil. Madame Forestier, deeply moved, takes her hands and says that her necklace was paste, worth at most five hundred francs.

The meaning is bitter and layered. On one level, it is a warning about vanity and borrowed finery. But it is also about the strange power of appearances: what was fake could not be told from what was real, and the whole drama of ruin was built on a misjudgment. Maupassant adds a line before the end about how strange life is, and how little is needed to ruin or save us. A reader might also notice that the loss of the necklace did not corrupt Mathilde. It hardened and, in a way, ennobled her. She faces the debt with courage. The story leaves open whether those ten years were wasted or whether they made her a person of real substance.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The Necklace is a model of concision and control. Maupassant moves from Mathilde's daydreams to the ball to the ruinous years with remarkable speed, giving us only what matters. The first section establishes her discontent in a few strokes of detail: the worn chairs, the three-day-old tablecloth, the husband's delight in a pot of stew. We feel her longing and, importantly, her pettiness. Maupassant never idealizes her. He shows her as vain, restless and unjust to the husband who loves her, and yet he lets us feel the ache under her vanity.

The ball is the story's hinge, and it is handled in a few sentences of intoxicated prose. The language swells, then collapses with the cold of the street, the cheap cab and the missing necklace. That structure, a rise and then a long, grim fall, is classic. The ten-year summary that follows is the real achievement. By compressing a decade into a single paragraph, Maupassant makes time itself the punishment, and the image of Mathilde's red hands and rough skirt does more than any scene of weeping could.

The ending is the famous piece of craft. The reveal is both a shock and an inevitability. In retrospect, the clues are all there: Madame Forestier never opened the case, and the narrator's insistence that no one could tell. The irony works because it arrives on the last line, with no commentary. Maupassant lets the fact stand, which is exactly the kind of restraint Flaubert preached.

Yet the story has weaknesses a careful reader will notice. The plot rests on a few conveniences. It is hard to believe that Mathilde would not simply tell her friend that the necklace was lost and ask for time, and the story gives only a thin reason, a pride that would have been more convincing with a little more preparation. Nor does Madame Forestier ever check the replacement, or notice that her own necklace came back a little different. A modern reader might also find the moral rather neat. The tale is sometimes read as a punishment of a vain woman, and the husband's contribution to the disaster is oddly forgotten.

There is also a hint of cruelty in the narration. The narrator is detached to the point of coldness, and some readers feel that Maupassant, for all his pity, rather enjoys the cosmic joke. The final twist is so elegant that it can feel like the whole story was built to deliver it, and the characters become instruments of the irony rather than people in their own right. Even so, the efficiency and the sting remain admirable.

One last point of craft deserves mention. Maupassant uses the narrator's voice to slip between pity and mockery within a single sentence, which keeps the reader slightly off balance. We are never quite sure whether we are meant to laugh at Mathilde or grieve with her, and that uncertainty gives the story a life that a purely moral fable would lack.

III. The Impact It Had

The Necklace made Maupassant's name even larger than it already was. It appeared in a daily newspaper in 1884, and in the same period he was publishing story after story in the French press, becoming one of the most widely read writers in France. Within a few years he had also written novels, including Bel-Ami, but his reputation has always rested most firmly on his short fiction, and this story is probably the best known of all.

Its greatest impact has been on the short story form. The idea of a story built toward a final, ironic reversal, in which one fact recasts everything that came before, became a model for later writers, and for popular magazine fiction in particular. O. Henry, who wrote his own surprise-ending tales, is often described as Maupassant's heir in this regard, even though his tone is warmer and more playful. Writers like Saki, Roald Dahl and the authors of countless television thrillers have used the twist, and they are working in a tradition that this story did much to shape.

The tale has been translated into dozens of languages and adapted many times for stage, radio, film and television, including productions in France, Britain, the United States, India and elsewhere. It has been retold as a modern parable about borrowed lifestyles, social media, credit and consumer debt. The central image, a fake that costs a fortune, makes it an easy story to retell in any era.

In education, The Necklace is arguably the most commonly assigned short story in the world. It is short, vivid and readily explained, and it teaches irony, characterization and plot structure in a single sitting. Teachers use it to discuss social class, materialism, pride, and the difference between what we want and what we have. Few students forget the ending.

It also shaped the image of Maupassant himself. Unlike many authors of his day, he wrote with clarity and without sentimentality, a style that influenced other writers around the world, Anton Chekhov among them, who is generally counted among the Russian writers who read him closely. Maupassant's death at forty-two, after a period of illness and mental breakdown, added to the sense of a brilliant but doomed talent, and his best stories have been read as a final testament to a society obsessed with appearances.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Necklace for its irresistible simplicity. The story can be told in a minute, yet it remains gripping on every reading. The premise, a woman borrows jewelry, loses it, and pays for it, is understandable to anyone, and the stakes feel real. We have all wanted something we could not afford, and the story gives that universal desire a vivid, shocking form.

Many love the ending above all. The reveal is among the most celebrated in literature, and it provokes a gasp or a groan even from readers who know it is coming. What makes it more than a trick is the emotional charge. Ten years of sacrifice, all for a counterfeit. The reader feels pity, irony and even dark humor all at once. The moment Madame Forestier takes Mathilde's hands, moved and astonished, is oddly tender, a flash of human feeling before the story ends.

Readers also admire the economy of the portraits. Mathilde, with her restless longing, is memorable because she is neither a villain nor a saint. Her husband, kind, modest and mildly dull, is equally real. Maupassant gives them just enough detail to feel alive. Admirers often point to the paragraph where Mathilde becomes a woman of the poor, strong, hard and loud, as the story's most powerful passage, because it shows a person transformed by circumstance without any preaching.

Another source of affection is the story's moral ambiguity. Is it a cautionary tale about vanity, or a lament about how brutally society punishes a small error? Is Mathilde foolish, or is she a victim of a world that values display and offers a poor woman no other route to dignity? Readers find that the story supports both readings, and that is part of the reason it can be returned to again and again.

Writers have praised it as a lesson in how to compress a lifetime into a few pages. It shows how a single object, a necklace, can carry a whole social world: ambition, class envy, pride and the cost of appearances. It is a story that rewards not only the first reading, when the twist surprises, but later ones, when you notice how carefully the trap has been laid.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The Necklace has attracted some familiar criticisms. One is that it is too neat, too much a mechanism. The twist is so central that the rest of the story can feel like machinery built to deliver it. Readers who prefer open, ambiguous fiction, the kind where life goes on without a punchline, sometimes find Maupassant's design a little schematic. Some of the story's earliest admirers also noted that the ending is cruelly arbitrary: the universe, in Maupassant's telling, seems to enjoy playing jokes on people.

A second objection concerns plausibility. As noted, it is difficult to believe that Mathilde would not simply have confessed to her friend, or that a jeweler would have so exactly matched a necklace. Some critics have argued that the whole disaster depends on a lack of simple honesty that the story fails to explain convincingly. Others reply that this is exactly the point: pride and fear of embarrassment are the very sins that the story is about.

Third, there is a moral objection. Many modern readers feel that the story is hard on Mathilde. It punishes her for wanting a better life, while her husband, who handed over his inheritance and works as hard as she does, is oddly invisible. Some see an undertone of misogyny in a plot that makes a vain woman the author of her own ruin. Others counter that Maupassant's sympathy is clearer than it looks, and that the story's real target is a society in which a woman's only capital is her charm. Both views have supporters, and both are worth weighing.

There is also the argument that the story is conservative in its lesson. Its moral appears to be that people should be content with their station and not reach beyond it. The cost of ambition is ruin. Some critics regard this as a bleak and even reactionary view, though others point out that Maupassant, a skeptic who rarely preached, may be showing us a cruelty rather than endorsing it.

Finally, some readers think the story's fame has damaged it. Because it is so often assigned, so often parodied, and so often reduced to its twist, it can be hard to see its subtlety. For many it is a story they have been told rather than one they have read. It deserves a second look, with the ending known, to appreciate its craft.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

The Necklace belongs to French literary realism and naturalism, the movements of the nineteenth century that tried to portray ordinary lives and social conditions with scientific honesty. Maupassant's teacher Flaubert, with Madame Bovary, had shown how a woman's longing for a more glamorous life could lead to ruin, and Mathilde Loisel is in some ways a miniature Emma Bovary, a woman whose imagination is larger than her circumstances. Maupassant also came out of the circle around Emile Zola, who argued that character and fate are shaped by environment. Yet Maupassant is more ironic and more compressed than either.

Behind both lies the long tradition of the moral tale, in which a vice has consequences, and the older folk tales of wishes granted and badly used. Maupassant's version is secular and modern. The magic is money, the genie is social status, and the price is a decade of labor.

The story's descendants are everywhere. The twist ending, as a staple of the American magazine story, was perfected by O. Henry, and in the twentieth century by writers like Saki, John Collier, Roald Dahl and Shirley Jackson, as well as in television series built on surprising final revelations. Its theme, that appearances deceive and that the pursuit of status can destroy a life, runs through novels such as The Great Gatsby, where a longing for glamour has fatal consequences. It also has cousins in stories of debt and class around the world, from Russian tales by Chekhov to modern realist fiction.

A useful comparison is with Kate Chopin and with Chekhov, both of whom learned from Maupassant. From him they took the lesson that a short story can carry a whole life in a few pages. They also moved beyond him, adding more ambiguity and more tenderness, so that reading Maupassant first, and then the others, shows how the form developed.

Why read it today? Because its subject has not aged. In a world of curated images and easy credit, the temptation to borrow a glittering surface and the shame of being seen without it are, if anything, more intense. The story reminds us that what looks precious may be paste, and that what we believe about value can cost us a decade. It takes ten minutes to read, and the last line has the unusual power to make a reader want to go back and begin again.
