Nightly Essay • Psychological & Philosophical Treatise

Habit

William James (1890) • 10 min read

Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits, they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state: we are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone.

Key Context & Why It Matters

William James's "Habit" is the chapter of a famous science book that escaped into everyday life. James was a Harvard physician turned psychologist and philosopher, the older brother of the novelist Henry James, and he spent twelve years writing his two-volume Principles of Psychology, published in 1890. This chapter was first printed as a magazine article, and it shows. It is clear, vivid and urgent, written for ordinary people, not specialists. James had his own reasons for caring. In his late twenties he fell into a deep depression, convinced that science had made human beings into helpless machines, and he climbed out by deciding to treat his own choices as real. Habit, for him, is both the machinery and the escape route. The brain is physically shaped by what we repeat, so repetition can trap us or free us. His chapter explains how, and ends with practical rules for building good habits and breaking bad ones.

When we look at living creatures from an outward point of view, one of the first things that strike us is that they are bundles of habits. In wild animals, the usual round of daily behavior seems a necessity implanted at birth; in animals domesticated, and especially in man, it seems, to a great extent, to be the result of education. The habits to which there is an innate tendency are called instincts; some of those due to education would by most persons be called acts of reason. It thus appears that habit covers a very large part of life, and that one engaged in studying the objective manifestations of mind is bound at the very outset to define clearly just what its limits are.

The moment one tries to define what habit is, one is led to the fundamental properties of matter. The laws of Nature are nothing but the immutable habits which the different elementary sorts of matter follow in their actions and reactions upon each other. In the organic world, however, the habits are more variable than this. Even instincts vary from one individual to another of a kind; and are modified in the same individual, as we shall later see, to suit the exigencies of the case. Plasticity, then, in the wide sense of the word, means the possession of a structure weak enough to yield to an influence, but strong enough not to yield all at once. Each relatively stable phase of equilibrium in such a structure is marked by what we may call a new set of habits. Organic matter, especially nervous tissue, seems endowed with a very extraordinary degree of plasticity of this sort; so that we may without hesitation lay down as our first proposition the following, that the phenomena of habit in living beings are due to the plasticity of the organic materials of which their bodies are composed.

If habits are due to the plasticity of materials to outward agents, we can immediately see to what outward influences, if to any, the brain-matter is plastic. Not to mechanical pressures, not to thermal changes, not to any of the forces to which all the other organs of our body are exposed; for nature has carefully shut up our brain and spinal cord in bony boxes, where no influences of this sort can get at them. She has floated them in fluid so that only the severest shocks can give them a concussion, and blanketed and wrapped them about in an altogether exceptional way. The only impressions that can be made upon them are through the blood, on the one hand, and through the sensory nerve-roots, on the other; and it is to the infinitely attenuated currents that pour in through these latter channels that the hemispherical cortex shows itself to be so peculiarly susceptible. The currents, once in, must find a way out. In getting out they leave their traces in the paths which they take. The only thing they can do, in short, is to deepen old paths or to make new ones; and the whole plasticity of the brain sums itself up in two words when we call it an organ in which currents pouring in from the sense-organs make with extreme facility paths which do not easily disappear.

Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent. It alone is what keeps us all within the bounds of ordinance, and saves the children of fortune from the envious uprisings of the poor. It alone prevents the hardest and most repulsive walks of life from being deserted by those brought up to tread therein. It keeps the fisherman and the deck-hand at sea through the winter; it holds the miner in his darkness, and nails the countryman to his log-cabin and his lonely farm through all the months of snow; it protects us from invasion by the natives of the desert and the frozen zone. It dooms us all to fight out the battle of life upon the lines of our nurture or our early choice, and to make the best of a pursuit that disagrees, because there is no other for which we are fitted, and it is too late to begin again. It keeps different social strata from mixing. Already at the age of twenty-five you see the professional mannerism settling down on the young commercial traveller, on the young doctor, on the young minister, on the young counsellor-at-law. You see the little lines of cleavage running through the character, the tricks of thought, the prejudices, the ways of the 'shop,' in a word, from which the man can by-and-by no more escape than his coat-sleeve can suddenly fall into a new set of folds. On the whole, it is best he should not escape. It is well for the world that in most of us, by the age of thirty, the character has set like plaster, and will never soften again.

The great thing, then, in all education, is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy. It is to fund and capitalize our acquisitions, and live at ease upon the interest of the fund. For this we must make automatic and habitual, as early as possible, as many useful actions as we can, and guard against the growing into ways that are likely to be disadvantageous to us, as we should guard against the plague. The more of the details of our daily life we can hand over to the effortless custody of automatism, the more our higher powers of mind will be set free for their own proper work. There is no more miserable human being than one in whom nothing is habitual but indecision, and for whom the lighting of every cigar, the drinking of every cup, the time of rising and going to bed every day, and the beginning of every bit of work, are subjects of express volitional deliberation. Full half the time of such a man goes to the deciding, or regretting, of matters which ought to be so ingrained in him as practically not to exist for his consciousness at all. If there be such daily duties not yet ingrained in any one of my readers, let him begin this very hour to set the matter right.

In Professor Bain's chapter on 'The Moral Habits' there are some admirable practical remarks laid down. Two great maxims emerge from his treatment. The first is that in the acquisition of a new habit, or the leaving off of an old one, we must take care to launch ourselves with as strong and decided an initiative as possible. Accumulate all the possible circumstances which shall re-enforce the right motives; put yourself assiduously in conditions that encourage the new way; make engagements incompatible with the old; take a public pledge, if the case allows; in short, envelop your resolution with every aid you know. This will give your new beginning such a momentum that the temptation to break down will not occur as soon as it otherwise might; and every day during which a breakdown is postponed adds to the chances of its not occurring at all.

The second maxim is: Never suffer an exception to occur till the new habit is securely rooted in your life. Each lapse is like the letting fall of a ball of string which one is carefully winding up; a single slip undoes more than a great many turns will wind again. Continuity of training is the great means of making the nervous system act infallibly right.

A third maxim may be added to the preceding pair: Seize the very first possible opportunity to act on every resolution you make, and on every emotional prompting you may experience in the direction of the habits you aspire to gain. It is not in the moment of their forming, but in the moment of their producing motor effects, that resolves and aspirations communicate the new 'set' to the brain. No matter how full a reservoir of maxims one may possess, and no matter how good one's sentiments may be, if one have not taken advantage of every concrete opportunity to act, one's character may remain entirely unaffected for the better. With mere good intentions, hell is proverbially paved. And this is an obvious consequence of the principles we have laid down. A 'character,' as J. S. Mill says, 'is a completely fashioned will'; and a will, in the sense in which he means it, is an aggregate of tendencies to act in a firm and prompt and definite way upon all the principal emergencies of life. A tendency to act only becomes effectively ingrained in us in proportion to the uninterrupted frequency with which the actions actually occur, and the brain 'grows' to their use. Every time a resolve or a fine glow of feeling evaporates without bearing practical fruit is worse than a chance lost; it works so as positively to hinder future resolutions and emotions from taking the normal path of discharge. There is no more contemptible type of human character than that of the nerveless sentimentalist and dreamer, who spends his life in a weltering sea of sensibility and emotion, but who never does a manly concrete deed. Rousseau, inflaming all the mothers of France, by his eloquence, to follow Nature and nurse their babies themselves, while he sends his own children to the foundling hospital, is the classical example of what I mean.

Keep the faculty of effort alive in you by a little gratuitous exercise every day. That is, be systematically ascetic or heroic in little unnecessary points, do every day or two something for no other reason than that you would rather not do it, so that when the hour of dire need draws nigh, it may find you not unnerved and untrained to stand the test. Asceticism of this sort is like the insurance which a man pays on his house and goods. The tax does him no good at the time, and possibly may never bring him a return. But if the fire does come, his having paid it will be his salvation from ruin. So with the man who has daily inured himself to habits of concentrated attention, energetic volition, and self-denial in unnecessary things. He will stand like a tower when everything rocks around him, and when his softer fellow-mortals are winnowed like chaff in the blast.

The physiological study of mental conditions is thus the most powerful ally of hortatory ethics. The hell to be endured hereafter, of which theology tells, is no worse than the hell we make for ourselves in this world by habitually fashioning our characters in the wrong way. Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits, they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state. We are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone. Every smallest stroke of virtue or of vice leaves its never so little scar. The drunken Rip Van Winkle, in Jefferson's play, excuses himself for every fresh dereliction by saying, 'I won't count this time!' Well! he may not count it, and a kind Heaven may not count it; but it is being counted none the less. Down among his nerve-cells and fibres the molecules are counting it, registering and storing it up to be used against him when the next temptation comes. Nothing we ever do is, in strict scientific literalness, wiped out. Of course, this has its good side as well as its bad one. As we become permanent drunkards by so many separate drinks, so we become saints in the moral, and authorities and experts in the practical and scientific spheres, by so many separate acts and hours of work. Let no youth have any anxiety about the upshot of his education, whatever the line of it may be. If he keep faithfully busy each hour of the working-day, he may safely leave the final result to itself. He can with perfect certainty count on waking up some fine morning, to find himself one of the competent ones of his generation, in whatever pursuit he may have singled out. Silently, between all the details of his business, the power of judging in all that class of matter will have built itself up within him as a possession that will never pass away. Young people should know this truth in advance. The ignorance of it has probably engendered more discouragement and faint-heartedness in youths embarking on arduous careers than all other causes put together.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

James begins from a simple observation: living creatures are "bundles of habits." Watch an animal and you see a round of routine. Watch yourself and you will see the same: the way you dress, speak, walk, think. His question is what habit actually is, and his answer is physical. The brain is made of living material that is "plastic," meaning it can take a shape and keep it. Just as a sheet of paper, once folded, tends to fold along the same crease, nerve tissue that has carried a current once finds it easier to carry it again. He sums it up: the brain is "an organ in which currents pouring in from the sense-organs make with extreme facility paths which do not easily disappear." Repeated actions wear paths, and the paths make the next repetition easier.

From that single idea, James builds a whole picture of society and character. Habit, he says, is "the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent." A flywheel is a heavy wheel that keeps a machine turning steadily. Habit keeps people at their jobs, in their routines, within the bounds of ordinary life. It keeps the fisherman going out in the cold and the laborer at his trade, which is why the world does not collapse into chaos. He also says that by the age of thirty "the character has set like plaster, and will never soften again." That is a striking line, and we will come back to whether it is true.

Then the chapter turns to advice, and this is where it becomes memorable. The great thing in education, he says, is "to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy." Think of habit as a bank account. Make useful actions automatic early, and you "live at ease upon the interest of the fund." The more details of daily life we hand over to automatic routine, the more mental energy remains for higher things. People who have to decide every morning whether to get up or how to dress are wasting their best attention.

Then come the rules. Start a new habit with as strong and decided an initiative as possible. Never let an exception occur until the habit is rooted, because a lapse is like dropping a ball of string you are carefully winding: "a single slip undoes more than a great many turns will wind again." Seize every chance to act on a resolution, since good intentions alone do nothing; "hell is proverbially paved" with them. And do something every day or two "for no other reason than that you would rather not do it," so that when real hardship comes you stand "like a tower."

The final point is moral and sobering. Every action leaves a trace. "We are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone." Even the small lapse leaves "its never so little scar." That is the layer first-time readers sometimes miss: this is not only a productivity manual, but a warning that you are always becoming someone.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As popular science writing, "Habit" is superb. James does what the best explainers do: he finds a handful of images and sticks to them. The paper crease, the flywheel, the bank account, the ball of string, the plaster setting. Each one is physical, each can be remembered in a sentence, and together they cover the whole argument. The ball of string in particular is a gem, because it captures something true and counterintuitive about habit formation: the cost of a lapse is out of proportion to the lapse itself.

His structure is also admirable. He moves from a general fact, to a physical mechanism, to a social consequence, to a personal program, and each step feels earned. The chapter reads like a lecture from a charismatic teacher who is trying not only to inform but to change what you do tomorrow morning. There is real humor, too, in the portrait of the dreamer who is moved to tears by a tragedy at the theater but never helps anyone, and in the swipe at Rousseau, who urged French mothers to nurse their babies while sending his own children to a foundling hospital.

But the chapter has weaknesses that become clearer with time. The neurological language is more confident than the evidence of 1890 could support. James had no way of seeing neurons change, and "paths" and "currents" are metaphors, not findings. He is guessing intelligently, and he turned out to be broadly right, but it is a guess. A reader who treats the chapter as a scientific report, and not as a brilliant piece of reasoning, will be misled.

The claim that character "sets like plaster" by thirty is the clearest overstatement. It is memorable and rhetorically useful, but we now know that adult brains remain far more adaptable than that, and James himself spent much of the chapter urging people to change. There is a tension in his message: on one hand, you are shaping your fate irreversibly; on the other, here is how to remake yourself. He never fully reconciles the two, and the dire passages about irreversible scars can read as heavy-handed, even a bit Victorian in their moral gloom.

There is also a class tone. The idea that habit "saves the children of fortune from the envious uprisings of the poor" and keeps people content in their stations reveals the outlook of a comfortable professional. He calls habit a conservative force with evident approval. And the ending, which urges a kind of daily asceticism, assumes that suffering in small things is character-building in itself, an assumption many readers now question. Still, the best parts of the chapter outweigh these flaws by a wide margin.

III. The Impact It Had

The chapter's influence has been broad and long. Within psychology, it helped establish habit as a central subject. James's Principles of Psychology became the standard text of the young science in America, and this chapter was one of its most read parts. Later schools drew on it in different ways. The behaviorists, who studied learning through repeated stimulus and response, saw in James an ancestor, and his picture of paths worn by repetition anticipates what we now call learning by reinforcement. Later neuroscientists, who showed that connections between neurons strengthen with use, found that his guess about plasticity had been remarkably close. The slogan "cells that fire together wire together" is often used to summarize what James described in different words.

In philosophy, the chapter belongs to the development of pragmatism, the distinctively American school of thought, led by James, Charles Peirce and later John Dewey, that judges ideas by their consequences in practice. Dewey's book Human Nature and Conduct, published in 1922, is a long meditation on habit that clearly builds on James's work. European thinkers took notice as well, and the French philosopher Henri Bergson was in conversation with James about the nature of mental life.

Culturally, the effect has been enormous, though often invisible. When a teacher says "practice makes permanent," when a coach talks about building routines, or when a self-help book tells you to start small and never miss twice, James is in the background. Modern bestsellers on habit, such as Charles Duhigg's The Power of Habit and James Clear's Atomic Habits, cover territory this chapter mapped, and his ball-of-string maxim is essentially the "never miss twice" rule.

The chapter also shaped how educators think. James gave lectures to teachers, published as Talks to Teachers, in which he applied the psychology of habit to the classroom. The idea that education is largely a matter of building useful automatic skills, such as reading, arithmetic, and manners, owes something to him.

In the broader culture of self-improvement, the chapter gave moral authority to the idea that character is built in small daily acts. This is the basis of countless routines, from morning disciplines to training regimens. It is taught today in courses on psychology, philosophy and education, and is a frequent selection in anthologies of American essays, usually as an example of how a scientist can write for the public with force and grace.

IV. What Readers Loved

People love this chapter for its usefulness. Few pieces of nineteenth-century science can be read as a practical guide, and fewer still hold up. Readers who pick it up expecting a dry old textbook are often startled to find that its advice is exactly what they would hear from a good coach today. Start strong. Do not allow exceptions. Act on your resolutions at once. Practice small acts of effort. These rules are easy to remember and demanding to follow.

They also love the images. The ball of string is the favorite, since anyone who has tried to get up early, keep a diet, or write every day knows how a single slip can unwind a week's work. The bank account of habit, from which we "live at ease upon the interest," gives a hopeful, almost financial way to think about discipline: it is an investment, not a punishment. The flywheel gives a sense of the dignity of routine. These are metaphors a reader can carry into the day.

Another source of affection is James's tone. He writes like a friend who is on your side, warm and direct, with flashes of humor. He addresses the reader openly: "let him begin this very hour." He does not scold from a height; he explains why the mechanism works so that you can use it. Many readers say that learning the reason behind the advice, that your brain is actually changing, made the advice feel more real.

The chapter has also been loved for its hope. Its final warning about irreversible scars could be read as grim, but the heart of the chapter is that you can make your own nervous system an ally. The reader who is stuck is told that the stuckness is not fate but a pattern, and patterns are made of repetition. That is why it has so often been quoted to people in difficult times. James himself had climbed out of a deep despair by acting as if his choices counted, and readers sense that the chapter comes from someone who has tested it on himself.

Finally, writers and scientists have admired the ambition: a short chapter that links the behavior of atoms, the structure of the brain, the order of society and the building of a personal character. It is rare to find an essay that moves so confidently from the small to the large.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The pushback on "Habit" falls into a few groups. The first is scientific. Later researchers pointed out that James's picture of the brain was speculative and that his claims about fixed paths were too simple. Habit formation, it turns out, involves several brain systems, and habits can be changed later in life far more readily than "set like plaster" suggests. The idea that adults cannot change after thirty has been widely rejected, and many modern writers on habit treat the line as an example of how not to think about aging. Some also note that modern popular claims, such as that it takes a fixed number of days to form a habit, are not found in James and have little evidence behind them; his version is more careful than much of what has followed.

The second is philosophical. James was a champion of free will, yet his chapter leans heavily on a mechanical picture of the nervous system. Critics have asked whether he could consistently believe both that we are bundles of habit and that we freely choose our paths. He took the question seriously, and elsewhere in the Principles he defends the power of attention and effort, but the chapter itself does not resolve it, and some readers feel it hovers between determinism and exhortation.

The third is moral and social. The statement that habit keeps the poor from rising against the rich has struck many later readers as complacent. James presents habit as a conservative force that holds society together, and his picture of people settling into their stations by thirty implies a rather fixed social order. Readers sensitive to inequality have noticed how much of the chapter assumes an educated reader with the freedom to arrange his own days. For a worker with unpredictable shifts or a caregiver with no free hour, advice to begin "this very hour" may feel remote.

There is also the charge of moralism. The ascetic advice to do something every day because you would rather not has been criticized as joyless, and the language about hell and scars can feel like a sermon in scientific dress. Modern psychologists often prefer to emphasize making good behavior easy and pleasant, not building willpower through small hardships.

Finally, some critics see a certain smugness in the swipes at "sentimentalists" who feel deeply but do nothing. The attack on Rousseau, though funny, is personal, and it contains an idea, that feeling without action corrupts, which some think is too harsh on emotional people. These objections do not undo the chapter's value, but they remind us that it is a lively and opinionated work, not a neutral report.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

James's "Habit" sits at the crossroads of science, philosophy and moral advice. Its deepest ancestor is Aristotle, who argued in the Nicomachean Ethics that we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts, so that character is built by repetition. The philosopher David Hume wrote of "custom" as the great guide of human life, and the British psychologist Alexander Bain, whom James cites in the text, laid out practical maxims for forming moral habits that James adopts and sharpens. What is new in James is the physical grounding: he takes the old moral doctrine and ties it to the living tissue of the brain, at a moment when physiology was just becoming a science.

Its descendants are plentiful. In psychology, it feeds into behaviorism and learning theory, and later into cognitive neuroscience. In philosophy, it is part of pragmatism, with Dewey's later book on habit its most direct heir. In public life, it underlies the modern self-help and productivity industry, as well as serious fields like habit-formation research in public health.

Comparing it with the other essays on reading and attention is rewarding. Bacon, in "Of Studies," made a very similar claim three centuries earlier, that studies "pass into manners," meaning that what you read and practice becomes who you are. James supplies the mechanism that Bacon only asserted. Montaigne's runaway mind, galloping without direction, is what James would call a nervous system without grooves, and Montaigne's remedy of writing is a habit in James's sense. Emerson's call for an original relation to the universe depends on a self not dragged by routine, and James would say you earn that freedom by automating the routine so that attention is free. And Woolf's description of how to read as an acquired skill, built with practice, is a Jamesian picture too.

Why still read it? Because it is one of the few older science texts that is also a manual for living. It says clearly what many people now spend money to learn: that the quality of a life is the sum of what you do repeatedly. It offers a way of thinking about change that is neither magical nor hopeless. And it is a pleasure to read, direct, witty and humane. You may disagree with details, but you will probably find yourself noticing, tomorrow morning, which of your actions are paths and which are choices.
