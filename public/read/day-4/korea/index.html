Daylighting Cheonggyecheon: How Seoul Tore Down a Highway to Resurrect a Joseon Stream

Korea — The 2003–2005 urban miracle that demolished 5.8 kilometers of elevated concrete expressway and dropped downtown Seoul's temperature by 3.6°C () • 20 min read

“A city that buries its rivers in concrete buries its own lungs; a city that uncovers its water learns to breathe again.” — Seoul Urban Restoration White Paper (2005)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Between July 2003 and October 2005, the megacity of Seoul executed one of the boldest urban ecological reversals of the twenty-first century: demolishing 5.8 kilometers of sixteen-meter-high elevated concrete highway that carried 168,000 vehicles a day in order to daylight Cheonggyecheon ('Clear Valley Stream'), the historic waterway of Joseon-era Hanyang. Originally dredged in 1411 under King Taejong and entombed beneath concrete slabs during South Korea's post-war industrialization between 1958 and 1976, the resurrected stream defied predictions of traffic gridlock when paired with Bus Rapid Transit reforms. Uncovering the buried river and its fifteenth-century Gwangtonggyo stone bridge cooled downtown summer temperatures by 3.6°C, restored natural wind corridors, and increased urban wildlife biodiversity more than sixfold.

Step down the granite stairs at Cheonggye Plaza in the heart of downtown Seoul just before dusk, and within thirty paces the roar of the twenty-five-million-person megacity softens into the sound of tumbling water. Five meters below the glass canyons of Sejong-daero and Jongno, clear water cascades over a two-tiered porphyry waterfall and winds eastward for 5.84 kilometers beneath twenty-two bridges. Office workers loosen their ties and cross the stream on rough-hewn Pocheon granite stepping stones (*jinggeomdari*); little egrets and striated herons stand motionless in the riffles stalking *shiri* minnows; and weeping willows and silver reeds brush the stone embankments. Yet if you had stood on these exact geographic coordinates in the stifling August heat of 2002, no sunlight or bird could have reached you. Directly overhead loomed the sixteen-meter-high, four-lane elevated concrete viaduct of the *Cheonggye Gogadoro*, shuddering beneath the weight of 168,000 cars and diesel trucks every twenty-four hours, while below your feet a ten-lane surface highway capped a hermetically sealed concrete box culvert choking on raw sewage, methane, and hydrogen sulfide gas.

The story of how Seoul entombed its ancient central waterway under half a million tons of twentieth-century concrete—and then summoned the audacity to tear that highway down and bring the stream back to the light of day—is the six-hundred-year biography of Korea's capital itself. When General Yi Seong-gye (King Taejo) founded the Joseon Dynasty in 1392 and searched for a new royal capital to replace Gaegyeong (modern Kaesong), his royal geomancers—including the Buddhist monk Muhak and the Neo-Confucian state architect Jeong Do-jeon—selected the basin of Hanyang in 1394 because it embodied the sacred topography of *pungsu-jiri* (Korean geomancy) and *baesan-imsu* ('mountains at the back, water in front'). Ringed like a lotus blossom by four inner guardian mountains (*Naesasan*): steep granite Bugaksan to the north, wooded Namsan to the south, Naksan ('Camel Mountain') to the east, and craggy Inwangsan to the west, the bowl of Hanyang collected dozens of clear mountain brooks into a single west-to-east central stream.

Known throughout the Joseon era as *Gaecheon* (개천, 'Open Stream'—from which the modern Korean idiom *Gaecheon-eseo yong nanda*, 'A dragon rises from a small stream,' meaning a great leader born in humble circumstances, derives), this 10.9-kilometer watercourse gathered the valleys of Baegundong and Samcheongdong before flowing east through the capital's five districts to pass beneath the five-arched floodgates of *Ogan-sumun* in the eastern city wall, joining Jungnangcheon and finally emptying into the Han River. Because Korea's climate is governed by dry springs and ferocious July monsoon cloudbursts (*jangma*), *Gaecheon* was naturally a seasonal torrent: a shallow, braided gravel brook for ten months of the year that transformed overnight during summer rains into a raging flood threatening the wooden palaces and thatched homes of the capital.

In 1411, the third Joseon monarch, King Taejong, established the *Gaecheon Dogam* (Directorate for Stream Works) and mobilized 52,800 conscripted laborers from Gyeongsang, Jeolla, and Chungcheong provinces to dredge the channel, reinforce its banks with timber pilings and stone walls, and replace washed-out wooden crossings with permanent granite bridges. Foremost among these was *Gwangtonggyo* (광통교, 'Broad Crossing Bridge'), completed in 1410 near the main commercial avenue of Unjongga (modern Jongno). In a ruthless display of dynastic politics, Taejong ordered his masons to strip the exquisitely carved granite foundation stones—adorned with scrolling clouds, lotus petals, and horned *bixie* guardian beasts—directly from *Jeongneung*, the royal tomb of his hated stepmother Queen Sindeok, so that every citizen and horse in Hanyang would trample over her stones daily.

A decade later, in 1420, Taejong's son King Sejong the Great—working to systematize hydrological science across the kingdom—erected *Supyogyo* (수표교, 'Water-Marker Bridge') further downstream and planted a three-meter-high hexagonal granite obelisk (*Supyo*) directly in the riverbed beside it. Incised with precise graduations in Joseon *cheok* (feet), *chon* (inches), and *pun* (tenths), ranging from one to ten *cheok*, the *Supyo* allowed royal hydrologists in the Ministry of Public Works to monitor real-time flood stages and issue evacuation warnings—making fifteenth-century *Gaecheon* one of the earliest quantitatively instrumented urban watersheds on earth. When Neo-Confucian purists led by Eo Hyo-chom (어효첨) debated in 1444 whether the stream should be kept pristine as sacred geomantic water (*myeongdangsu*) or used as a practical municipal drain to carry away the household waste of 100,000 residents, Sejong pragmatically ruled that a living city required an open, breathing drainage artery.

Across five centuries of Joseon life, *Gaecheon* served as both the hydraulic spine and the social commons of Hanyang. Dividing the northern aristocratic neighborhoods (*Bukchon*, home to high court ministers near Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung Palaces) from the southern quarters (*Namchon*) and the middle-class translators, physicians, and astronomers (*Jungin*) who clustered right along its bridges, the stream was where commoner women beat laundry with wooden paddles (*dadeumi*), children caught minnows, and merchants unloaded grain and salt. By the eighteenth century, however, deforestation on the surrounding hills had washed so much granite sand into the streambed that monsoon waters overflowed into neighboring homes every summer. In 1760, sixty-six-year-old King Yeongjo launched the most ambitious public works project of the late Joseon era: the *Gyeongjin Juncheon* (Great Dredging of the Year Gyeongjin). Over fifty-seven days between February and April 1760, some 200,000 citizens, soldiers, and paid laborers excavated the choked channel with iron-shod wooden shovels (*garae*) towed by ropes, straightened the banks with dressed granite blocks (*seokchuk*), and planted willow groves to anchor the soil—a civic triumph commemorated in the vivid court painting album *Juncheon-cheop*. Every year thereafter on the first full moon of the lunar calendar (*Jeongwol Daeboreum*), thousands of Seoulites dressed in silk robes crowded onto Gwangtonggyo and Supyogyo after midnight to 'tread the bridges' (*dari-bapgi*), believing that walking across twelve stone spans under the full moon would keep their legs strong for the coming year.

The twentieth century turned that vibrant civic stream into an open wound. During the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945), the Government-General renamed *Gaecheon* as *Cheonggyecheon* ('Clear Valley Stream'), even as encroaching tenements and factories turned the water black and colonial urban planners drafted the first abortive blueprints to pave it over. Then came the cataclysm of the Korean War (1950–1953), during which Seoul changed hands four times and was reduced to smoldering rubble. As millions of destitute war refugees poured into the ruined capital from the north and the countryside, tens of thousands nailed together precarious shanties of ammunition crates, flattened tin cans, and tar paper (*panjatchon*) perched on wooden stilts directly over the banks of Cheonggyecheon. With no working sewers, garbage collection, or clean running water, the stream became a stagnant, cholera- and typhus-breeding mire.

To the post-war authoritarian governments of Syngman Rhee and General Park Chung-hee, the open, foul-smelling stream and its ramshackle stilt slums were an intolerable badge of national humiliation and poverty. Beginning in 1958 and continuing in stages until 1977, municipal crews bulldozed the wooden shanties and entombed the entire length of Cheonggyecheon beneath a massive, fifty-to-eighty-meter-wide box girder of reinforced concrete. Not content with merely burying the water, Seoul's 'Bulldozer Mayor' Kim Hyun-ok launched construction in August 1967 on the *Cheonggye Gogadoro*—a 5.8-kilometer, sixteen-meter-high elevated concrete expressway straddling the buried stream deck on twin rows of heavy concrete piers. When the main ramp opened on March 22, 1969, South Korean newsreels and cinema celebrated the gleaming elevated freeway as the supreme monument of the 'Miracle on the Han River,' a futuristic skyway carrying exports and automobiles out of the ashes of war.

Beneath the dark shadow of the concrete viaduct, a remarkable blue-collar industrial ecosystem sprang to life. Along the narrow alleys of Euljiro, Sewoon Sangga (the kilometer-long megastructure designed by architect Kim Swoo-geun in 1967), Cheonggye 3-ga through 8-ga, and the sprawling textile hives of Pyeonghwa Market and Dongdaemun, more than 60,000 tiny machine shops, metal foundries, electrical component wholesalers, toolmakers, neon-sign benders, and garment lofts packed shoulder to shoulder. Machinists in the grease-stained alleys of Cheonggyecheon boasted that if you handed them a blueprint at dawn, they could pass parts from lathe to welding torch down a single block and assemble a working tank or airplane by nightfall. Yet the human and environmental toll inside that concrete canyon was staggering: in the windowless garment attics of Pyeonghwa Market along Cheonggyecheon—where teenage girls sewed fifteen hours a day breathing fabric dust—twenty-two-year-old tailor Jeon Tae-il set himself ablaze in November 1970 holding a copy of the Labor Standards Act, igniting South Korea's modern labor movement.

By the early 1990s, the concrete monument itself was dying from the inside out. Beneath the surface road, trapped hydrogen sulfide and methane gases rising from the anaerobic sewer culvert reacted with moisture to form sulfuric acid that ate into the underside of the concrete deck, while winter de-icing salts seeping down from the elevated highway corroded the high-tensile steel prestressing tendons inside the piers. When the Korean Society of Civil Engineers conducted a comprehensive structural safety audit between 1991 and 1997—shortly after the traumatic collapses of Seoul's Seongsu Bridge in 1994 and Sampoong Department Store in 1995—they delivered a grim verdict: the Cheonggye Expressway was suffering from severe structural fatigue. Passenger cars only were permitted on the upper deck after 1997, trucks over 1.5 tons were banned, and engineers estimated that merely patching and replacing the corroded deck panels would cost well over 100 billion won while paralyzing downtown traffic for years.

It was at a small academic symposium on September 1, 2000, that a group of visionary professors and urban researchers—calling themselves the *Cheonggyecheon Research Group*, anchored by Yonsei University environmental engineer Professor Noh Soo-hong and transportation planner Dr. Kee Yeon Hwang of the Seoul Development Institute—floated an idea that initially struck city hall bureaucrats as sheer lunacy. Why spend 100 billion won repairing an ugly, crumbling 1960s concrete viaduct over a dead sewer? Why not demolish the entire 5.8-kilometer elevated freeway, rip open the ten-lane surface road beneath it, excavate the buried Joseon bridges, and 'daylight' Cheonggyecheon as a living ecological river park right through the center of the capital?

In 2002, former corporate executive Lee Myung-bak made the restoration of Cheonggyecheon the centerpiece of his campaign for Mayor of Seoul. Traditional traffic engineers and political opponents predicted an urban catastrophe: removing a downtown corridor that carried 168,000 vehicles a day on its upper and lower decks, they warned, would trigger permanent gridlock across the metropolitan region and bankrupt the 60,000 shopkeepers and 220,000 workers of the Cheonggyecheon Merchant Association. Upon taking office on July 1, 2002, the new administration established a three-pillared institutional machinery on his very first morning: the Cheonggyecheon Restoration Headquarters (staffed by elite city engineers), the Cheonggyecheon Restoration Research Corps at the Seoul Development Institute (led by Dr. Kee Yeon Hwang), and a Citizens' Committee comprising civic leaders, ecologists, historians, and urbanists.

Instead of bulldozing over merchant opposition as authoritarian regimes had done in the 1960s, the city waged an unprecedented campaign of democratic negotiation. Over twelve months prior to demolition, municipal officials held more than 4,200 face-to-face meetings—in wholesale markets, machine-shop alleys, and late-night soup restaurants—with tool vendors, textile merchants, and street hawkers. To ensure the project did not destroy livelihoods, the city committed 70 billion won in low-interest business stabilization loans, remodeled existing market arcades with modern lighting and ventilation, and constructed the massive Garden Five commercial logistics complex in southeastern Seoul (Munjeong-dong) to relocate displaced industrial and wholesale tenants.

Simultaneously, Dr. Kee Yeon Hwang and Seoul's transportation planners bet the city's mobility on a bold application of Dietrich Braess's 1968 mathematical traffic paradox—the counterintuitive theorem proving that removing road capacity from a congested network, when paired with high-capacity public transit, can actually improve overall traffic flow by eliminating bottlenecks and inducing mode shift. Rather than building an expensive underground highway tunnel to replace the demolished viaduct, Seoul left only two-lane one-way service streets along the upper banks of the stream and, on July 1, 2004, completely revolutionized its public transit system: carving dedicated center-median Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) lanes across major arteries, reorganizing 400 chaotic private bus routes into four color-coded tiers (Blue trunk buses, Green feeder buses, Red metropolitan express buses, and Yellow downtown loops), and launching the T-money contactless smart card with free transfers between buses and subways.

At 5:00 a.m. on July 1, 2003, heavy machinery rolled onto the closed Cheonggye Expressway. Because detonating dynamite in the middle of dense commercial high-rises would have shattered thousands of shop windows and damaged buried utility lines, engineers used industrial diamond-impregnated wire saws and giant hydraulic crushers to slice the concrete deck and piers silently into blocks. Over just twenty-seven months, crews removed 620,000 tons of reinforced concrete and 289,000 tons of asphalt—recycling 100 percent of the 36,000 tons of scrap steel rebar at ironworks and crushing 96 percent of the concrete into roadbed sub-base aggregate. As the concrete lid was peeled back for the first time in nearly half a century, archaeologists from the Seoul Museum of History descended into the dark silt and discovered a time capsule: the intact, intricately carved 1410 Joseon granite piers and cloud-scroll panels of *Gwangtonggyo Bridge*, along with stretches of King Yeongjo's 1760 dressed-stone embankments (*seokchuk*). Carefully cleaning and numbering each stone, conservators reconstructed *Gwangtonggyo* 155 meters upstream of its original site so it could stand clear of floodwaters.

To solve the hydrological challenge of keeping a naturally seasonal stream flowing clear twelve months a year while surviving Korea's ferocious summer monsoons, engineers designed a dual-layered hydraulic system. Beneath the two-lane bankside streets, the recessed channel was sculpted with stepped stone terraces wide enough to contain a 200-year catastrophic flood event of 118 millimeters of rain per hour—allowing high monsoon waters to submerge the lower pedestrian walkways safely without ever spilling onto the city streets above. For dry seasons, engineers laid an underground pipeline pumping 120,000 tons of purified water every day—98,000 tons drawn from the Jayang intake station on the Han River and treated at the Ttukdo purification plant, plus 22,000 tons of clean groundwater collected from thirteen nearby Line 5 subway stations—maintaining a constant, sparkling forty-centimeter flow. When the restored Cheonggyecheon opened to the public on October 1, 2005, every doomsday traffic prediction evaporated: downtown car speeds actually ticked upward as commuters shifted to BRT buses and subways, summer ambient temperatures along the stream corridor dropped by an average of 3.6°C (6.5°F) compared to parallel asphalt avenues four blocks away, cool breeze velocities down the valley rose by fifty percent, PM10 particulate pollution fell by more than a third, and biodiversity surged from 98 pre-restoration species to 626 species of fish, birds, amphibians, insects, and riparian plants—inspiring cities from Madrid and Paris to Seattle and Tokyo to tear down their own twentieth-century highways and let their buried rivers breathe.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. The Physics of 'Braess's Paradox' and Induced Demand in Reverse

Transportation planners around the world study Cheonggyecheon as the most dramatic real-world confirmation of 'Braess's Paradox' and 'evaporated traffic.' For decades, conventional traffic engineering assumed that removing a highway carrying 168,000 vehicles per day would cause gridlock on surrounding streets. Instead, when Seoul paired the highway demolition with dedicated median bus lanes (BRT) and integrated subway transfers, discretionary car trips simply vanished or shifted modes. Cheonggyecheon directly inspired highway-removal and river-daylighting projects from Madrid's Río Manzanares and Paris's Seine embankments to Seattle's Alaskan Way Viaduct. 2. Urban Microclimate & Biodiversity Engineering

By carving a recessed green canyon five meters below street level across twenty-two bridges, landscape architects created a natural thermal corridor. Meteorological sensors placed along Cheonggyecheon documented a 3.6°C temperature drop during August heatwaves and a 50 percent increase in wind speed along the stream compared to 1970s concrete conditions. Stepped embankments were engineered to handle 200-year monsoon flood events—submerging the lower pedestrian walkways during torrential July rains while tiered wetlands purify runoff and shelter mandarin ducks, common kingfishers, and Korean spotted barbels. 3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Three terms unlock the layered history of the stream: first, Gaecheon (개천, 'Open Stream'), the Joseon-era name reflecting King Taejong and King Yeongjo's view that clearing the capital's waterway was a sacred duty of benevolent governance; second, Gwangtonggyo (광통교), the 1410 stone bridge whose exquisite cloud-and-lotus carvings survived fifty years buried under asphalt and can be touched today near Jonggak; and third, Jinggeomdari (징검다리), the traditional stepping-stone crossings that invite office workers to slow their pace and hear rushing water.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Start at the spiral stone monument ('Spring' by Claes Oldenburg) at Cheonggye Plaza (37.5691° N, 126.9776° E) at dusk and walk east along the water's edge. Within your first kilometer you will pass under the reconstructed 1410 Gwangtonggyo Bridge and the ceramic-tile wall painting of King Jeongjo's 1795 royal procession to Hwaseong. Continue past Euljiro and Dongdaemun to the Cheonggyecheon Museum in Seongdong-gu, where two original weathered concrete pillars from the demolished 1970s elevated highway have been left standing directly in the water as a monument to the city's transformation.
