Daylighting Cheonggyecheon: How Seoul Tore Down a Highway to Resurrect a Joseon Stream

Korea — The 2003–2005 urban miracle that demolished 5.8 kilometers of elevated concrete expressway and dropped downtown Seoul's temperature by 3.6°C (Joseon Dynasty to Contemporary Seoul (1411–2005)) • 10 min read

“A city that buries its rivers in concrete buries its own lungs; a city that uncovers its water learns to breathe again.” — Seoul Urban Restoration White Paper (2005)

Stand today at Cheonggye Plaza in the heart of downtown Seoul, twenty meters below the glass skyscrapers of Gwanghwamun, and you can step across rough-hewn granite stepping stones (*jinggeomdari*) while little egrets stalk minnows through clear running water and willow branches brush your shoulders. Yet if you had stood on this exact spot in the summer of 2002, you would have been choking on diesel exhaust beneath a sixteen-meter-high, four-lane elevated concrete highway (*Cheonggye Gogadoro*) thundered over by 168,000 cars and trucks every single day, atop a sealed concrete box culvert hiding a toxic sewer.

To understand how Cheonggyecheon ('Clear Valley Stream') died and was reborn is to read the six-hundred-year biography of Seoul itself. When King Taejo founded Hanyang as the capital of the Joseon Dynasty in 1394, royal geomancers (*pungsu-jiri* masters) selected the basin because it was cradled by four guardian mountains (*Naesasan*) and bisected from west to east by a natural seasonal waterway—then called *Gaecheon* ('Open Stream')—that collected runoff from Mount Inwangsan and Mount Bugaksan and carried it ten kilometers east into the Jungnangcheon and the Han River.

In 1411, King Taejong mobilized 52,800 laborers to dredge the stream, line its embankments, and build stone bridges such as *Supyogyo* ('Water-Marker Bridge,' whose stone pillar measured flood depths in Joseon尺 units) and *Gwangtonggyo*, constructed using intricately carved foundation stones recycled from the tomb of Queen Sindeok. For five centuries during the Joseon era, the stream served as the social and hydrological spine of the capital: royal dredging projects under King Yeongjo in 1760 employed 200,000 citizens over fifty-seven days—recorded in vibrant court paintings—while commoners washed linen on its stones and flew kites from its bridges during the first full moon of the lunar year.

Then came the devastation of the Korean War (1950–1953). Millions of refugees flooded into a bombed-out Seoul, erecting precarious wooden shanties on wooden stilts directly over the banks of Cheonggyecheon. Without sewage infrastructure, the stream became an open, disease-ridden cesspool. Between 1958 and 1977, under the rapid industrialization drive of President Park Chung-hee’s 'Miracle on the Han River,' the city entombed the entire stream beneath a 5.8-kilometer slab of reinforced concrete and, between 1967 and 1976, erected the elevated Cheonggye Expressway directly on top of it.

At the time, the concrete highway was hailed as a triumph of modern engineering—Korean filmmakers even shot movies of couples driving convertibles along the elevated ramp as a symbol of escaping post-war poverty. Around the concrete pillars below, a sprawling ecosystem of 60,000 small machine shops, tool vendors, lighting wholesalers, and textile stalls took root in Sewoon Sangga and Dongdaemun. Yet by the late 1990s, structural engineers discovered that salt, moisture, and hydrogen sulfide gas rising from the buried sewer beneath had severely corroded the steel rebar inside the highway piers. Keeping the expressway standing would cost over 100 billion won in emergency repairs alone.

In 2002, a group of urban engineers and environmental scientists led by Professor Noh Soo-hong and Kee Yeon Hwang proposed a heresy that sounded like madness to traffic planners: instead of repairing the highway, tear the entire 5.8-kilometer elevated freeway down, rip up the ten-lane surface road beneath it, and 'daylight' the buried Joseon stream. When Seoul Mayor Lee Myung-bak made the demolition his signature initiative in July 2003, critics predicted an apocalyptic 'traffic Armageddon' that would paralyze the economic heart of South Korea, while the 220,000 merchants along the corridor staged massive protests fearing ruin.

The city responded with surgical coordination on two fronts: democratic negotiation and transit redesign. Municipal teams held over 4,200 face-to-face meetings with Cheonggyecheon merchants—negotiating relocation complexes, low-interest business loans, and phased construction schedules—while simultaneously overhauling Seoul’s entire public transit network with dedicated median Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) lanes, color-coded buses, and a unified smart-card transfer system (T-money). Instead of building another highway to replace the lost capacity, Seoul reduced downtown car lanes and moved commuters onto buses and subways.

Over twenty-seven months between July 2003 and September 2005, engineers diamond-sawed and removed 620,000 tons of concrete and asphalt—recycling 96 percent of the scrap steel and concrete aggregate. When archaeologists excavated the riverbed, they uncovered the intact fifteenth-century carved stones of *Gwangtonggyo Bridge*, buried beneath sixty years of silt and concrete, and reconstructed the bridge 150 meters upstream. To ensure the naturally seasonal stream flowed year-round even during dry winters, engineers laid a pipeline pumping 120,000 tons of treated water daily from the Han River and subway groundwater stations.

When the restored 5.84-kilometer Cheonggyecheon opened on October 1, 2005, the results stunned urban planners worldwide. Not only did the predicted traffic collapse never materialize—average vehicle speeds in downtown Seoul actually *increased* as drivers shifted to transit—but the 'urban heat island' temperature along the corridor dropped by an average of 3.6°C (6.5°F) compared to parallel asphalt avenues just four blocks away. Prevailing winds funneled cool air along the water channel, reducing particulate air pollution by 35 percent, while biodiversity surged from 98 species before restoration to more than 626 species of fish, birds, insects, and plants—proving that a twenty-first-century megacity can heal the wounds of twentieth-century concrete.

1. The Physics of 'Braess's Paradox' and Induced Demand in Reverse

Transportation planners around the world study Cheonggyecheon as the most dramatic real-world confirmation of 'Braess's Paradox' and 'evaporated traffic.' For decades, conventional traffic engineering assumed that removing a highway carrying 168,000 vehicles per day would cause gridlock on surrounding streets. Instead, when Seoul paired the highway demolition with dedicated median bus lanes (BRT) and integrated subway transfers, discretionary car trips simply vanished or shifted modes. Cheonggyecheon directly inspired highway-removal and river-daylighting projects from Madrid's Río Manzanares and Paris's Seine embankments to Seattle's Alaskan Way Viaduct.
