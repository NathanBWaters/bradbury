Bradbury 1000 • Night 4 • Nightly Poem

The Tyger

William Blake (1794) • 4 min read

Fourteen unanswered questions hammered out in a cosmic smithy confront the terrifying symmetry of ferocity and creation.

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,

In the forests of the night;

What immortal hand or eye,

Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies.

Burnt the fire of thine eyes?

On what wings dare he aspire?

What the hand, dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder, & what art,

Could twist the sinews of thy heart?

And when thy heart began to beat,

What dread hand? & what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain,

In what furnace was thy brain?

What the anvil? what dread grasp,

Dare its deadly terrors clasp!

When the stars threw down their spears

And water'd heaven with their tears:

Did he smile his work to see?

Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Tyger Tyger burning bright,

In the forests of the night:

What immortal hand or eye,

Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

Five-Paragraph Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Historical & Biographical Context

Engraved, hand-printed, and watercolored by William Blake himself in London in 1794 as part of Songs of Innocence and of Experience Showing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul, 'The Tyger' was forged in the white-hot crucible of the Industrial Revolution and the French Revolution's Reign of Terror. Across the English Channel, revolutionary idealism had erupted into the guillotine's bloody fury; across Britain, coal-fired blast furnaces and iron foundries ('dark Satanic Mills') were transforming the landscape. Blake designed 'The Tyger' as the direct dialectical counterpart to 'The Lamb' from Songs of Innocence (1789), where a child easily answers the question 'Little Lamb, who made thee?' with gentle Christian reassurance.

2. Core Themes & Central Metaphor

In the world of Experience, the question of creation cannot be answered with Sunday-school platitudes. The poem confronts the problem of theodicy and sublime terror: how can the same cosmos—and the same Creator—produce both the vulnerable innocence of the Lamb and the lethal, breathtaking magnificence of the Tyger? Blake refuses to reduce the Tyger to mere 'evil'; instead, the creature radiates 'fearful symmetry,' a fusion of terrifying violence and supreme aesthetic order. In stanza four, the Creator is imagined as a cosmic blacksmith—simultaneously Hephaestus, Prometheus ('seize the fire'), and Blake the copperplate engraver—forging a living predator with hammer, chain, furnace, and anvil inside 'the forests of the night.'

3. Craft, Form & Poetic Technique

Acoustically, 'The Tyger' is one of the most percussive poems ever written. Blake uses catalectic trochaic tetrameter—lines beginning and ending on stressed syllables (`TY-ger TY-ger, BURN-ing BRIGHT`)—whose falling, anvil-strike rhythm mimics the blacksmith's hammer blows. The entire 24-line poem consists of fourteen rapid-fire rhetorical questions that never receive an answer. Notice the masterstroke in the final stanza, which repeats the opening stanza almost word for word, except that 'Could frame thy fearful symmetry?' becomes 'Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?' That single verb shift moves the inquiry from capability ('Could') to moral and existential audacity ('Dare'). Every stressed syllable lands like glowing iron on an anvil, turning theological terror into pure, unforgettable music that reverberates in the reader's chest long after the final question mark.

4. Critical Reception & Literary Legacy

Virtually unknown to the wider public during Blake's lifetime—when he was dismissed by many contemporaries as an eccentric artisan or madman—'The Tyger' was championed by Charles Lamb, Algernon Charles Swinburne, and W. B. Yeats in the nineteenth century and is now the most anthologized poem in the English language. Alfred Kazin and Northrop Frye analyzed it as the cornerstone of Blake's mythology of 'Contraries,' in which Innocence and Experience, reason (Urizen) and energy (Orc/Los), must coexist for progression to occur. From Jorge Luis Borges's lifelong obsession with tigers to modern literature and music, Blake's burning beast remains the ultimate emblem of untamed creative energy.

5. Bradbury Metaphor Collision & Nightly Reflection

On Night 4, Blake's cosmic forge collides with Charlotte Perkins Gilman's 'The Yellow Wallpaper' and William James's 'Habit.' In Gilman's story, a woman locked inside a patriarchal nursery sees a figure creeping behind the bars of the hideous wallpaper pattern and eventually tears the paper down to free her trapped double; in James's essay, every repeated action carves physical grooves into the plastic matter of our nervous system like molten metal cooling in a mold. Blake asks what furnace forged our brain and what 'fearful symmetry' governs our habits. Tonight, recognize that the fiery creative energy you repress in the daytime ('forests of the night') can either imprison you or be hammered into art.
