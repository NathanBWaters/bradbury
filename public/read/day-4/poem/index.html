Nightly Poem • Romantic Ode & Lyric

The Tyger

William Blake (1794) • 4 min read

Fourteen unanswered questions hammered out in a cosmic smithy confront the terrifying symmetry of ferocity and creation.

Key Context & Why It Matters

The Tyger is one of the most famous poems in English, and almost everyone who has met it remembers its first line. William Blake published it in 1794, in his book Songs of Experience, as the dark twin of an earlier book of gentle poems called Songs of Innocence. Blake was a London engraver and a visionary, who claimed to see angels and spirits, and who made his books by hand, engraving the words and pictures himself on copper plates. He wrote during the French Revolution and the first stirrings of the Industrial Revolution, when the old world seemed to be burning and a new, harsher one was being forged. The poem asks a single huge question about a tiger: what kind of creator could make something so beautiful and so terrifying? Notice that the poem never answers. It only asks, over and over, until the questions themselves become the point. Listen for the hammering rhythm. It sounds like a blacksmith at work.

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,

In the forests of the night;

What immortal hand or eye,

Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies.

Burnt the fire of thine eyes?

On what wings dare he aspire?

What the hand, dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder, & what art,

Could twist the sinews of thy heart?

And when thy heart began to beat,

What dread hand? & what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain,

In what furnace was thy brain?

What the anvil? what dread grasp,

Dare its deadly terrors clasp!

When the stars threw down their spears

And water'd heaven with their tears:

Did he smile his work to see?

Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Tyger Tyger burning bright,

In the forests of the night:

What immortal hand or eye,

Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is made almost entirely of questions, and the first thing to notice is that the speaker never answers a single one. He looks at a tiger burning bright in the forests of the night and asks who could have made it. The poem's pressure builds because every question is a little more astonished than the last.

The first stanza sets the stage. The tiger is on fire, blazing in the dark, and its body is a symmetry that is fearful, a word that can mean both frightening and full of fear. Is the tiger afraid, or are we? Blake lets the word do both jobs. The second stanza asks where the fire in its eyes was lit, in what distant deeps or skies, as though the tiger's glow came either from hell or from heaven. The third asks about the shoulder and the art that twisted the sinews of its heart, and then what dread hand and what dread feet began its heartbeat. Here the poem imagines the creator not as a gentle god but as a craftsman wrestling with raw, violent material.

The fourth stanza is the poem's centre and its best-known image. What the hammer, what the chain, in what furnace was thy brain? What the anvil? The creator becomes a blacksmith, and the tiger is forged like a weapon. This was a vivid picture in Blake's London, where the heat and noise of workshops and foundries were spreading. It is a picture of creation as hard, hot labour rather than a peaceful act.

Then the fifth stanza turns strange. When the stars threw down their spears and watered heaven with their tears: did he smile his work to see? Most readers connect this with the war in heaven, the rebel angels of Milton's Paradise Lost thrown down from the sky, though Blake leaves it open. The image of stars weeping suggests that the tiger's creation was a cosmic event, one that made even the heavens grieve. Then comes the stinging line, did he who made the Lamb make thee? This is the question the poem has been heading toward. The Lamb is the gentle creature of Blake's earlier poem, The Lamb, from Songs of Innocence, where the Lamb is praised as a symbol of Christ and of childlike goodness. The tiger is its opposite. The great puzzle is how one creator could have made both mildness and ferocity.

The final stanza repeats the first with one tiny change. Where the opening asked what hand or eye could frame the tiger, the last asks what hand or eye dare frame it. Could becomes dare. The question moves from ability to courage, from wonder to a hint of moral challenge. Who would have the nerve? And the poem stops.

The deep meaning is that it refuses to settle the problem of evil. Why does a good world contain terror? Blake does not preach an answer. He lets the tiger be both radiant and menacing, and the reader hold both at once. The poem is a hymn that is also an accusation, and it never decides which.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, the poem is close to flawless, and its great strength is momentum. The rhythm is a hard, hammering beat, with four stresses to the line, and the rhymes come in tight couplets. Read it aloud and you can hear the anvil. The form imitates the subject, which is making by force. Because the questions arrive one after another, the poem gives no room to relax.

Then there is the diction. Most of the words are plain and one syllable long. Blake does not need elaborate vocabulary because the images do the work: fire, hammer, chain, furnace, anvil. A few precise words carry big weight, fearful, dread, deadly, aspire, dare. The old spelling, Tyger, makes the animal strange, as if summoned from an older, wilder world.

The repetition is also skilful. The first and last stanzas are almost identical, which makes the poem a circle, but the one changed word turns the circle into a spiral. A modest alteration, and it adds a whole new feeling.

Blake the artist should be mentioned too. The poem was meant to be seen with its illustration, and the original plate shows a rather mild, almost bemused tiger, hardly a terrifying beast. Some viewers find the picture comic and strangely at odds with the verse. It may be Blake's irony, or it may be that he was a better poet than draughtsman of tigers, which he probably had never seen alive.

Now for the weaknesses. The middle of the poem can feel overcrowded. The images of fire, forge, hammer, chain, anvil and furnace pile up and mix somewhat. Is the creator a smith or an eye or a hand or a dread foot? The shift from one figure to another is thrilling, but it is not always coherent, and a stricter reader might object that the poem is more atmosphere than logic. The fifth stanza, in particular, is dense and baffling, depending on references that Blake never explains, and some readers find that its pivotal moment is the least clear.

There is also a certain relentlessness. If you are not swept up by the rhythm, the repeated what and what and what can start to feel like rhetoric, a gesture of awe more than a developing thought.

These are small complaints. The poem does something remarkable. It makes a reader feel awe, fear and a kind of dizzy admiration, and it does it in twenty-four lines.

III. The Impact It Had

The Tyger had very little impact when it first appeared. Blake printed his Songs in tiny numbers, coloured each copy by hand, and sold them to a handful of friends and patrons. He died in 1827 in relative obscurity, mostly known, if at all, as an engraver and an eccentric. A famous remark from the diarist Henry Crabb Robinson records Wordsworth saying that there was no doubt Blake was mad, but that there was something in his madness that interested him more than the sanity of Lord Byron. It captures the way even sympathetic contemporaries saw him.

The revival came slowly, and it began in the middle of the nineteenth century. Alexander Gilchrist's biography of Blake, finished after his death by his wife Anne and published in 1863, brought Blake back before the public, and the Pre-Raphaelite artists and poets, Dante Gabriel Rossetti and others, championed him. The poem soon became a fixture of anthologies, and by the early twentieth century Blake was being treated as one of the supreme English poets. The critic Northrop Frye's 1947 book on Blake, Fearful Symmetry, took its title from this poem and helped establish him as a serious thinker as well as a poet.

The poem's cultural footprint is huge. It is among the most frequently anthologised lyrics in the English language and is taught in schools around the world. Its first line has been borrowed endlessly. The science fiction novel by Alfred Bester that was published in Britain as Tiger! Tiger! uses the Blake echo directly, and Kipling used a similar phrase for a chapter of The Jungle Book. Countless titles of books, films and albums nod to the burning tiger, and the phrase fearful symmetry has become a standard way to describe an unsettling kind of perfection.

Composers have loved it too. Benjamin Britten set the poem in his Songs and Proverbs of William Blake in the 1960s, and many popular and folk musicians have followed.

Blake's wider influence on later writers has been enormous. The Romantic and Victorian poets slowly absorbed him. The twentieth century's poets and thinkers found in him a prophet. W. B. Yeats co-edited an edition of his works in the 1890s. The Beat poet Allen Ginsberg claimed to have heard Blake's voice reading to him in a vision in 1948, and counted Blake as an inspiration. Blake's opposition to rigid authority and to cold rationalism inspired the counterculture of the 1960s, and the title of Aldous Huxley's The Doors of Perception comes from his work.

In the classroom, the poem is a favourite for teaching a poet's use of symbol and the Romantic idea of the sublime, the feeling of awe mixed with terror. For many students it is the first poem that taught them that a poem can be a question rather than an answer.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved The Tyger first of all for its sound. It is a poem that sticks. The rhythm gets into the head and stays there, and the opening line is among the most recognisable in the language. Many people can recite the first stanza from memory without ever having tried to learn it. That kind of music is rare, and it is part of why the poem has endured for more than two hundred years.

They love its sense of awe. The poem captures something readers know from real life, which is the feeling of being faced with something beautiful and frightening at once. A thunderstorm, a great cat, a vast night sky: all produce the mixed delight and dread that the Romantics called the sublime. The poem makes the feeling instantly available. A reader does not need any theory to be moved, only a pulse.

Another reason is the sheer boldness of its question. Few poems dare to ask, in plain words, whether the same God could have made the lamb and the tiger. This is a problem that thoughtful people in every age have struggled with, and Blake puts it in a way a child can grasp. Readers admire that. They enjoy a poem that is willing to look at the hardest question and then refuse to give a false answer. For many believers and non-believers alike, the poem feels honest.

Readers love the imagery of the forge. It makes creation tangible. Instead of the abstract language of philosophy, Blake gives us sparks, sweat, muscle and iron, so that the origin of life feels physical and dangerous. Writers have praised how the poem turns an abstract problem into a scene of work.

Other writers have loved the way it invites reading. Because the poem is open, it supports a wide range of interpretations, from the religious to the political to the psychological, and each generation has found its own meaning. That openness is a form of generosity. It means the poem is never finished, and a reader coming back after twenty years is likely to find something new.

Finally, readers love Blake himself, the stubborn, visionary craftsman who made his books by hand and cared nothing for fame. There is something moving in the idea that a man who worked in obscurity produced words that millions of people now know by heart. The poem, in its energy and its refusal to be tamed, seems to express the personality of its author. People feel they are hearing a real voice, urgent and original, and that voice has not aged.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Critics have rarely been negative about the poem's quality, but they have disagreed sharply about what it means, and some have pushed back on how it is read.

In Blake's own time, the poem was barely noticed, so there was little contemporary criticism. What we do have is a general sense of unease about Blake. Many of his contemporaries regarded him as eccentric, or worse, and his style and mystical symbolism were dismissed as obscure. His work stood outside the fashions of the time, and it was often called unintelligible.

Later critics have disputed the reading. One long-standing debate is whether the tiger represents evil or something good. On one side, it is a symbol of the fallen, destructive side of nature and of human cruelty. On the other, it represents energy, which Blake famously celebrated in another work as eternal delight. Some political critics have connected the tiger to the French Revolution, which Blake supported at first and then watched turn violent, seeing in the beast the glory and the terror of revolt. Others resist these readings. They argue that any single interpretation shrinks the poem. Skeptics say that this open-endedness can become an excuse for readers to project whatever they like.

Another objection is that the poem is too often treated as a stand-alone work, when Blake meant it to be read alongside The Lamb. Critics have complained that anthologies that print it alone encourage a shallow reading, a kind of generic spooky nature poem, and cut it off from the careful structure of Innocence and Experience. Some also note that Blake himself appears to be critical of both states, so that the poem may be less about terrifying tigers than about the limits of a narrow view of the world.

Religious objections have also arisen. The poem's implication that God might have made something terrible, and might even have enjoyed the work, has seemed blasphemous to some, or at least to flirt with it. Blake was a deeply religious man, in his own unorthodox way, but his refusal to defend divine goodness comfortably unsettled conventional readers.

On aesthetic grounds, a few critics have found the poem's repeated questioning mechanical, or have felt that the famous opening is more impressive than what follows. There is also a view that the poem's popularity has dulled it, and that it is now taught so heavily that students recite its symbols without feeling the shock. These are complaints about handling rather than substance. The core of the poem has proved sturdy against all of them.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Blake belongs to British Romanticism, the movement that prized imagination, emotion and nature over cold reason, but he is a very strange member of it. Wordsworth sought truth in quiet landscapes. Coleridge explored dreams and the supernatural. Blake saw visions. He thought the imagination was the real world and ordinary sight a pale shadow of it. He also fought against what he saw as the narrowing forces of his age: the rationalism of Newton and Locke, the church's rigid morality, and the dehumanising factories of early industrial England.

The poem descends from several traditions. The simple, song-like form echoes the ballads and hymns Blake grew up hearing, particularly the hymns of Isaac Watts, whose Divine Songs for children Blake both imitated and, in his Songs, subverted. The question about who made the world has deep roots in the Book of Job, where God answers Job's complaints with a thunderous list of questions about creation, among them whether Job can draw out Leviathan. Blake's poem reverses that scene, with the human asking the questions and God staying silent. Milton's Paradise Lost, with its rebel angels and its examination of evil, is another source, one that Blake admired and argued with all his life.

What descends from it is wide. Romantic poets after him drew on his example of the poem as vision, and the Victorians recovered him as a source of spiritual energy. Modern poets from Yeats to Ginsberg to Ted Hughes found in him a model of poetry as prophecy. Hughes, in particular, wrote tiger and jaguar poems, such as The Jaguar, that show an obvious kinship, treating animal power with the same blend of awe and dread. Contemporary fantasy writers, including Philip Pullman, who named his trilogy after a phrase in Milton and who is a great admirer of Blake, continue to work the territory Blake opened.

Compare it with Yeats's The Second Coming, which also imagines a terrifying beast emerging as the world changes. Yeats was a devoted student of Blake, and the later poem can be seen as an answer to this one: Blake wonders who could make the tiger, and Yeats wonders what rough beast is about to be born. Compare it also with Dickinson's courteous Death and with Frost's dark, deep woods. Each of these poems turns to the natural world and finds there both beauty and menace, and each declines to make the menace comfortable.

Why does it still matter? Because the question has not been answered. We still live in a world that contains tenderness and terror, creation and destruction, and we still wonder what to make of that. The poem does not tell us. It does something rarer, which is to teach us how to ask. In an age of quick answers, a poem that stays inside a great question and makes that question burn is something to be treasured.
