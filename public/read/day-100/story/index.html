After Twenty Years

O. Henry (1906) • 6 min read

“Until five years ago,” said the policeman. “It was torn down then.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

After Twenty Years is a very short story by O. Henry, the pen name of William Sydney Porter, published in 1906 and later collected in a book of city stories called The Voice of the City. Porter knew his subject. He had lived in Texas, fled the country when he was accused of embezzling from a bank, returned home, and served time in prison, so the questions of law, loyalty, and second chances were not abstract to him. He then became one of the most popular storytellers in America, famous for tales with a surprise at the end. This one takes place on a single chilly night on a New York street, where a man waits in a doorway for an old friend he promised to meet twenty years ago. It runs only a few minutes, and what it says about friendship and duty is more complicated than it first appears.

The policeman on the beat moved up the avenue impressively. The impressiveness was habitual and not for show, for spectators were few. The time was barely 10 o’clock at night, but chilly gusts of wind with a taste of rain in them had well nigh de-peopled the streets.

Trying doors as he went, twirling his club with many intricate and artful movements, turning now and then to cast his watchful eye adown the pacific thoroughfare, the officer, with his stalwart form and slight swagger, made a fine picture of a guardian of the peace. The vicinity was one that kept early hours. Now and then you might see the lights of a cigar store or of an all-night lunch counter; but the majority of the doors belonged to business places that had long since been closed.

When about midway of a certain block the policeman suddenly slowed his walk. In the doorway of a darkened hardware store a man leaned, with an unlighted cigar in his mouth. As the policeman walked up to him the man spoke up quickly.

“It’s all right, officer,” he said, reassuringly. “I’m just waiting for a friend. It’s an appointment made twenty years ago. Sounds a little funny to you, doesn’t it? Well, I’ll explain if you’d like to make certain it’s all straight. About that long ago there used to be a restaurant where this store stands⁠—‘Big Joe’ Brady’s restaurant.”

“Until five years ago,” said the policeman. “It was torn down then.”

The man in the doorway struck a match and lit his cigar. The light showed a pale, square-jawed face with keen eyes, and a little white scar near his right eyebrow. His scarfpin was a large diamond, oddly set.

“Twenty years ago tonight,” said the man, “I dined here at ‘Big Joe’ Brady’s with Jimmy Wells, my best chum, and the finest chap in the world. He and I were raised here in New York, just like two brothers, together. I was eighteen and Jimmy was twenty. The next morning I was to start for the West to make my fortune. You couldn’t have dragged Jimmy out of New York; he thought it was the only place on earth. Well, we agreed that night that we would meet here again exactly twenty years from that date and time, no matter what our conditions might be or from what distance we might have to come. We figured that in twenty years each of us ought to have our destiny worked out and our fortunes made, whatever they were going to be.”

“It sounds pretty interesting,” said the policeman. “Rather a long time between meets, though, it seems to me. Haven’t you heard from your friend since you left?”

“Well, yes, for a time we corresponded,” said the other. “But after a year or two we lost track of each other. You see, the West is a pretty big proposition, and I kept hustling around over it pretty lively. But I know Jimmy will meet me here if he’s alive, for he always was the truest, stanchest old chap in the world. He’ll never forget. I came a thousand miles to stand in this door tonight, and it’s worth it if my old partner turns up.”

The waiting man pulled out a handsome watch, the lids of it set with small diamonds.

“Three minutes to ten,” he announced. “It was exactly ten o’clock when we parted here at the restaurant door.”

“Did pretty well out West, didn’t you?” asked the policeman.

“You bet! I hope Jimmy has done half as well. He was a kind of plodder, though, good fellow as he was. I’ve had to compete with some of the sharpest wits going to get my pile. A man gets in a groove in New York. It takes the West to put a razor-edge on him.”

The policeman twirled his club and took a step or two.

“I’ll be on my way. Hope your friend comes around all right. Going to call time on him sharp?”

“I should say not!” said the other. “I’ll give him half an hour at least. If Jimmy is alive on earth he’ll be here by that time. So long, officer.”

“Good night, sir,” said the policeman, passing on along his beat, trying doors as he went.

There was now a fine, cold drizzle falling, and the wind had risen from its uncertain puffs into a steady blow. The few foot passengers astir in that quarter hurried dismally and silently along with coat collars turned high and pocketed hands. And in the door of the hardware store the man who had come a thousand miles to fill an appointment, uncertain almost to absurdity, with the friend of his youth, smoked his cigar and waited.

About twenty minutes he waited, and then a tall man in a long overcoat, with collar turned up to his ears, hurried across from the opposite side of the street. He went directly to the waiting man.

“Is that you, Bob?” he asked, doubtfully.

“Is that you, Jimmy Wells?” cried the man in the door.

“Bless my heart!” exclaimed the new arrival, grasping both the other’s hands with his own. “It’s Bob, sure as fate. I was certain I’d find you here if you were still in existence. Well, well, well!⁠—twenty years is a long time. The old restaurant’s gone, Bob; I wish it had lasted, so we could have had another dinner there. How has the West treated you, old man?”

“Bully; it has given me everything I asked it for. You’ve changed lots, Jimmy. I never thought you were so tall by two or three inches.”

“Oh, I grew a bit after I was twenty.”

“Doing well in New York, Jimmy?”

“Moderately. I have a position in one of the city departments. Come on, Bob; we’ll go around to a place I know of, and have a good long talk about old times.”

The two men started up the street, arm in arm. The man from the West, his egotism enlarged by success, was beginning to outline the history of his career. The other, submerged in his overcoat, listened with interest.

At the corner stood a drug store, brilliant with electric lights. When they came into this glare each of them turned simultaneously to gaze upon the other’s face.

The man from the West stopped suddenly and released his arm.

“You’re not Jimmy Wells,” he snapped. “Twenty years is a long time, but not long enough to change a man’s nose from a Roman to a pug.”

“It sometimes changes a good man into a bad one,” said the tall man. “You’ve been under arrest for ten minutes, ‘Silky’ Bob. Chicago thinks you may have dropped over our way and wires us she wants to have a chat with you. Going quietly, are you? That’s sensible. Now, before we go on to the station here’s a note I was asked to hand you. You may read it here at the window. It’s from Patrolman Wells.”

The man from the West unfolded the little piece of paper handed him. His hand was steady when he began to read, but it trembled a little by the time he had finished. The note was rather short.

Bob: I was at the appointed place on time. When you struck the match to light your cigar I saw it was the face of the man wanted in Chicago. Somehow I couldn’t do it myself, so I went around and got a plainclothes man to do the job.

Jimmy.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

After Twenty Years is a story built on one night and one conversation, and its meaning rests on a question it asks quietly: what do you owe a friend when you also owe something to the law?

The story begins with a patrolman walking his beat in the cold, trying doors and twirling his club. In the doorway of a darkened hardware store, he finds a man waiting with an unlit cigar. The man explains that twenty years ago, a restaurant stood here, and that he and his best friend, Jimmy Wells, promised to meet at this spot, at ten o'clock, exactly twenty years later, "no matter what our conditions might be or from what distance we might have to come." He has come a thousand miles. He is prosperous, with a diamond in his scarf pin and a watch with diamonds on its lid, and he speaks with the loud confidence of a man who has made his pile out West. Jimmy, he admits, was always "a kind of plodder," a good fellow who would never have the sharp wits needed to succeed.

The patrolman listens, chats, and moves on. A little later, a tall man in a long overcoat arrives, greets the waiting man warmly, takes his arm, and starts to walk him toward a place where they can talk. Then, under the bright electric light of a drugstore, the man from the West stops. "You're not Jimmy Wells," he snaps. The tall man replies that he is a plainclothes officer, that the man is under arrest, and that Chicago wants a word with him. He hands over a note. It reads, "I was at the appointed place on time. When you struck the match to light your cigar I saw it was the face of the man wanted in Chicago. Somehow I couldn't do it myself, so I went around and got a plainclothes man to do the job. Jimmy."

The reader, in a moment, realizes the truth. The patrolman at the start was Jimmy Wells himself. He had stood and talked with his old friend, listened to him brag, and said nothing. The story's emotional core is in that one silent encounter, and in the sentence "Somehow I couldn't do it myself." The layers beneath this are what give the story its power. Bob, who thought Jimmy a plodder, is outdone by him in both honesty and character. Jimmy kept the promise, and he also did his duty, and he did it with a conscience. He does not hide behind the law, but he does not betray it either. The line "It sometimes changes a good man into a bad one," spoken by the plainclothes man in answer to Bob's complaint about his nose, is a sharp bit of irony, for it speaks of what twenty years did to Bob, and only by implication of what it did not do to Jimmy.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Judged purely as a piece of craft, After Twenty Years is admirably compact, and it is also a little too neat. It is worth looking at both sides.

The strengths are plain. The story does a great deal in very little space, around twelve hundred words, and every detail is useful. The opening description of the patrolman, with his "slight swagger" and his twirling club, seems at first to be scene-setting, and only on a second reading do we realize that Henry is introducing a character in disguise. The little clues, such as the policeman's remark that the restaurant was torn down "five years ago," or his question, "Did pretty well out West, didn't you?" are fair signs for an attentive reader. Bob himself gives away every detail needed to identify him, the scar near his right eyebrow, the odd diamond, the cigar and match, and he does it out of pure vanity. The final twist does not cheat. It is built from what we have already been given.

The writing has a pleasing plainness. Unlike some of Henry's more ornamental stories, this one keeps its language cleaner, and the cold, windy atmosphere, the rising wind and "fine, cold drizzle," supports the tone of waiting. The ending, with the note, is economical and moving. Jimmy never explains himself. He says only that he could not do it. That restraint is what makes it work. Yet the story has weaknesses. It is, in the end, a sketch more than a story. The two men are seen almost entirely from outside, and Bob is a rather one-dimensional braggart. We never learn what crime he committed, only that Chicago wants him. We know little about Jimmy except through one note. The moral conflict is stated rather than explored, and a reader who wishes to feel what it cost Jimmy to act may feel short-changed.

There are also plausibility problems. Would a man wanted by the police wait for twenty years in the hope of seeing a friend, and at a fixed place where the law could easily find him? Would Jimmy, who recognized him, not have simply made the arrest on the spot, or at least warned him, rather than hurrying off to find a plainclothes officer, which allows twenty minutes for the fugitive to flee? And is it believable that Bob chats face to face with a patrolman and never suspects it might be the boyhood friend he came a thousand miles to see? Henry is counting on the reader to accept these things for the sake of the story's emotional design. These are small matters beside the story's real accomplishment, which is to leave a reader with a silence and a feeling.

III. The Impact It Had

After Twenty Years was published in 1906, when O. Henry was at the height of his popularity and writing steadily for New York newspapers and magazines, and it was later collected in The Voice of the City, a book of New York tales. The story did not become as famous as The Gift of the Magi, but it grew steadily as a favorite of schools and anthologies, and it is now among the most frequently reprinted short stories in English.

Its biggest impact has been in the classroom. Teachers choose it because it is short enough to read in a single period, and because it demonstrates several important ideas at once. It is a textbook example of dramatic irony, in which the reader understands something the character does not. It shows how a surprise ending can be prepared by fair clues. It presents a moral dilemma that students can debate, whether loyalty to a friend or duty to the law should come first. Many English teachers use it to teach inference, asking students to go back through the story and find the signs that the first policeman was Jimmy.

It has also been adapted. The story has been dramatized on radio, in television anthology series, and in short films and student productions in several countries. Its basic situation, in which one friend discovers that the other is on the wrong side of the law, has been repeated in countless crime stories and films, from police dramas in which an officer must arrest a childhood friend to novels about old companions who find themselves on opposite sides. The detail of an appointment kept after many years is a familiar romantic device, and Henry's version, with its dark twist, may have helped make that device a staple of fiction.

In terms of Henry's reputation, the story shows a harder, more severe side of his work. Much of his fame came from sentimental tales of sacrifice and from comic romps. This one is crisp, short, and morally serious, and it suggests that he could write with discipline when he wished. It also reflects the experience of a man who had himself been on the wrong side of the law, and who understood what it meant to be pursued and to be judged. The phrase "O. Henry ending" is associated with stories like this one, and it has become a common way to describe any narrative that closes with a sharp reversal. Even people who have never read him use it. After Twenty Years is one of the pieces that put the phrase into circulation, along with a general expectation that a short story should end with a surprise. That expectation, good or bad, has shaped generations of short fiction.

IV. What Readers Loved

What have readers loved about After Twenty Years? First, they love the ending. Many people recall the moment when "You're not Jimmy Wells" is spoken, and the gradual unfolding of the truth. The reveal works because it makes the reader reinterpret the whole story. The patrolman, who seemed a mere background figure, becomes the center. Few endings in short fiction manage to reverse the meaning of what came before so completely and so fairly, and readers often say that they went back and read the story again at once, in order to see how it was done.

They also love the emotional complexity. Readers admire Jimmy because he does not do the easy thing. He could have turned a blind eye, or he could have made the arrest himself without feeling anything. Instead he does his duty in a way that shows his love. The idea that mercy and justice can coexist, that a man can be loyal and honest at the same time, has a moving simplicity. Many readers have said that the note, with its brief confession, is among the most affecting passages in Henry's work, precisely because it explains so little.

Another source of pleasure is the atmosphere. The night, the drizzle, the empty street, the policeman's slow walk, all create a mood of loneliness and anticipation. Readers who enjoy a story that has a strong sense of place often cite this one. It has the feel of an old black-and-white film, and it is easy to picture.

People also respond to the theme of time. Twenty years is long enough for a boy to become a stranger, and the story plays on the shock of meeting someone you thought you knew. Readers in middle age sometimes say that the story struck them differently than it did in school, because they now know how much a person can change, and how impossible it is to keep a youthful promise exactly as it was made. The pleasure, then, is partly one of recognition. Finally, many appreciate how short it is. It tells its whole story in the time it takes to drink a cup of coffee. Readers often praise Henry's economy and say that the story demonstrates what the short form can do. For many people, it is the first story that made them realize how much can be said in a few pages.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Despite its popularity, After Twenty Years has been criticized on several grounds. The commonest complaint is that it is too contrived. Critics have pointed out that the story depends on a long series of coincidences and conveniences. The two friends happen to choose a spot that later becomes a policeman's beat. The policeman happens to be the very friend. The friend happens to recognize the fugitive from his description. And the fugitive, a man wanted by the police, happens to walk into a trap through his own sentiment. Those who prefer fiction that grows from character rather than design say that Henry is working too hard to produce his surprise, and that the result feels more like a clever puzzle than a human event.

A second line of criticism concerns characterization. Bob is a thin figure, a boastful man defined by his diamonds and his pride, and some readers feel that Henry has made it too easy for us to accept his arrest. We are not told what Bob did, and we are given little reason to care about him except as a foil. Jimmy, for his part, barely speaks as a character. The moral weight of the story rests on a single note, and a demanding reader might wish for more of the struggle behind it.

There is also a moral objection. Some readers dislike Jimmy's decision, finding it either cold or cowardly. By sending someone else to do the arrest, he avoids the difficult confrontation, and one might argue that he deceives his friend twice, first by standing silently beside him and then by letting a stranger take his place. The note presents this as an act of feeling, but it can also be read as an evasion. Others argue the opposite, that he should have let Bob go, and that loyalty to a friend ought to outweigh the law. Henry does not settle the debate, which some find admirable and others find evasive.

Finally, a few readers have found the story slight, a trick without much substance. In comparison with Henry's more complex pieces, it can seem small. The prose, though clear, has little of the humor and richness that characterize his best work. And some have noted that the portrait of the West as a place where sharp wits get rich, while the East breeds plodders, is a stock idea of the period that does not stand up to much thought. Even so, these complaints rarely lessen the story's effect. They tend to come from readers who admire it but wish it were something else.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

After Twenty Years belongs to the tradition of the short story with a twist, which was brought to its highest polish in the late nineteenth century by the French writer Guy de Maupassant, whose stories often turn on a sudden reversal, and by American magazine writers who adapted the method for a mass audience. Henry was the supreme American master of the form. His surprises were seldom cruel, and often tender, and the best of them, like this one, made the reader feel as well as gasp. The story is also part of the literature of the city. In the early 1900s, American writers began to explore urban life with fresh attention, and Henry's New York stories, with their policemen, clerks, and drifters, were among the most popular examples. The man on the beat is a familiar figure of that world, and Henry treats him with dignity, as someone who sees much and says little. In this respect the story fits with The Cop and the Anthem, which makes gentler fun of the same kind of officer.

Its central subject, the clash between friendship and duty, is as old as literature. Greek tragedy is full of characters who must choose between personal loyalty and public law, and the question has returned in countless forms, from stories of soldiers who must judge their comrades to modern crime fiction in which detectives face the criminals they once knew. Henry's treatment is modest and compact, but it touches the same nerve. In particular, it resembles later police stories in which the hardest moment is not the chase but the quiet decision about what to do with someone you love.

The promised reunion is another ancient device. Stories about pledges kept across years, from old ballads to the novels of Dumas, depend on the idea that a promise can bind across time. Henry's twist is that the promise is kept by both friends, but in ways that destroy it. Bob keeps the promise out of sentiment, and Jimmy keeps it out of conscience, and each is rewarded as he deserves. Compared with Henry's other famous stories, this one is leaner and darker. The Gift of the Magi and The Last Leaf end in tender sacrifice, and The Ransom of Red Chief ends in comedy. After Twenty Years ends in an arrest, with a note that carries a shadow of grief. It shows another side of his range.

Why read it now? Because it asks an old question in a fresh and brief form, and because it still works. A reader in the twenty-first century, who may never have heard of Big Joe Brady's restaurant, will recognize the feeling of seeing an old friend after many years and wondering who each of you has become. The story takes five minutes to read, and its question, what would you do in Jimmy's place, can last much longer.
