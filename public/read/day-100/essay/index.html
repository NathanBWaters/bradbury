Meditations, Book XII: The Final Reckoning of the Citizen of the World

Marcus Aurelius (c. 180 AD) • 17 min read

Man, thou hast been a citizen in this great state, the world: what does it matter to thee whether for five years or three? For that which is conformable to the laws is just for all. Depart then satisfied, for he also who releases thee is satisfied.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Book Twelve is the last chapter of Marcus Aurelius's private notebook, the Meditations. Marcus was emperor of Rome from 161 to 180, and he died in 180 on campaign along the Danube, after years of war against Germanic tribes and a plague that had swept the empire. He wrote in Greek, to himself, as a follower of Stoicism, the philosophy that teaches us to control our own judgments and accept what we cannot change. Nothing marks this book as a deliberate farewell, and scholars are cautious about the order in which the books were written. But it reads like one. It gathers the notebook's ideas into a final reckoning: stop deferring the good life, because everything you want from philosophy is available now. It faces death, fame and the gods with calm, and it ends with one of the great images of letting go. Its meaning is that a life is complete when it is lived honestly, however short.

All those things at which thou wishest to arrive by a circuitous road, thou canst have now, if thou dost not refuse them to thyself. And this means, if thou wilt take no notice of all the past, and trust the future to providence, and direct the present only conformably to piety and justice. Conformably to piety, that thou mayest be content with the lot which is assigned to thee, for nature designed it for thee and thee for it. Conformably to justice, that thou mayest always speak the truth freely and without disguise, and do the things which are agreeable to law and according to the worth of each. And let neither another man’s wickedness hinder thee, nor opinion nor voice, nor yet the sensations of the poor flesh which has grown about thee; for the passive part will look to this. If then, whatever the time may be when thou shalt be near to thy departure, neglecting everything else thou shalt respect only thy ruling faculty and the divinity within thee, and if thou shalt be afraid not because thou must some time cease to live, but if thou shalt fear never to have begun to live according to nature⁠—then thou wilt be a man worthy of the universe which has produced thee, and thou wilt cease to be a stranger in thy native land, and to wonder at things which happen daily as if they were something unexpected, and to be dependent on this or that.

God sees the minds [ruling principles] of all men bared of the material vesture and rind and impurities. For with his intellectual part alone he touches the intelligence only which has flowed and been derived from himself into these bodies. And if thou also usest thyself to do this, thou wilt rid thyself of thy much trouble. For he who regards not the poor flesh which envelops him, surely will not trouble himself by looking after raiment and dwelling and fame and suchlike externals and show.

The things are three of which thou art composed, a little body, a little breath [life], intelligence. Of these the first two are thine, so far as it is thy duty to take care of them; but the third alone is properly thine. Therefore if thou shalt separate from thyself, that is, from thy understanding, whatever others do or say, and whatever thou hast done or said thyself, and whatever future things trouble thee because they may happen, and whatever in the body which envelops thee or in the breath [life], which is by nature associated with the body, is attached to thee independent of thy will, and whatever the external circumfluent vortex whirls round, so that the intellectual power exempt from the things of fate can live pure and free by itself, doing what is just and accepting what happens and saying the truth: if thou wilt separate, I say, from this ruling faculty the things which are attached to it by the impressions of sense, and the things of time to come and of time that is past, and wilt make thyself like Empedocles’ sphere,

All round, and in its joyous rest reposing;

and if thou shalt strive to live only what is really thy life, that is, the present⁠—then thou wilt be able to pass that portion of life which remains for thee up to the time of thy death, free from perturbations, nobly, and obedient to thy own daemon [to the god that is within thee].

I have often wondered how it is that every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others. If then a god or a wise teacher should present himself to a man and bid him to think of nothing and to design nothing which he would not express as soon as he conceived it, he could not endure it even for a single day. So much more respect have we to what our neighbours shall think of us than to what we shall think of ourselves.

How can it be that the gods after having arranged all things well and benevolently for mankind, have overlooked this alone, that some men and very good men, and men who, as we may say, have had most communion with the divinity, and through pious acts and religious observances have been most intimate with the divinity, when they have once died should never exist again, but should be completely extinguished?

But if this is so, be assured that if it ought to have been otherwise, the gods would have done it. For if it were just, it would also be possible; and if it were according to nature, nature would have had it so. But because it is not so, if in fact it is not so, be thou convinced that it ought not to have been so:⁠—for thou seest even of thyself that in this inquiry thou art disputing with the diety; and we should not thus dispute with the gods, unless they were most excellent and most just;⁠—but if this is so, they would not have allowed anything in the ordering of the universe to be neglected unjustly and irrationally.

Practise thyself even in the things which thou despairest of accomplishing. For even the left hand, which is ineffectual for all other things for want of practice, holds the bridle more vigorously than the right hand; for it has been practised in this.

Consider in what condition both in body and soul a man should be when he is overtaken by death; and consider the shortness of life, the boundless abyss of time past and future, the feebleness of all matter.

Contemplate the formative principles [forms] of things bare of their coverings; the purposes of actions; consider what pain is, what pleasure is, and death, and fame; who is to himself the cause of his uneasiness; how no man is hindered by another; that everything is opinion.

In the application of thy principles thou must be like the pancratiast, not like the gladiator; for the gladiator lets fall the sword which he uses and is killed; but the other always has his hand, and needs to do nothing else than use it.

See what things are in themselves, dividing them into matter, form and purpose.

What a power man has to do nothing except what God will approve, and to accept all that God may give him.

With respect to that which happens conformably to nature, we ought to blame neither gods, for they do nothing wrong either voluntarily or involuntarily, nor men, for they do nothing wrong except involuntarily. Consequently we should blame nobody.

How ridiculous and what a stranger he is who is surprised at anything which happens in life.

Either there is a fatal necessity and invincible order, or a kind Providence, or a confusion without a purpose and without a director [Book IV]. If then there is an invincible necessity, why dost thou resist? But if there is a Providence which allows itself to be propitiated, make thyself worthy of the help of the divinity. But if there is a confusion without governor, be content that in such a tempest thou hast in thyself a certain ruling intelligence. And even if the tempest carry thee away, let it carry away the poor flesh, the poor breath, everything else; for the intelligence at least it will not carry away.

Does the light of the lamp shine without losing its splendour until it is extinguished; and shall the truth which is in thee and justice and temperance be extinguished before thy death?

When a man has presented the appearance of having done wrong, say, How then do I know if this is a wrongful act? And even if he has done wrong, how do I know that he has not condemned himself? and so this is like tearing his own face. Consider that he, who would not have the bad man do wrong, is like the man who would not have the fig-tree to bear juice in the figs and infants to cry and the horse to neigh, and whatever else must of necessity be. For what must a man do who has such a character? If then thou art irritable, cure this man’s disposition.

If it is not right, do not do it: if it is not true, do not say it. For let thy efforts be⁠—

In everything always observe what the thing is which produces for thee an appearance, and resolve it by dividing it into the formal, the material, the purpose, and the time within which it must end.

Perceive at last that thou hast in thee something better and more divine than the things which cause the various affects, and as it were pull thee by the strings. What is there now in my mind? Is it fear, or suspicion, or desire, or anything of the kind?

First, do nothing inconsiderately, nor without a purpose. Second, make thy acts refer to nothing else than to a social end.

Consider that before long thou wilt be nobody and nowhere, nor will any of the things exist which thou now seest, nor any of those who are now living. For all things are formed by nature to change and be turned and to perish in order that other things in continuous succession may exist.

Consider that everything is opinion, and opinion is in thy power. Take away then, when thou choosest, thy opinion, and like a mariner, who has doubled the promontory, thou wilt find calm, everything stable, and a waveless bay.

Any one activity whatever it may be, when it has ceased at its proper time, suffers no evil because it has ceased; nor he who has done this act, does he suffer any evil for this reason that the act has ceased. In like manner then the whole which consists of all the acts, which is our life, if it cease at its proper time, suffers no evil for this reason that it has ceased; nor he who has terminated this series at the proper time, has he been ill dealt with. But the proper time and the limit nature fixes, sometimes as in old age the peculiar nature of man, but always the universal nature, by the change of whose parts the whole universe continues ever young and perfect. And everything which is useful to the universal is always good and in season. Therefore the termination of life for every man is no evil, because neither is it shameful, since it is both independent of the will and not opposed to the general interest, but it is good, since it is seasonable and profitable to and congruent with the universal. For thus too he is moved by the deity who is moved in the same manner with the deity and moved towards the same things in his mind.

These three principles thou must have in readiness. In the things which thou doest do nothing either inconsiderately or otherwise than as justice herself would act; but with respect to what may happen to thee from without, consider that it happens either by chance or according to Providence, and thou must neither blame chance nor accuse Providence. Second, consider what every being is from the seed to the time of its receiving a soul, and from the reception of a soul to the giving back of the same, and of what things every being is compounded and into what things it is resolved. Third, if thou shouldst suddenly be raised up above the earth, and shouldst look down on human things, and observe the variety of them how great it is, and at the same time also shouldst see at a glance how great is the number of beings who dwell around in the air and the æther, consider that as often as thou shouldst be raised up, thou wouldst see the same things, sameness of form and shortness of duration. Are these things to be proud of?

Cast away opinion: thou art saved. Who then hinders thee from casting it away?

When thou art troubled about anything, thou hast forgotten this, that all things happen according to the universal nature; and forgotten this, that a man’s wrongful act is nothing to thee; and further thou hast forgotten this, that everything which happens, always happened so and will happen so, and now happens so everywhere; forgotten this too, how close is the kinship between a man and the whole human race, for it is a community, not of a little blood or seed, but of intelligence. And thou hast forgotten this too, that every man’s intelligence is a god, and is an efflux of the deity; and forgotten this, that nothing is a man’s own, but that his child and his body and his very soul came from the deity; forgotten this, that everything is opinion; and lastly thou hast forgotten that every man lives the present time only, and loses only this.

Constantly bring to thy recollection those who have complained greatly about anything, those who have been most conspicuous by the greatest fame or misfortunes or enmities or fortunes of any kind: then think where are they all now? Smoke and ash and a tale, or not even a tale. And let there be present to thy mind also everything of this sort, how Fabius Catullinus lived in the country, and Lucius Lupus in his gardens, and Stertinius at Baiæ, and Tiberius at Capreæ and Velius Rufus [or Rufus at Velia]; and in fine think of the eager pursuit of anything conjoined with pride; and how worthless everything is after which men violently strain; and how much more philosophical it is for a man in the opportunities presented to him to show himself just, temperate, obedient to the gods, and to do this with all simplicity: for the pride which is proud of its want of pride is the most intolerable of all.

To those who ask, Where hast thou seen the gods or how dost thou comprehend that they exist and so worshipest them? I answer, in the first place, they may be seen even with the eyes; in the second place, neither have I seen even my own soul and yet I honour it. Thus then with respect to the gods, from what I constantly experience of their power, from this I comprehend that they exist and I venerate them.

The safety of life is this, to examine everything all through, what it is itself, what is its material, what the formal part; with all thy soul to do justice and to say the truth. What remains except to enjoy life by joining one good thing to another so as not to leave even the smallest intervals between?

There is one light of the sun, though it is interrupted by walls, mountains, and other things infinite. There is one common substance, though it is distributed among countless bodies which have their several qualities. There is one soul, though it is distributed among infinite natures and individual circumscriptions [or individuals]. There is one intelligent soul, though it seems to be divided. Now in the things which have been mentioned all the other parts, such as those which are air and matter, are without sensation and have no fellowship: and yet even these parts the intelligent principle holds together, and the gravitation towards the same. But intellect in a peculiar manner tends to that which is of the same kin, and combines with it, and the feeling for communion is not interrupted.

What dost thou wish? to continue to exist? Well, dost thou wish to have sensation? movement? growth? and then again to cease to grow? to use thy speech? to think? What is there of all these things which seems to thee worth desiring? But if it is easy to set little value on all these things, turn to that which remains, which is to follow reason and god. But it is inconsistent with honouring reason and god to be troubled because by death a man will be deprived of the other things.

How small a part of the boundless and unfathomable time is assigned to every man! for it is very soon swallowed up in the eternal. And how small a part of the whole substance! and how small a part of the universal soul! and on what a small clod of the whole earth thou creepest! Reflecting on all this, consider nothing to be great, except to act as thy nature leads thee, and to endure that which the common nature brings.

How does the ruling faculty make use of itself? for all lies in this. But everything else, whether it is in the power of thy will or not, is only lifeless ashes and smoke.

This reflection is most adapted to move us to contempt of death, that even those who think pleasure to be a good and pain an evil still have despised it.

The man to whom that only is good which comes in due season, and to whom it is the same thing whether he has done more or fewer acts conformable to right reason, and to whom it makes no difference whether he contemplates the world for a longer or a shorter time⁠—for this man neither is death a terrible thing [iii, 7; vi, 23; x, 20; xii, 23].

Man, thou hast been a citizen in this great state [the world]; what difference does it make to thee whether for five years [or three]? for that which is conformable to the laws is just for all. Where is the hardship then, if no tyrant nor yet an unjust judge sends thee away from the state, but nature who brought thee into it? the same as if a prætor who has employed an actor dismisses him from the stage.⁠—“But I have not finished the five acts, but only three of them.”⁠—Thou sayest well, but in life the three acts are the whole drama; for what shall be a complete drama is determined by him who was once the cause of its composition, and now of its dissolution: but thou art the cause of neither. Depart then satisfied, for he also who releases thee is satisfied.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Book Twelve opens with a bracing offer. All the things you hope to reach by a roundabout road, Marcus says, you can have now, if you do not refuse them to yourself. The method is to ignore the past, trust the future to providence, and direct the present according to piety and justice. Piety here means being content with the lot assigned to you, since nature designed it for you and you for it. Justice means speaking the truth openly and acting according to law and the worth of each person. The important thing is the word now. Marcus has spent eleven books postponing the life he describes, and he tells himself so.

The next movement turns to death, and it contains one of the book's most striking reversals. When you are near the end, Marcus says, you should fear not that you must cease to live, but that you will never have begun to live according to nature. That is the central insight. Fear of death, he suggests, is really fear of an unlived life. If you have lived rightly, you will be worthy of the universe that produced you, and you will cease to be a stranger in your own country, startled by the daily events of life as though they were surprises.

Then he offers a vision of how a god sees us: not by our clothes and rank, but by our minds, bare of their material covering. If you train yourself to look at yourself that way, he says, you will be rid of much trouble, since you will not worry about clothes, houses and fame. He divides a person into three parts, a little body, a little breath, and intelligence. Only the third is truly yours. If you can separate your understanding from what others say and do, from your past deeds, from worries about the future, and from the body's whirl, then you can live in the present, free and unafraid.

One of the most revealing passages is Marcus's wonder at human psychology. Everyone loves himself more than others, he observes, yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinions of other people. We would be ashamed to reveal our private thoughts to our neighbors, but not to ourselves. He also asks, with real pain, why the gods allow good and pious people to die and vanish forever, and he works out a reply, that if it were wrong the gods would have arranged it otherwise.

The book ends with the image of life as a play. A praetor who has hired an actor dismisses him from the stage. The actor protests that he has done three acts, not five. Marcus answers that in life the three acts are the whole drama, for the one who composed it decides when it is complete. Depart satisfied, he concludes, for the one who releases you is satisfied. It is a farewell that asks for no more time.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Book Twelve is a fitting end, though its power is quieter than that of the best passages in earlier books. Its strength is concentration. The opening is direct, with its insistence on now, and the line about fearing never to have begun to live is among the most arresting in the whole work. It turns the usual fear of death on its head in one sentence. That kind of reversal is the mark of real philosophical writing: it takes a feeling we have, shows it is pointing somewhere else, and does so in plain words.

The final image of the actor and the five acts is masterly. It acknowledges the human protest, that it is not fair, I was not finished, and then replies with a calm that never becomes cold. The phrase that the three acts are the whole drama puts the real question about a life's shape in a new light, since completeness is not measured in length. The ending also lacks sentimentality. It does not promise an afterlife. It simply asks that we leave the stage with the grace of someone who knows the play was not ours.

Yet there are limitations. Parts of Book Twelve are dryer, concerned with technical Stoic matters about the ruling faculty, the unity of the universe, and the relation of body, breath and mind. The division of a person into three parts is neat, but readers unsympathetic to Stoic psychology may find it schematic. The treatment of the gods is also uneasy. Marcus wavers, as elsewhere, between a confident providence and a more skeptical view. His answer to why good people die, essentially that the universe must be fine as it is, can read as circular. It is a hard question, and he gives a dutiful reply rather than a satisfying one.

There is also a risk in the book's contempt for the body. The phrase poor flesh and the instruction to see the body as an envelope that can be set aside, while consoling, shades toward a denial of physical life that many modern readers resist. A man who suffered from illness may have needed that denial, but it is not obviously wisdom.

The book also lacks a sense of closure as a work, because it was not designed as one. Some of its passages are lists of thoughts recycled from earlier books, with references to other sections. It is a notebook, not an epic. But this is part of its honesty. The most convincing farewells are rarely planned, and the plainness of the last page, without trumpet or summary, lends it dignity. The writing is not always beautiful, but it is always sincere, and in a book about authenticity that counts for much.

III. The Impact It Had

Book Twelve's impact is bound up with the fate of the whole Meditations, but its themes have had a life of their own, especially its treatment of death. The image of the actor leaving the stage has been quoted by writers, preachers and teachers for centuries, and variations appear in many cultures. Its message, that a life is complete when it is lived well, rather than when it reaches a particular length, has comforted readers facing early death, their own or that of others. Palliative care writers and chaplains have occasionally drawn on Stoic thinking about death, and Marcus is among the most quoted.

Historically, the end of the book resonated with the story of Marcus's death. According to tradition, he died in March of 180 in the army camp near the Danube. The next emperor was his son Commodus, whose reign was widely regarded as a decline. The ancient historian Cassius Dio, writing a little later, famously described the shift from Marcus's age to what followed as one from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust. This sense of a golden age ending added poignancy to the final book. Later historians, including Edward Gibbon in the eighteenth century, praised the age of the Antonine emperors as among the happiest in human history.

The Meditations as a whole reached English readers through Meric Casaubon's 1634 translation and George Long's of 1862, and the final book became a favorite of Victorian moralists. Matthew Arnold's essay of 1863 portrayed Marcus as a noble figure who held to duty without the consolations of faith, and Book Twelve's calm resignation supported that image. In the twentieth century, Pierre Hadot's The Inner Citadel read the work as a series of spiritual exercises, a view that framed the final book as the culmination of a lifelong discipline.

In recent decades, Gregory Hays's translation of 2002 and the revival of popular Stoicism have given the book a new audience. The phrase about living now, and about not refusing yourself what is available, appears often in self-improvement writing. The film Gladiator, released in 2000, featured an aged Marcus, and although its plot is invented, its mood of a weary philosopher leaving the world owes something to the image these final pages create.

Perhaps the deepest impact is on how people think about endings. The book offers a language for accepting mortality without despair or denial, and that language has been borrowed by many who have never heard of Stoicism.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers love Book Twelve first for its mercy toward the self. It does not say, you have wasted your life, and now it is too late. It says, everything you want is available now, if you stop refusing it. That is a generous idea. People who feel behind in life, or who are approaching the end, often find it unexpectedly freeing. The book makes the present moment a place where one can start again.

They also love the reversal about death. The sentence about fearing never to have begun to live is quoted by readers of all kinds because it names a fear they recognize, the fear of waste, and gives it a direction. It turns terror into a task, and a task is easier to bear. Many people say it helped them look at their own lives and decide what to do differently.

The observation about human nature is another favorite. Marcus wonders how we can love ourselves more than anyone else and yet care more about what others think of us than what we think. That line is often cited as strikingly modern, a diagnosis of social anxiety, image-consciousness and the habit of performing for an audience. In an era of social media, readers say it feels almost unnervingly accurate. It also shows the emperor's willingness to admit that he shares the common weakness.

The ending is cherished for its grace. The image of the actor dismissed after three acts has an unforced beauty. It carries an ache, since most of us feel we are not finished, and yet it offers dignity, not despair. Readers note that it avoids pretending to know what comes next. It stays within the human frame and asks only for gratitude. There is something moving about the final request to depart satisfied because the one who releases us is satisfied.

Finally, readers value the sense of arrival that comes from finishing the book. Having followed Marcus through twelve books of self-scolding, doubt and resolve, the reader reaches a place of rest. Whether or not Marcus meant it as a conclusion, it feels like one. The book rewards patience, and people often remark that they carry its last pages with them for years, using them as a guide for when their own hardest moments come.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

As with the other books, there were no contemporary reviews of Book Twelve, since it was not written for readers. The objections come from later thinkers, and some are serious.

The first concerns the consolations about death. Critics argue that Marcus's reasoning, that death is natural, that the elements simply change, and that a short life is as complete as a long one, may soothe the mind but does not answer grief. The actor's protest, that three acts is not five, is dismissed rather quickly, and some find that the dismissal sounds more like an order to stop complaining than a real answer. In particular, the analogy to a play may seem hollow to those who have lost a child or a young friend, since the idea that the composer decides when the drama is complete assumes a benevolent author. Religious readers sometimes feel the lack of personal hope, while skeptical readers feel the reliance on providence is an unproven assumption.

A second complaint is about the treatment of the body and the emotions. The phrase poor flesh and the teaching that only intelligence is truly ours have been seen as discounting physical suffering and love. Some philosophers argue that Stoicism, by treating everything outside the mind as indifferent, flattens the real moral weight of human attachments. If a loved one's death is only an external event, critics ask, is that wisdom, or an evasion?

Third, the question of the gods. Marcus's asking why the gods overlook the deaths of good men, and then answering that justice would otherwise have been provided, is considered by some critics a weak point. It looks like an argument that assumes its conclusion. Historians of philosophy note that this fits a pattern in the book of oscillating between providence and atoms, never settling on one.

Fourth, there is the criticism of the man. The final book, with its detached serenity, sits beside the facts of his reign: continuing wars, persecution of Christians in some provinces, including at Lyons in 177, and the choice of Commodus as successor. Some historians argue that the calm of the Meditations stands in contrast to the burdens of government and its failures. Marcus did not write to defend himself, but readers cannot help comparing the page and the record.

Finally, some scholars caution that treating Book Twelve as a deliberate conclusion is a modern habit. We do not know that Marcus wrote it last, or that he meant any part as a farewell.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Book Twelve sits at the end of a work that belongs to Roman Stoicism, the late phase of a philosophy that began in Athens around 300 BC with Zeno of Citium. By Marcus's time, the school had moved from system-building to the cultivation of character, as in the letters of Seneca and the lectures of Epictetus. Marcus's closing book distills the discipline, with its emphasis on present action, acceptance of fate, and the cultivation of the ruling faculty, into a farewell that is also a summary.

It also belongs to a larger literature about facing death. Plato's Phaedo portrays Socrates calmly drinking hemlock and describes philosophy as practice for dying, and Marcus, who names Socrates elsewhere in the book, stands in that tradition. Cicero's essay on old age, Seneca's letters written near the end of his life, and later Christian meditations on death share the aim of making mortality bearable. Montaigne, in the sixteenth century, revisited the Stoic view and wrote that to philosophize is to learn to die, though he also grew to prefer a gentler approach.

The theatrical image of life as a play is an old one. Epictetus uses the metaphor of an actor playing a role assigned by the author, and Shakespeare's famous line that all the world is a stage is the best-known later expression. Marcus's version is unusual in that it focuses on the leaving of the stage rather than the playing of the role.

What descends from it? The idea of a good death shaped Renaissance and Enlightenment ethics, and its tone resounds in the secular consolations of later writers. In modern times, its language of acceptance has been taken up in psychotherapy and in the recent revival of Stoicism. Compare it with other farewell texts, such as the last discourses in religious scriptures or the final essays of writers facing illness. Marcus's text is distinct for its refusal to comfort with promises.

Why read it now? Because most of us avoid thinking about our ending, and this book shows how it can be done with dignity and without morbidity. It is short enough to read in a sitting, and its central point is generous: the life you are looking for is not somewhere else or later. As a last page of a twelve-book effort to become a good person, it carries unusual weight. Even if you disagree with its metaphysics, you may find that its final gesture, of leaving with thanks, is one you want to remember.
