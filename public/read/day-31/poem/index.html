Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock

T. S. Eliot (1915) • 8 min read

Beneath a sky like a patient etherised upon a table, a paralyzed modern urbanite measures out his life with coffee spoons while hesitating to disturb the universe.

Key Context & Why It Matters

This is the poem that announced modern poetry in English. T. S. Eliot began it around 1910, when he was a twenty-two-year-old graduate student, an American from St. Louis studying at Harvard and then in Paris. Ezra Pound, the energetic poet who championed new writing, read it and was astonished. He pushed it into print in a Chicago magazine called Poetry in June 1915, and it reached a wider public in Eliot's first book two years later. At the time, English poetry was still dominated by the gentle, moralizing manner of the Victorians. Eliot gave readers instead a nervous, well-dressed middle-aged man who cannot bring himself to ask a woman a simple question, or maybe any question at all. The poem is a love song in title only. It is really a portrait of paralysis, of a mind so busy imagining how others will judge it that it never acts. Listen for the half-lit streets, the drawing rooms, the sea at the end, and the voice talking itself out of everything.

Let us go then, you and I,

When the evening is spread out against the sky

Like a patient etherized upon a table;

Let us go, through certain half-deserted streets,

The muttering retreats

Of restless nights in one-night cheap hotels

And sawdust restaurants with oyster-shells:

Streets that follow like a tedious argument

Of insidious intent

To lead you to an overwhelming question. …

Oh, do not ask, “What is it?”

Let us go and make our visit.

In the room the women come and go

Talking of Michelangelo.

The yellow fog that rubs its back upon the windowpanes,

The yellow smoke that rubs its muzzle on the windowpanes

Licked its tongue into the corners of the evening,

Lingered upon the pools that stand in drains,

Let fall upon its back the soot that falls from chimneys,

Slipped by the terrace, made a sudden leap,

And seeing that it was a soft October night,

Curled once about the house, and fell asleep.

And indeed there will be time

For the yellow smoke that slides along the street,

Rubbing its back upon the window panes;

There will be time, there will be time

To prepare a face to meet the faces that you meet

There will be time to murder and create,

And time for all the works and days of hands

That lift and drop a question on your plate;

Time for you and time for me,

And time yet for a hundred indecisions,

And for a hundred visions and revisions,

Before the taking of a toast and tea.

In the room the women come and go

Talking of Michelangelo.

And indeed there will be time

To wonder, “Do I dare?” and, “Do I dare?”

Time to turn back and descend the stair,

With a bald spot in the middle of my hair—

(They will say: “How his hair is growing thin!”)

My morning coat, my collar mounting firmly to the chin,

My necktie rich and modest, but asserted by a simple pin—

(They will say: “But how his arms and legs are thin!”)

Do I dare

Disturb the universe?

In a minute there is time

For decisions and revisions which a minute will reverse.

For I have known them all already, known them all:

Have known the evenings, mornings, afternoons,

I have measured out my life with coffee spoons;

I know the voices dying with a dying fall

Beneath the music from a farther room.

So how should I presume?

And I have known the eyes already, known them all—

The eyes that fix you in a formulated phrase,

And when I am formulated, sprawling on a pin,

When I am pinned and wriggling on the wall,

Then how should I begin

To spit out all the butt-ends of my days and ways?

And how should I presume?

And I have known the arms already, known them all—

Arms that are braceleted and white and bare

(But in the lamplight, downed with light brown hair!)

Is it perfume from a dress

That makes me so digress?

Arms that lie along a table, or wrap about a shawl.

And should I then presume?

And how should I begin?

Shall I say, I have gone at dusk through narrow streets

And watched the smoke that rises from the pipes

Of lonely men in shirtsleeves, leaning out of windows? …

I should have been a pair of ragged claws

Scuttling across the floors of silent seas.

And the afternoon, the evening, sleeps so peacefully!

Smoothed by long fingers,

Asleep … tired … or it malingers.

Stretched on the floor, here beside you and me.

Should I, after tea and cakes and ices,

Have the strength to force the moment to its crisis?

But though I have wept and fasted, wept and prayed,

Though I have seen my head (grown slightly bald) brought in upon a platter,

I am no prophet—and here’s no great matter;

I have seen the moment of my greatness flicker,

And I have seen the eternal Footman hold my coat, and snicker,

And in short, I was afraid.

And would it have been worth it, after all,

After the cups, the marmalade, the tea,

Among the porcelain, among some talk of you and me,

Would it have been worth while,

To have bitten off the matter with a smile,

To have squeezed the universe into a ball

To roll it toward some overwhelming question,

To say: “I am Lazarus, come from the dead,

Come back to tell you all, I shall tell you all”—

If one, settling a pillow by her head,

Should say: “That is not what I meant at all;

That is not it, at all.”

And would it have been worth it, after all,

Would it have been worth while,

After the sunsets and the dooryards and the sprinkled streets,

After the novels, after the teacups, after the skirts that trail along the floor—

And this, and so much more?—

It is impossible to say just what I mean!

But as if a magic lantern threw the nerves in patterns on a screen:

Would it have been worth while

If one, settling a pillow or throwing off a shawl,

And turning toward the window, should say:

“That is not it at all,

That is not what I meant, at all.”

No! I am not Prince Hamlet, nor was meant to be;

Am an attendant lord, one that will do

To swell a progress, start a scene or two,

Advise the prince; no doubt, an easy tool,

Deferential, glad to be of use,

Politic, cautious, and meticulous;

Full of high sentence, but a bit obtuse;

At times, indeed, almost ridiculous—

Almost, at times, the Fool.

I grow old … I grow old …

I shall wear the bottoms of my trousers rolled.

Shall I part my hair behind? Do I dare to eat a peach?

I shall wear white flannel trousers, and walk upon the beach.

I have heard the mermaids singing, each to each.

I do not think that they will sing to me.

I have seen them riding seaward on the waves

Combing the white hair of the waves blown back

When the wind blows the water white and black.

We have lingered in the chambers of the sea

By sea-girls wreathed with seaweed red and brown

Till human voices wake us, and we drown.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

The poem is a monologue. A man named J. Alfred Prufrock is talking, mostly to himself, though he begins by inviting a "you" to come along with him. Scholars still argue about who that "you" is. It may be a friend, a lover, or simply the other half of Prufrock's own divided mind. They are going somewhere, to a party or a tea, where there is a woman and a question he wants to ask. He never tells us what the question is. He calls it "an overwhelming question," and then he spends the rest of the poem finding reasons not to ask it.

The first thing to understand is that the poem's action is almost entirely internal. Nothing happens in the outer world except walking and standing around in rooms. Everything dramatic happens in Prufrock's head, as he rehearses what he might say, imagines how people will look at him, and then loses his nerve. The famous opening image of the evening spread out "like a patient etherized upon a table" tells you the mood at once. This is not a romantic sunset. It is a body, numbed and helpless, waiting for someone to cut. Prufrock is that patient.

Notice how often he imagines being looked at. The women who "come and go / Talking of Michelangelo" are a kind of chorus of social judgment, trivial and cultured at once. He imagines them saying his hair is thinning, that his arms and legs are thin. Then he pictures himself "pinned and wriggling on the wall," like an insect specimen. This is a man who feels examined and found lacking before he has said a word. His great fear is not rejection exactly. It is being fixed in place by other people's eyes, summed up, and dismissed.

Mixed in are several half-serious comparisons. He thinks of Lazarus, who came back from the dead to "tell you all." He thinks of John the Baptist, whose head was brought on a platter. And he thinks of Hamlet, then immediately says he is not Prince Hamlet but only an attendant lord, someone who "will do / To swell a progress, start a scene or two." That is a painfully honest self-assessment. He wants to be the hero of a story and knows he is a minor character, polite, cautious, a little ridiculous.

The most moving passage comes near the end, when he turns to the mermaids singing "each to each," and says he does not think they will sing to him. Then comes the last image, where he has lingered "in the chambers of the sea" with the sea-girls until "human voices wake us, and we drown." Reality, in the form of other people, ends the dream. The poem's quiet tragedy is that the only freedom Prufrock can imagine exists in fantasy, and that even fantasy cannot survive contact with the real world.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

It is hard to be coolly critical about a poem this famous, but it deserves it, and it mostly survives the test. The great strength is the voice. Eliot found a way to make a mind think on the page, with its hesitations, its reversals and its sudden self-mockery. The refrain "Do I dare?" and the line "There will be time, there will be time" create a rhythm of postponement that feels exactly like procrastination. The irregular rhymes seem to jerk and stall, so that the very sound of the poem imitates the nervous character who speaks it.

The imagery is another triumph. Eliot's yellow fog behaving like a cat, rubbing its back on the windowpanes, is one of the finest extended images in English. It is vivid, funny and slightly sinister, and it turns a gloomy city into a living, sleepy animal. Likewise the line about measuring out his life "with coffee spoons" captures in a few words a life of tiny, repeated, trivial acts. A skilled poet can spend a career hoping to write one phrase that lodges in the language like that, and Eliot wrote several in one poem.

The poem's irony is also finely controlled. Prufrock is pitiable and comic at once. We laugh at his fussing over whether to part his hair behind or dare to eat a peach, and then we feel the sadness of a man who treats such questions as matters of life and death, because to him they are. Eliot never lets us settle into mere mockery or mere sympathy.

There are weaknesses, or at least things a modern reader may find hard to love. The poem is very much a portrait of a certain kind of educated, anxious, male self-absorption. Its women are almost entirely objects of fearful regard, seen as arms, hair and perfume, and they have no voice. The line about the woman who may say "That is not what I meant at all" is the only moment she gets, and even that is Prufrock imagining her. You can read that as the poem's honesty about its narrator's limits, but you can also read it as a limit of the poem itself.

The middle sections can also feel a little overloaded. The allusions to Hamlet, Lazarus and John the Baptist arrive quickly, and some readers feel Eliot is showing off his learning. In a few lines the self-pity risks becoming so thick that the irony does not quite cut through it. And the pace sags a little where the questions pile up.

Still, these are small complaints. The poem's compression is remarkable. It runs only about a hundred and thirty lines yet feels like a short novel about a single evening of failure. Its mixture of high literary reference and shabby city detail, oyster-shells, cheap hotels, cigarette ends, was genuinely new. It succeeds because the shabbiness and the learning belong to the same man, and because the feeling underneath, the fear of living, is real.

III. The Impact It Had

Few single poems changed the course of an art form as much as this one. Before it, the typical English poem of the early twentieth century was still mostly about nature, patriotism or tidy emotion, written in smooth, regular verse. Prufrock offered a city, a neurotic, an unreliable voice and a jagged music. Ezra Pound recognized this immediately and wrote that Eliot had trained himself and modernized himself, and he worked hard to get the poem published when other editors hesitated. Harriet Monroe printed it in Poetry in 1915, and Eliot's first collection, Prufrock and Other Observations, appeared in 1917.

For Eliot himself the poem was a launch. It established him as a serious new voice before The Waste Land arrived in 1922, the work that cemented his reputation as the leading poet of his age. The dramatic monologue, a form associated with Robert Browning, was bent in Prufrock into something psychological and modern, where the speaker reveals more than he intends. That technique runs straight through later modernist poetry.

The influence beyond poetry has been just as large. Prufrock gave the language several usable phrases. People who have never read Eliot talk about measuring out their lives in coffee spoons, about the women talking of Michelangelo, or about daring to eat a peach. The poem helped create the idea of the modern anti-hero, the self-conscious, ineffectual, overthinking figure who populates twentieth-century fiction, from the narrators of Saul Bellow and Philip Roth to countless film characters. The paralysed observer, desperate for connection and unable to reach for it, became a staple.

The poem has been widely taught, often as a first doorway into modernism for students in high school and university. It is easy to point to, because it is short, dramatic and built around a recognizable human fear. It also shows students how difficult poetry can still be accessible. Its epigraph from Dante's Inferno, spoken by a soul who believes no one will ever return from hell to repeat his confession, frames the whole poem as a private confession that the speaker thinks is safe from exposure. That detail is a wonderful lesson in how an epigraph can change a poem.

The poem has also been set to music, parodied, quoted and adapted many times, and the name Prufrock itself has become shorthand for a certain timid type. Eliot reportedly took the name from a furniture company in St. Louis, which tells you something about his deadpan joke: the most romantic-sounding title in the language, attached to someone with a very ordinary, even comic, name. That tension between the grand and the petty is the poem's lasting influence on how modern writers handle feeling.

IV. What Readers Loved

What readers have loved most, again and again, is recognition. Almost everyone has stood in a doorway, rehearsing a sentence they are afraid to say, imagining the faces of the people waiting. Readers often report an odd shock on first meeting this poem, as if someone had written down thoughts they were embarrassed to have. Eliot made private social anxiety into art, and people felt less alone for it.

Many readers also love the sound. If you listen to it aloud, which is the best way to meet it, the poem moves with a strange hypnotic music. There are long, rolling lines that suggest a mind running on and on, then short, abrupt ones that suddenly drop, like a stumble. The refrain of the women and Michelangelo returns like a bored social murmur. The slow cadence of the ending, with its drowning in the sea, leaves a hush that readers remember long after the details fade.

Writers and fellow poets have admired it as a technical achievement. Ezra Pound's strong enthusiasm is the best-known example, but the poem was also taken up by young poets of the 1910s and 1920s who felt that Eliot had opened a door. His blend of colloquial speech, urban detail and literary allusion showed a path out of the stale conventions they had inherited. For many of them it felt like a permission slip to write about the actual modern world in a voice that sounded like real thought.

Readers have also loved its humour, which is easy to overlook if one thinks of Eliot as forbidding. There is a great deal of dry comedy, from the bald spot to the trousers rolled and the worry about the peach. Prufrock sees himself with an unsparing and self-deprecating eye, and that makes him lovable in spite of everything. We want him to ask his question, even as we know he will not.

Finally, readers respond to the poem's emotional honesty about wasted time and regret. The idea that a life can slip by while one waits for the right moment is a universal fear, and the poem articulates it without sentimentality. It does not offer comfort, but it offers company. People in their twenties tend to love it for its drama of nerves, while people later in life often love it more, because they have lived long enough to feel the weight of the lines about growing old, about wearing the bottoms of the trousers rolled. It is one of the few poems that seems to change with the reader's own age.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

The first reactions were not all admiring. When Prufrock and Other Observations appeared in 1917, some reviewers were baffled or openly hostile. The Times Literary Supplement notably dismissed the collection, treating it as the oddity of an author whose private musings could hardly be of public interest. Many early readers simply did not understand why a poem should be so fragmentary, so full of unexplained allusions, or why it should refuse the usual comforts of rhyme and plot. A poem about a man who does nothing struck some of them as not a poem at all.

Even among those who admired Eliot, there were doubts about his attitude. Critics have long noted that the poem reflects a narrow social world, that of cultured, well-off, anxious men, and that it treats women as a kind of threat or audience rather than as people. Later feminist critics have pointed out that the women in the poem are mostly seen from the outside and that their speech is filtered through Prufrock's fears. Others reply that this is the point and that the poem satirizes his blindness, but the question remains lively.

Some critics have also felt that the poem is too learned for its own good. The allusions to Dante, Shakespeare, the Bible and Andrew Marvell can seem like a barrier, and in the years after, Eliot's reputation for difficulty sometimes hardened into a charge of elitism. Readers who came to poetry hoping for clarity and shared feeling sometimes felt shut out by the notes and references. The reply is that the poem works even if you catch only half of them, but the objection has never gone away.

There is also an argument about whether the poem is ultimately more clever than moving. Some readers find Prufrock's self-pity so extreme that it becomes tiresome, and they wish the poem would give him a harder shove. Others suggest that the poem's fastidious irony protects it from the risk of real emotion, a charge sometimes made against Eliot's early work in general. In later decades, some critics also pointed to the poem's quiet snobbery, with its disdain for cheap hotels and sawdust restaurants, as a flaw that sits uneasily beside its sympathy.

Finally, a few critics have complained that the poem's fame has made it hard to read freshly. When a text is quoted so often, they argue, readers arrive already knowing what to feel. That is perhaps less a criticism of the poem than a hazard of being a classic. The most careful modern readers tend to agree that its reputation is deserved, while remaining aware of its blind spots, its class assumptions and the particular, limited kind of person whose consciousness it so brilliantly captures.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Prufrock sits at the start of modernism in English poetry. That movement, which flourished roughly between 1910 and 1940, broke with the polished, regular verse of the nineteenth century and tried to capture the fragmented, rapid, uncertain experience of modern city life. Alongside Pound, William Carlos Williams, H.D. and later Wallace Stevens, Eliot helped create a new poetic idiom. Prufrock was among the first major poems to show what that idiom could do.

It descends from several lines of ancestry. One is the dramatic monologue of Robert Browning and Tennyson, in which a speaker unwittingly reveals his character. Another is French Symbolism, especially the ironic, urban poetry of Jules Laforgue, whom Eliot read as a student and whose tone of weary self-mockery echoes through the poem. A third is Dante, whose Inferno supplies the epigraph, and a fourth is Shakespeare, whose Hamlet haunts the middle of the piece. What Eliot did was mix these into something wholly his own.

What descends from it is enormous. Eliot's own later work, including The Waste Land and The Hollow Men, extends its themes of spiritual emptiness, with Prufrock as an early sketch of the modern hollow person. Beyond Eliot, a long line of poets and novelists have written about paralysed, introspective characters, from the early work of W. H. Auden and John Berryman to the confessional poets who followed. Novels like Saul Bellow's Herzog, or the self-lacerating narrators of postwar fiction, feel like descendants of Prufrock's anxious inner chatter.

You can compare it fruitfully with other works. Next to Browning's "My Last Duchess," it shows how the monologue shifted from a confident monster to a timid man. Next to Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground, it shares the voice of a self-conscious, humiliated mind talking to an imagined audience. And next to Eliot's later poems, it is warmer and more human, still hopeful that a love song, however failed, could be written at all.

Why does it matter to read it today? Partly because the experience it describes has not gone away. We now live with far more ways of imagining how others see us, and social media has intensified Prufrock's dread of being pinned and wriggling on the wall. Partly because its craft remains dazzling, and it rewards re-reading over a lifetime. And partly because it is short. You can read it in ten minutes and carry it for the rest of your life. If you want to know where the modern voice in poetry began, with all its doubt, irony and ache, this is the place to start.
