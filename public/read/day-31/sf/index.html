Jack London's Teenage Oyster Pirates and the 1891 Scow Schooner Alma on San Francisco Bay

San Francisco — How fifteen-year-old Johnny London bought the sloop Razzle Dazzle to raid Morgan's Oyster Beds—while flat-bottomed 'hay scows' served as the workhorse freight trucks of the Estuary (Gilded Age Bay Maritime Era (1891–1906)) • 9 min read

“Before I was sixteen I had earned the title of Prince of the Oyster Pirates. I owned the sloop Razzle Dazzle and knew every shoal and tide-rip of San Francisco Bay.” — Jack London, John Barleycorn (1913)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Moored today at Hyde Street Pier in the San Francisco Maritime National Historical Park floats the 1891 flat-bottomed scow schooner Alma—the last surviving working 'hay scow' of the three hundred rectangular wooden sailing barges that once hauled hay, bricks, salt, and lumber across the shallow sloughs of San Francisco Bay before highway bridges existed. In that exact same year of 1891, across the water at the Oakland estuary waterfront, fifteen-year-old cannery laborer Jack London borrowed $300 from his formerly enslaved foster mother Virginia Prentiss to buy the centerboard sloop Razzle Dazzle, raiding the monopolistic Southern Pacific–backed Morgan Oyster Company beds by moonlight before joining the California Fish Patrol and immortalizing the working bay in Tales of the Fish Patrol (1905) and The Sea-Wolf (1904).

Born in San Francisco near Third and Brannan streets on January 12, 1876, and raised in working-class West Oakland during the industrial depression of the 1880s, fourteen-year-old John Griffith 'Jack' London worked ten-to-eighteen-hour shifts packing pickle jars at Hickmott's Cannery for ten cents an hour—an exhausting factory grind that left him desperate to escape onto the open water of San Francisco Bay. Every spare hour London could steal from the cannery was spent either reading adventure novels lent by Oakland Public Library poet-librarian Ina Coolbrith (who later became California's first Poet Laureate) or sailing a tiny skiff out across the Alameda mudflats toward the forest of tall masts crowding the San Francisco Embarcadero.

In the spring of 1891, at age fifteen, London turned to his beloved foster mother and former wet nurse Virginia ('Jennie') Prentiss—a formerly enslaved woman from Virginia who had helped raise him from infancy—and borrowed $300 in twenty-dollar gold pieces from her hard-saved life savings to purchase the fast centerboard sloop Razzle Dazzle from veteran pirate French Frank. Why were oysters the most lucrative contraband on San Francisco Bay in 1891? After the completion of the Transcontinental Railroad in 1869, wealthy merchants led by the Morgan Oyster Company and backed by the Southern Pacific Railroad had leased thousands of acres of public bay tidelands from San Bruno Point to San Mateo and Millbrae, importing boxcars of live Eastern Atlantic oysters (Crassostrea virginica) to fatten in fenced underwater beds.

Because the native Olympia oyster (Ostrea lurida) had been overharvested and buried by hydraulic gold-mining silt washing down from the Sierra Nevada, big meaty Eastern oysters fetched premium prices in the saloons of the Barbary Coast and the dining rooms of the Palace Hotel—creating an armed nocturnal guerrilla conflict between corporate watchmen and independent bay boatmen known as the Oyster Pirates. Sailing the Razzle Dazzle out into the South Bay on moonless nights with his young crewman Spider Healey, fifteen-year-old London learned to read the tidal currents and wind shifts of San Francisco Bay by feel: dropping anchor quietly inside Morgan's fenced oyster beds at low tide, raking burlap sacks full of prime oysters in the dark, and racing north before daybreak to sell his haul at the Oakland and San Francisco wharves.

Sharing the bay channels with London's swift centerboard sloop in that summer of 1891 was an entirely different breed of indigenous San Francisco Bay vessel: the flat-bottomed, square-bowed scow schooner—nicknamed the 'hay scow'—including the fifty-nine-foot Alma, launched in July 1891 at Fred Siemer's boatyard at Hunters Point in San Francisco. Before paved highways or bridges spanned the bay or the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta, over three hundred scow schooners like the Alma functioned as the eighteen-wheel flatbed trucks of Northern California: their broad, flat, keelless plank bottoms allowed them to sail up shallow five-foot-deep tidal sloughs in Petaluma, Alviso, and Napa, settle directly onto the mud at low tide to load mountains of baled hay for San Francisco's 40,000 workhorses, and float off on the rising flood tide.

To sail with an eighteen-foot-high deck load of hay bales blocking the helmsman's forward view entirely, scow schooner captains stood atop a tall wooden platform or steered by commands shouted back from a bow lookout seated atop the hay stack, lowering a heavy centerboard for windward stability in the rough central bay chop off Alcatraz and Angel Island. Meanwhile, after rival oyster pirates burned the mainsail of the Razzle Dazzle and sunk a partner's boat in a drunken feud along the Carquinez Strait in 1892, sixteen-year-old Jack London switched sides with characteristic pragmatism: he signed on as a deputy deputy patrolman with the state California Fish Patrol.

Cruising the northern reaches of San Francisco Bay, San Pablo Bay, and Suisun Bay aboard the patrol sloop Reindeer, London arrested illegal Chinese shrimp-net trappers and Greek salmon gill-netters who violated mesh-size conservation laws in the Sacramento River delta—real-life nocturnal chases he later turned into his 1905 book Tales of the Fish Patrol. In January 1893, days after turning seventeen, London walked across the San Francisco waterfront and signed onto the three-masted sealing schooner Sophia Sutherland as an able-bodied seaman bound for the coast of Japan and the Bering Sea—where a brutal, philosophical captain and a violent typhoon gave him the blueprint for Wolf Larsen and the schooner Ghost in his 1904 masterpiece The Sea-Wolf.

Notice how the opening scene of The Sea-Wolf happens right in the Golden Gate channel off Hyde Street Pier: literary critic Humphrey Van Weyden is riding the steam ferry Martinez across the fog-shrouded bay from Sausalito to San Francisco when a collision in the tidal rip throws him into the freezing water, to be hauled aboard Wolf Larsen's sealing schooner. While London rose from the Klondike Gold Rush of 1897 to become the highest-paid, most translated working-class author on earth before his death at age forty in 1916, thebay oyster industry he raided collapsed entirely by the 1930s due to raw municipal sewage and industrial heavy-metal runoff poisoning the South Bay mudflats.

At the same time, the completion of highway bridges and diesel tugboats rendered the bay's sailing scow schooners obsolete: dozens were stripped of their masts, converted into motorized oyster-shell dredges or mud barges, and abandoned to rot in the marshes of Alviso and Newark—except for Fred Siemer's 1891 Alma, which hauled oyster shells until 1957 before being acquired by the California State Park system in 1959. Designated a National Historic Landmark in 1988 and lovingly restored at Hyde Street Pier, the Alma still hoists her canvas gaff sails out onto the chop between Alcatraz and the Golden Gate—letting twenty-first-century passengers feel the exact heel of timber and salt spray that turned fifteen-year-old Jack London into a writer.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Naval Architecture of the Bay Scow Schooner ('Hay Scow')

Why did San Francisco Bay shipwrights like Fred Siemer build boats shaped like rectangular wooden shoeboxes with two-mast schooner rigs? Ninety percent of nineteenth-century San Francisco Bay's shoreline consisted of shallow tidal mudflats and narrow meandering salt-marsh sloughs. A deep-keel hull would run aground miles from shore, whereas a flat-bottomed chine-built scow schooner like the 1891 Alma drew less than four feet of water with its centerboard raised and could sit flat and upright on the mud at low tide while farmers loaded hay bales directly from horse wagons.

2. The Political Economy of the 1890s Bay Oyster Monopoly

Why did working-class bay communities view teenage oyster pirates like Jack London with folk-hero sympathy in 1891? Under California's 1870s tideland disposal laws, the Morgan Oyster Company—allied with the hated 'Octopus' of the Southern Pacific Railroad—had enclosed thousands of acres of traditionally public bay fishing grounds behind armed guardhouse stilts. For young Oakland and San Francisco laborers earning starvation wages in canneries, raiding the corporate beds was seen as reclaiming the maritime commons.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three historical anchors in view: first, Virginia ('Jennie') Prentiss, Jack London's Black foster mother whose $300 loan of gold coins funded the sloop Razzle Dazzle; second, the 1891 scow schooner Alma at Hyde Street Pier, the sole surviving sailing scow of the three-hundred-vessel bay fleet; and third, the opening fog collision of The Sea-Wolf (1904) set in the Golden Gate shipping channel between Sausalito and Hyde Street.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Walk to the northern end of the Hyde Street cable car line at Aquatic Park and step onto Hyde Street Pier at the San Francisco Maritime National Historical Park. Walk down the wooden pier past the 1886 square-rigger Balclutha and the 1890 steam ferry Eureka to inspect the flat bow, centerboard winch, and canvas rigging of the 1891 scow schooner Alma, and check the Park Service schedule for public bay sails.
