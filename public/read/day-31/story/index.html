Nightly Short Story • Sci-Fi & Speculative

Beyond Lies the Wub

Philip K. Dick (1952) • 13 min read

“The slovenly wub might well have said: Many men talk like philosophers and live like fools.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

In 1952, a twenty-three-year-old Californian named Philip K. Dick saw this story, his first professional publication, printed in the pulp magazine Planet Stories. He was writing fast, for pennies a word, but he was already doing something stranger than most of his rivals. The setup is standard space adventure: a freighter crew loads up with Martian animals and picks up one extra, a huge, sleepy, pig-like creature called a wub. Then the wub starts talking. Dick would spend the rest of his career asking what makes someone a real person, what counts as alive, and who gets to decide, and all three questions are already here, wrapped in a joke about dinner. The story also belongs to the early Cold War, an age of atomic fear and suspicion of anything unfamiliar. Listen for how it plays appetite against philosophy, and for how its last few lines turn the whole thing around. It is short, funny, and quietly unsettling.

The slovenly wub might well have said: Many men talk like philosophers and live like fools.

They had almost finished with the loading. Outside stood the Optus, his arms folded, his face sunk in gloom. Captain Franco walked leisurely down the gangplank, grinning.

“What’s the matter?” he said. “You’re getting paid for all this.”

The Optus said nothing. He turned away, collecting his robes. The Captain put his boot on the hem of the robe.

“Just a minute. Don’t go off. I’m not finished.”

“Oh?” The Optus turned with dignity. “I am going back to the village.” He looked toward the animals and birds being driven up the gangplank into the spaceship. “I must organize new hunts.”

Franco lit a cigarette. “Why not? You people can go out into the veldt and track it all down again. But when we run out halfway between Mars and Earth⁠—”

The Optus went off, wordless. Franco joined the first mate at the bottom of the gangplank.

“How’s it coming?” he said. He looked at his watch. “We got a good bargain here.”

The mate glanced at him sourly. “How do you explain that?”

“What’s the matter with you? We need it more than they do.”

“I’ll see you later, Captain.” The mate threaded his way up the plank, between the long-legged Martian go-birds, into the ship. Franco watched him disappear. He was just starting up after him, up the plank toward the port, when he saw it.

“My God!” He stood staring, his hands on his hips. Peterson was walking along the path, his face red, leading it by a string.

“I’m sorry, Captain,” he said, tugging at the string. Franco walked toward him.

“What is it?”

The wub stood sagging, its great body settling slowly. It was sitting down, its eyes half shut. A few flies buzzed about its flank, and it switched its tail.

It sat. There was silence.

“It’s a wub,” Peterson said. “I got it from a native for fifty cents. He said it was a very unusual animal. Very respected.”

“This?” Franco poked the great sloping side of the wub. “It’s a pig! A huge dirty pig!”

“Yes sir, it’s a pig. The natives call it a wub.”

“A huge pig. It must weigh four hundred pounds.” Franco grabbed a tuft of the rough hair. The wub gasped. Its eyes opened, small and moist. Then its great mouth twitched.

A tear rolled down the wub’s cheek and splashed on the floor.

“Maybe it’s good to eat,” Peterson said nervously.

“We’ll soon find out,” Franco said.

The wub survived the takeoff, sound asleep in the hold of the ship. When they were out in space and everything was running smoothly, Captain Franco bade his men fetch the wub upstairs so that he might perceive what manner of beast it was.

The wub grunted and wheezed, squeezing up the passageway.

“Come on,” Jones grated, pulling at the rope. The wub twisted, rubbing its skin off on the smooth chrome walls. It burst into the anteroom, tumbling down in a heap. The men leaped up.

“Good Lord,” French said. “What is it?”

“Peterson says it’s a wub,” Jones said. “It belongs to him.” He kicked at the wub. The wub stood up unsteadily, panting.

“What’s the matter with it?” French came over. “Is it going to be sick?”

They watched. The wub rolled its eyes mournfully. It gazed around at the men.

“I think it’s thirsty,” Peterson said. He went to get some water. French shook his head.

“No wonder we had so much trouble taking off. I had to reset all my ballast calculations.”

Peterson came back with the water. The wub began to lap gratefully, splashing the men.

Captain Franco appeared at the door.

“Let’s have a look at it.” He advanced, squinting critically. “You got this for fifty cents?”

“Yes, sir,” Peterson said. “It eats almost anything. I fed it on grain and it liked that. And then potatoes, and mash, and scraps from the table, and milk. It seems to enjoy eating. After it eats it lies down and goes to sleep.”

“I see,” Captain Franco said. “Now, as to its taste. That’s the real question. I doubt if there’s much point in fattening it up any more. It seems fat enough to me already. Where’s the cook? I want him here. I want to find out⁠—”

The wub stopped lapping and looked up at the Captain.

“Really, Captain,” the wub said. “I suggest we talk of other matters.”

The room was silent.

“What was that?” Franco said. “Just now.”

“The wub, sir,” Peterson said. “It spoke.”

They all looked at the wub.

“What did it say? What did it say?”

“It suggested we talk about other things.”

Franco walked toward the wub. He went all around it, examining it from every side. Then he came back over and stood with the men.

“I wonder if there’s a native inside it,” he said thoughtfully. “Maybe we should open it up and have a look.”

“Oh, goodness!” the wub cried. “Is that all you people can think of, killing and cutting?”

Franco clenched his fists. “Come out of there! Whoever you are, come out!”

Nothing stirred. The men stood together, their faces blank, staring at the wub. The wub swished its tail. It belched suddenly.

“I beg your pardon,” the wub said.

“I don’t think there’s anyone in there,” Jones said in a low voice. They all looked at each other.

The cook came in.

“You wanted me, Captain?” he said. “What’s this thing?”

“This is a wub,” Franco said. “It’s to be eaten. Will you measure it and figure out⁠—”

“I think we should have a talk,” the wub said. “I’d like to discuss this with you, Captain, if I might. I can see that you and I do not agree on some basic issues.”

The Captain took a long time to answer. The wub waited good-naturedly, licking the water from its jowls.

“Come into my office,” the Captain said at last. He turned and walked out of the room. The wub rose and padded after him. The men watched it go out. They heard it climbing the stairs.

“I wonder what the outcome will be,” the cook said. “Well, I’ll be in the kitchen. Let me know as soon as you hear.”

“Sure,” Jones said. “Sure.”

The wub eased itself down in the corner with a sigh. “You must forgive me,” it said. “I’m afraid I’m addicted to various forms of relaxation. When one is as large as I⁠—”

The Captain nodded impatiently. He sat down at his desk and folded his hands.

“All right,” he said. “Let’s get started. You’re a wub? Is that correct?”

The wub shrugged. “I suppose so. That’s what they call us, the natives, I mean. We have our own term.”

“And you speak English? You’ve been in contact with Earthmen before?”

“No.”

“Then how do you do it?”

“Speak English? Am I speaking English? I’m not conscious of speaking anything in particular. I examined your mind⁠—”

“My mind?”

“I studied the contents, especially the semantic warehouse, as I refer to it⁠—”

“I see,” the Captain said. “Telepathy. Of course.”

“We are a very old race,” the wub said. “Very old and very ponderous. It is difficult for us to move around. You can appreciate that anything so slow and heavy would be at the mercy of more agile forms of life. There was no use in our relying on physical defenses. How could we win? Too heavy to run, too soft to fight, too good-natured to hunt for game⁠—”

“How do you live?”

“Plants. Vegetables. We can eat almost anything. We’re very catholic. Tolerant, eclectic, catholic. We live and let live. That’s how we’ve gotten along.”

The wub eyed the Captain.

“And that’s why I so violently objected to this business about having me boiled. I could see the image in your mind⁠—most of me in the frozen food locker, some of me in the kettle, a bit for your pet cat⁠—”

“So you read minds?” the Captain said. “How interesting. Anything else? I mean, what else can you do along those lines?”

“A few odds and ends,” the wub said absently, staring around the room. “A nice apartment you have here, Captain. You keep it quite neat. I respect life-forms that are tidy. Some Martian birds are quite tidy. They throw things out of their nests and sweep them⁠—”

“Indeed.” The Captain nodded. “But to get back to the problem⁠—”

“Quite so. You spoke of dining on me. The taste, I am told, is good. A little fatty, but tender. But how can any lasting contact be established between your people and mine if you resort to such barbaric attitudes? Eat me? Rather you should discuss questions with me, philosophy, the arts⁠—”

The Captain stood up. “Philosophy. It might interest you to know that we will be hard put to find something to eat for the next month. An unfortunate spoilage⁠—”

“I know.” The wub nodded. “But wouldn’t it be more in accord with your principles of democracy if we all drew straws, or something along that line? After all, democracy is to protect the minority from just such infringements. Now, if each of us casts one vote⁠—”

The Captain walked to the door.

“Nuts to you,” he said. He opened the door. He opened his mouth.

He stood frozen, his mouth wide, his eyes staring, his fingers still on the knob.

The wub watched him. Presently it padded out of the room, edging past the Captain. It went down the hall, deep in meditation.

The room was quiet.

“So you see,” the wub said, “we have a common myth. Your mind contains many familiar myth symbols. Ishtar, Odysseus⁠—”

Peterson sat silently, staring at the floor. He shifted in his chair.

“Go on,” he said. “Please go on.”

“I find in your Odysseus a figure common to the mythology of most self-conscious races. As I interpret it, Odysseus wanders as an individual, aware of himself as such. This is the idea of separation, of separation from family and country. The process of individuation.”

“But Odysseus returns to his home.” Peterson looked out the port window, at the stars, endless stars, burning intently in the empty universe. “Finally he goes home.”

“As must all creatures. The moment of separation is a temporary period, a brief journey of the soul. It begins, it ends. The wanderer returns to land and race.⁠ ⁠…”

The door opened. The wub stopped, turning its great head.

Captain Franco came into the room, the men behind him. They hesitated at the door.

“Are you all right?” French said.

“Do you mean me?” Peterson said, surprised. “Why me?”

Franco lowered his gun. “Come over here,” he said to Peterson. “Get up and come here.”

There was silence.

“Go ahead,” the wub said. “It doesn’t matter.”

Peterson stood up. “What for?”

“It’s an order.”

Peterson walked to the door. French caught his arm.

“What’s going on?” Peterson wrenched loose. “What’s the matter with you?”

Captain Franco moved toward the wub. The wub looked up from where it lay in the corner, pressed against the wall.

“It is interesting,” the wub said, “that you are obsessed with the idea of eating me. I wonder why.”

“Get up,” Franco said.

“If you wish.” The wub rose, grunting. “Be patient. It is difficult for me.” It stood, gasping, its tongue lolling foolishly.

“Shoot it now,” French said.

“For God’s sake!” Peterson exclaimed. Jones turned to him quickly, his eyes gray with fear.

“You didn’t see him⁠—like a statue, standing there, his mouth open. If we hadn’t come down, he’d still be there.”

“Who? The Captain?” Peterson stared around. “But he’s all right now.”

They looked at the wub, standing in the middle of the room, its great chest rising and falling.

“Come on,” Franco said. “Out of the way.”

The men pulled aside toward the door.

“You are quite afraid, aren’t you?” the wub said. “Have I done anything to you? I am against the idea of hurting. All I have done is try to protect myself. Can you expect me to rush eagerly to my death? I am a sensible being like yourselves. I was curious to see your ship, learn about you. I suggested to the native⁠—”

The gun jerked.

“See,” Franco said. “I thought so.”

The wub settled down, panting. It put its paw out, pulling its tail around it.

“It is very warm,” the wub said. “I understand that we are close to the jets. Atomic power. You have done many wonderful things with it⁠—technically. Apparently, your scientific hierarchy is not equipped to solve moral, ethical⁠—”

Franco turned to the men, crowding behind him, wide-eyed, silent.

“I’ll do it. You can watch.”

French nodded. “Try to hit the brain. It’s no good for eating. Don’t hit the chest. If the rib cage shatters, we’ll have to pick bones out.”

“Listen,” Peterson said, licking his lips. “Has it done anything? What harm has it done? I’m asking you. And anyhow, it’s still mine. You have no right to shoot it. It doesn’t belong to you.”

Franco raised his gun.

“I’m going out,” Jones said, his face white and sick. “I don’t want to see it.”

“Me, too,” French said. The men straggled out, murmuring. Peterson lingered at the door.

“It was talking to me about myths,” he said. “It wouldn’t hurt anyone.”

He went outside.

Franco walked toward the wub. The wub looked up slowly. It swallowed.

“A very foolish thing,” it said. “I am sorry that you want to do it. There was a parable that your Saviour related⁠—”

It stopped, staring at the gun.

“Can you look me in the eye and do it?” the wub said. “Can you do that?”

The Captain gazed down. “I can look you in the eye,” he said. “Back on the farm we had hogs, dirty razorback hogs. I can do it.”

Staring down at the wub, into the gleaming, moist eyes, he pressed the trigger.

The taste was excellent.

They sat glumly around the table, some of them hardly eating at all. The only one who seemed to be enjoying himself was Captain Franco.

“More?” he said, looking around. “More? And some wine, perhaps.”

“Not me,” French said. “I think I’ll go back to the chart room.”

“Me, too.” Jones stood up, pushing his chair back. “I’ll see you later.”

The Captain watched them go. Some of the others excused themselves.

“What do you suppose the matter is?” the Captain said. He turned to Peterson. Peterson sat staring down at his plate, at the potatoes, the green peas, and at the thick slab of tender, warm meat.

He opened his mouth. No sound came.

The Captain put his hand on Peterson’s shoulder.

“It is only organic matter, now,” he said. “The life essence is gone.” He ate, spooning up the gravy with some bread. “I, myself, love to eat. It is one of the greatest things that a living creature can enjoy. Eating, resting, meditation, discussing things.”

Peterson nodded. Two more men got up and went out. The Captain drank some water and sighed.

“Well,” he said. “I must say that this was a very enjoyable meal. All the reports I had heard were quite true⁠—the taste of wub. Very fine. But I was prevented from enjoying this pleasure in times past.”

He dabbed at his lips with his napkin and leaned back in his chair. Peterson stared dejectedly at the table.

The Captain watched him intently. He leaned over.

“Come, come,” he said. “Cheer up! Let’s discuss things.”

He smiled.

“As I was saying before I was interrupted, the role of Odysseus in the myths⁠—”

Peterson jerked up, staring.

“To go on,” the Captain said. “Odysseus, as I understand him⁠—”

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its surface, this is a very simple tale. A spaceship has been loading up on a Martian trading stop, and a crewman named Peterson buys a wub from a native for fifty cents. It is a four-hundred-pound pig-like animal that sags, sleeps, and eats almost anything. The ship's captain, Franco, looks at it and sees one thing: meat. The crew is short on food, and the wub looks fat enough. Then the wub speaks, politely suggesting that they talk about other matters, and the story becomes a debate about whether you can eat something that can argue with you.

The debate is lopsided, and that is the point. The wub is gentle, patient, and wise. It explains that its people are old and ponderous, too heavy to run and too soft to fight, so they survived by being tolerant. It talks about democracy and suggests the crew draw straws instead of simply picking the victim. It talks about myth, and especially about the Odyssey, reading Odysseus as a figure who stands for separation and return, the individual who wanders and finally goes home. Peterson, the one crewman who listens, is plainly moved. Franco, meanwhile, gets more irritated with every reasonable sentence. His answer to philosophy is a gun. "Back on the farm we had hogs, dirty razorback hogs. I can do it." He looks the wub in the eye and shoots it.

The first layer, then, is a moral fable about cruelty and appetite. Franco cannot afford to see the wub as a person, because a person would be murder and a pig would be dinner. He picks the label that lets him do what he already wants to do. Notice how often the wub notes this itself: it wonders aloud why Franco is so obsessed with eating it.

The second layer is the ending, which is the cleverest part. At dinner the crew can barely eat. Only the Captain enjoys himself. He announces that the meat is only organic matter now, that the life essence is gone, and then he leans toward Peterson and begins, "As I was saying before I was interrupted, the role of Odysseus in the myths." Franco is now talking in the wub's voice, with the wub's ideas. Peterson jerks up and stares, and the story ends.

What exactly happened is left open. Perhaps the wub's mind survived death and moved into the man who ate its body. Perhaps it took over earlier, when Franco stood frozen with his mouth open at his office door. Either way, the person Franco killed has not been removed from the world. He has been swallowed and is still talking. The deepest joke is that the captain who insisted the wub was nothing but food has become, quite literally, what he ate.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

As craft, this is a remarkably confident piece for a first publication, and it is worth being specific about why. The strongest quality is tone. Dick keeps the surface pulpy, with a gruff captain, a ship, and a hold full of animals, and then lets an absurdly articulate pig take over the conversation. The comedy depends on the wub's mildness. It belches and says "I beg your pardon." It admits it is addicted to relaxation. It compliments the captain on how tidy his office is. A creature this courteous makes the humans look worse with each exchange, and Dick never has to underline it.

The structure is also well made. The story moves in clean steps: the discovery, the first conversation, the Captain's paralysis, the second conversation with Peterson, the execution, the dinner, the last line. It is short and nothing is wasted. The ending lands because everything before it has set up the wub's habit of drifting toward Odysseus whenever it is left alone with a listener. When Franco picks up that thread, the reader hears it before Peterson does.

There are weaknesses, and they should be named. The characters are thin. Franco is a blustering brute with a farm-boy past, and the crew members French and Jones are barely distinguishable from each other. Peterson is mainly a conscience with a name. That is partly the nature of the form, since a pulp magazine story of a few thousand words has little room for portraiture, but it means the story works more as a fable than as a drama.

The science is also loose in a way modern readers may find distracting. Telepathy is invoked without explanation. The wub's ability to freeze the Captain with a look is never accounted for, and the final possession happens by magic, not by any mechanism. Dick clearly did not care, and he was right not to, because the story is about ideas and not machinery. Still, the wub's philosophizing can run a little neat. Its remarks about democracy protecting the minority and its ethical complaints about atomic power are slightly on the nose, as if the author were making sure the reader got the message. Dick is subtler when the wub just talks about Odysseus.

One more point of balance: the story is sometimes praised for its horror, but it is hardly frightening. It is closer to a dark joke with a shudder at the end. That is part of its charm, and also a limit. The twist works as a punchline more than as an emotional blow, and the death of the wub, which should hurt, is handled quickly and slightly coldly. For all that, it is an unusually good example of how much a skilled writer can fit into a tiny box.

III. The Impact It Had

Within Dick's career, this story matters first of all as a beginning. It was his first professional publication, in Planet Stories in July 1952, at the start of an astonishing burst of short fiction. Over the next few years he wrote and sold a huge number of stories, often for a penny or two a word, and this one shows nearly all the themes he would return to for the rest of his life. The idea that identity can be hijacked, that a mind may inhabit the wrong body, that the line between human and not human is unstable, and that authority figures often confuse their own appetites with principle: all of these run through his later novels, from the paranoid worlds of the 1960s to the late religious experiments.

The story's influence has been quieter than that of Dick's famous novels, which is natural. Nobody films a short, odd pulp story the way they film a full-length book. But it has been reprinted in many collections of his stories and remains one of his best-known early pieces. It is often the story people are handed when they want to see what Dick was like before the great novels. Teachers of science fiction use it as an example of the genre doing philosophy, because it is easy to read, funny, and short enough to discuss in a single class.

Its ideas have also seeped into later popular culture without much credit. The notion of an alien creature that argues for its own life in perfect reasonable language, and so forces its captors to face what they are doing, recurs in many later stories, as does the notion of a visitor who is gentler than the people who capture it. The refusal to say what the possession means, leaving the reader with an uneasy last image, is a technique that later writers of short horror and science fiction have used often.

There is a larger effect that is harder to measure. Dick helped teach science fiction readers to be suspicious of the point of view of the person with the gun. In a great deal of earlier pulp fiction, the human spaceship crew was the hero and the alien was the threat. Here, the humans are the ones with something to be ashamed of, and the alien is a better thinker and a better person. Dick was not the first to do this. But he did it with such good humor that it was easy to take in.

Finally, it fits the mood of its time. Published as the Korean War was grinding on and Americans were worrying about atomic weapons and spies, it asks what a society is willing to destroy simply because it is strange. Readers in the early 1950s did not need that spelled out, and Dick did not spell it out.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about this story? The short answer is the wub. It is one of the most endearing creatures in early science fiction, and much of its appeal is that it is so unheroic. It is fat, tired, and polite. It does not fight back. It does not even try very hard to escape. It simply keeps talking, and the more it talks, the more likable it becomes. Readers feel the injustice of its fate because it is so good-natured. A more dramatic alien would have been easier to dislike and so would have made the captain's choice less shameful.

Many readers also enjoy the humor. Dick has a natural comic ear. The wub's discussion of his own taste, "a little fatty, but tender", and the captain's grunted "Nuts to you" as he opens the door are funny precisely because the characters take themselves so seriously. Franco has a pulp hero's jaw and a pulp hero's lines, and the story quietly lets the air out of him. The exchange where the wub suggests that democracy should protect it from being eaten, and the Captain simply stops listening, is a small gem of satire.

The ending gets its own share of admiration. Readers who encounter the story for the first time often say they felt a jolt at the last line, when Franco begins to speak about Odysseus. It is the kind of ending that makes you go back to the beginning to see how it was done. Dick's contemporaries in the magazines were fond of twist endings, but many of them were mechanical. This one has an emotional logic, because it is built from the wub's character. The creature is a talker, and in the end it talks.

There is also a real affection for the story among people who care about Dick's whole body of work. For them it carries a particular pleasure of recognition. Here is the writer, twenty-three years old and not yet famous, already asking what makes a mind a mind, already worried about the way ordinary people turn other beings into things so as to use them. Readers who know the later novels often describe the story as a seed. It is small, but you can find the whole plant in it.

Finally, a good number of readers simply appreciate its kindness toward the idea of thinking. The wub's greatest enthusiasm is for myth and meaning, not for power. In a magazine full of rayguns and rocket ships, it is a pleasure to meet an alien whose weapon is conversation, and whose idea of a good evening is a long talk about Homer.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

There are not many famous attacks on this story, and it is fair to say so. It was a pulp tale in a pulp magazine, and the kind of detailed contemporary reviewing that a novel might receive was not given to a short piece in Planet Stories. So most of what can be said about its weaknesses comes from later readers and critics looking back at Dick's early work, not from any big public controversy.

The most common criticism of this kind concerns depth. Some readers feel that the story is clever without being truly moving. It has a neat idea and a neat twist, but the characters are sketched rather than felt, and the wub's death does not carry the weight it might. Critics who prefer Dick's novels often say that the early stories are more like jokes with philosophical punchlines than like finished fiction. They find the writing serviceable and the plotting tidy, but they miss the strangeness and personal anguish that fill the later work.

A second line of criticism has to do with the message. The moral is delivered rather heavily, in that the wub itself explains the ethics of the situation: its remarks about democracy, killing, and technology are close to speeches. Some readers feel that a better version would let the actions speak and trust the audience to draw the conclusion. Others point out that a story where the animal is more articulate than every human in the room risks sentimentality. The wub is almost too good, and the crew almost too bad, so that the moral comfort of the reader is never really tested.

There is also a more modern objection that a few readers raise. The story asks us to feel sympathy for the wub because it can talk and reason, and the argument it makes is, in effect, that intelligence makes killing wrong. Some readers have noted that this sits uneasily with the fact that the wub is, at first, just an animal that looks like a pig, and that the story does not ask whether the pig would deserve mercy if it did not speak. The Captain's farm memories of razorback hogs hint at this, but the story does not follow the thought. It might be read as a plea for vegetarianism, or merely as a plea for tolerance, and Dick does not choose.

Finally, a handful of readers find the ending a little too easy. The possession is unexplained and a bit magical, and one can argue that it undercuts the moral of the story, since the wub gets its revenge. If the creature survives anyway, was its killing really a tragedy? Dick seems to want both effects at once, and that is slightly greedy. Whether you find that a flaw or a strength is a matter of taste.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place this story, begin with the tradition it comes from. In the late 1940s and early 1950s, American science fiction was mostly written for cheap magazines, and the typical story was about men with ray guns exploring or conquering planets. Planet Stories, where this appeared, was famous for brash adventure. But the genre also had a thoughtful side, represented by writers such as Ray Bradbury, Theodore Sturgeon, and Clifford Simak, who used the alien to talk about what is human. Dick belongs to that second tradition. His wub is a cousin of the gentle Martians and sympathetic visitors found in that kind of story.

Behind them all is an older line of satire, which uses a visitor from elsewhere to expose the absurdity and cruelty of ordinary society. Jonathan Swift, in Gulliver's Travels, did this by sending a man among giants and wise horses. The wise animal who is more rational than the people who capture it is directly in that line, and the wub's ironic manners would not have shocked Swift. There is also a hint of the old fable, where animals talk so that humans may be reminded of their own behavior.

Looking forward, the story points toward the central obsessions of Dick's later work. In novels such as Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?, written in the 1960s, the question of whether a being deserves moral treatment depends on whether it has an inner life, and the humans who deny that inner life are often the ones who have lost their own. The idea of identity being taken over, which closes this story, appears again and again in his fiction, in the replacement of humans by imposters or machines. Seen this way, the wub is an early draft of the android: a being that looks like a thing and turns out to be a person.

It can also be compared with other stories that ask who counts as a person. Later fiction about intelligent animals, uploaded minds, and artificial intelligence keeps returning to the same awkward question, and this story is an economical early version. It does not have the scale of a novel, but it has a purity that bigger works sometimes lose.

Why read it today? Partly because it is quick and funny, which makes it an ideal entrance into Dick's world. Partly because the central question has not gone away. We still decide, often with great speed and little thought, which creatures deserve our attention and which are only material, and we still tend to reach for the label that justifies what we already wanted to do. Dick saw that very early, in a story about a pig that would not stop talking. It is worth the ten minutes it takes.
