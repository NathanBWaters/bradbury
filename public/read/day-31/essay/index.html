Nightly Essay • Transcendentalism & Nature

Night and Moonlight

Henry David Thoreau (1863) • 15 min read

Chancing to take a memorable walk by moonlight some years ago, I resolved to take more such walks, and make acquaintance with another side of nature.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Night and Moonlight is one of the last essays by Henry David Thoreau to reach print, and it appeared after he was gone. Thoreau died of tuberculosis in May 1862, at forty-four. The essay ran in The Atlantic Monthly in November 1863, put together from his journals by friends and family. For years he had walked around Concord, Massachusetts, after dark while his neighbors slept, writing down what the night looked, smelled and sounded like. The essay matters because it takes something we think we know, a walk in the moonlight, and treats it like an unexplored continent. Thoreau was writing in the age of American Transcendentalism, a movement that trusted direct experience of nature over inherited authority. His claim here is gentle but bold. Daylight shows us the world's busy surface, and night shows us a quieter, stranger half that we mostly sleep through. Notice how often he turns a small sensory detail into a thought about the mind.

Chancing to take a memorable walk by moonlight some years ago, I resolved to take more such walks, and make acquaintance with another side of nature. I have done so.

According to Pliny, there is a stone in Arabia called Selenites, “wherein is a white, which increases and decreases with the moon.” My journal for the last year or two, has been selenitic in this sense.

Is not the midnight like Central Africa to most of us? Are we not tempted to explore it⁠—to penetrate to the shores of its lake Tchad, and discover the source of its Nile, perchance the Mountains of the Moon? Who knows what fertility and beauty, moral and natural, are there to be found? In the Mountains of the Moon, in the Central Africa of the night, there is where all Niles have their hidden heads. The expeditions up the Nile as yet extend but to the Cataracts, or perchance to the mouth of the White Nile; but it is the Black Nile that concerns us.

I shall be a benefactor if I conquer some realms from the night, if I report to the gazettes anything transpiring about us at that season worthy of their attention⁠—if I can show men that there is some beauty awake while they are asleep⁠—if I add to the domains of poetry.

Night is certainly more novel and less profane than day. I soon discovered that I was acquainted only with its complexion, and as for the moon, I had seen her only as it were through a crevice in a shutter, occasionally. Why not walk a little way in her light?

Suppose you attend to the suggestions which the moon makes for one month, commonly in vain, will it not be very different from anything in literature or religion? But why not study this Sanskrit? What if one moon has come and gone with its world of poetry, its weird teachings, its oracular suggestions⁠—so divine a creature freighted with hints for me, and I have not used her? One moon gone by unnoticed?

I think it was Dr. Chalmers who said, criticising Coleridge, that for his part he wanted ideas which he could see all round, and not such as he must look at away up in the heavens. Such a man, one would say, would never look at the moon, because she never turns her other side to us. The light which comes from ideas which have their orbit as distant from the earth, and which is no less cheering and enlightening to the benighted traveller than that of the moon and stars, is naturally reproached or nicknamed as moonshine by such. They are moonshine, are they? Well, then, do your night-travelling when there is no moon to light you; but I will be thankful for the light that reaches me from the star of least magnitude. Stars are lesser or greater only as they appear to us so. I will be thankful that I see so much as one side of a celestial idea, one side of the rainbow and the sunset sky.

Men talk glibly enough about moonshine, as if they knew its qualities very well, and despised them⁠—as owls might talk of sunshine. None of your sunshine!⁠—but this word commonly means merely something which they do not understand, which they are abed and asleep to, however much it may be worth their while to be up and awake to it.

It must be allowed that the light of the moon, sufficient though it is for the pensive walker, and not disproportionate to the inner light we have, is very inferior in quality and intensity to that of the sun. But the moon is not to be judged alone by the quantity of light she sends to us, but also by her influence on the earth and its inhabitants. “The moon gravitates toward the earth, and the earth reciprocally toward the moon.” The poet who walks by moonlight is conscious of a tide in his thought which is to be referred to lunar influence. I will endeavor to separate the tide in my thoughts from the current distractions of the day. I would warn my hearers that they must not try my thoughts by a daylight standard, but endeavor to realize that I speak out of the night. All depends on your point of view. In Drake’s Collection of Voyages, Wafer says of some Albinoes among the Indians of Darien⁠—“They are quite white, but their whiteness is like that of a horse, quite different from the fair or pale European, as they have not the least tincture of a blush or sanguine complexion.⁠ ⁠… Their eyebrows are milk-white, as is likewise the hair of their heads, which is very fine.⁠ ⁠… They seldom go abroad in the daytime, the sun being disagreeable to them, and causing their eyes, which are weak and poring, to water, especially if it shines towards them, yet they see very well by moonlight, from which we call them moon-eyed.”

Neither in our thoughts in these moonlight walks, methinks, is there “the least tincture of a blush or sanguine complexion,” but we are intellectually and morally Albinoes⁠—children of Endymion⁠—such is the effect of conversing much with the moon.

I complain of Arctic voyagers that they do not enough remind us of the constant peculiar dreariness of the scenery, and the perpetual twilight of the Arctic night. So he whose theme is moonlight, though he may find it difficult, must, as it were, illustrate it with the light of the moon alone.

Many men walk by day; few walk by night. It is a very different season. Take a July night, for instance. About ten o’clock⁠—when man is asleep, and day fairly forgotten⁠—the beauty of moonlight is seen over lonely pastures where cattle are silently feeding. On all sides novelties present themselves. Instead of the sun there are the moon and stars, instead of the wood-thrush there is the whip-poor-will⁠—instead of butterflies in the meadows, fireflies, winged sparks of fire! who would have believed it? What kind of cool deliberate life dwells in those dewy abodes associated with a spark of fire? So man has fire in his eyes, or blood, or brain. Instead of singing birds, the half-throttled note of a cuckoo flying over, the croaking of frogs, and the intenser dream of crickets⁠—but above all, the wonderful trump of the bullfrog, ringing from Maine to Georgia. The potato-vines stand upright, the corn grows apace, the bushes loom, the grainfields are boundless. On our open river terraces once cultivated by the Indian, they appear to occupy the ground like an army⁠—their heads nodding in the breeze. Small trees and shrubs are seen in the midst, overwhelmed as by an inundation. The shadows of rocks and trees and shrubs and hills are more conspicuous than the objects themselves. The slightest irregularities in the ground are revealed by the shadows, and what the feet find comparatively smooth appears rough and diversified in consequence. For the same reason the whole landscape is more variegated and picturesque than by day. The smallest recesses in the rocks are dim and cavernous; the ferns in the wood appear of tropical size. The sweet fern and indigo in overgrown wood-paths wet you with dew up to your middle. The leaves of the shrub-oak are shining as if a liquid were flowing over them. The pools seen through the trees are as full of light as the sky. “The light of the day takes refuge in their bosoms,” as the Purana says of the ocean. All white objects are more remarkable than by day. A distant cliff looks like a phosphorescent space on a hillside. The woods are heavy and dark. Nature slumbers. You see the moonlight reflected from particular stumps in the recesses of the forest, as if she selected what to shine on. These small fractions of her light remind one of the plant called moonseed⁠—as if the moon were sowing it in such places.

In the night the eyes are partly closed or retire into the head. Other senses take the lead. The walker is guided as well by the sense of smell. Every plant and field and forest emits its odor now⁠—swamp-pink in the meadow and tansy in the road; and there is the peculiar dry scent of corn which has begun to show its tassels. The senses both of hearing and smelling are more alert. We hear the tinkling of rills which we never detected before. From time to time, high up on the sides of hills, you pass through a stratum of warm air: a blast which has come up from the sultry plains of noon. It tells of the day, of sunny noontide hours and banks, of the laborer wiping his brow and the bee humming amid flowers. It is an air in which work has been done⁠—which men have breathed. It circulates about from woodside to hillside like a dog that has lost its master, now that the sun is gone. The rocks retain all night the warmth of the sun which they have absorbed. And so does the sand: if you dig a few inches into it you find a warm bed.

You lie on your back on a rock in a pasture on the top of some bare hill at midnight, and speculate on the height of the starry canopy. The stars are the jewels of the night, and perchance surpass anything which day has to show. A companion with whom I was sailing one very windy but bright moonlight night, when the stars were few and faint, thought that a man could get along with them, though he was considerably reduced in his circumstances⁠—that they were a kind of bread and cheese that never failed.

No wonder that there have been astrologers⁠—that some have conceived that they were personally related to particular stars. Du Bartas, as translated by Sylvester, says he’ll

“not believe that the great architect With all these fires the heavenly arches decked Only for show, and with these glistering shields, T’ awake poor shepherds, watching in the fields,”⁠—

he’ll

“not believe that the least flower which pranks Our garden borders, or our common banks, And the least stone, that in her warming lap Our Mother Earth doth covetously wrap, Hath some peculiar virtue of its own, And that the glorious stars of heav’n have none.”

And Sir Walter Raleigh well says, “the stars are instruments of far greater use than to give an obscure light, and for men to gaze on after sunset”; and he quotes Plotinus as affirming that they “are significant, but not efficient”; and also Augustine as saying, “Deus regit inferiora corpora per superiora”: God rules the bodies below by those above. But best of all is this which another writer has expressed: “Sapiens adjuvabit opus astrorum quemadmodum agricola terrae naturam”: A wise man assisteth the work of the stars as the husbandman helpeth the nature of the soil.

It does not concern men who are asleep in their beds, but it is very important to the traveller, whether the moon shines brightly or is obscured. It is not easy to realize the serene joy of all the earth, when she commences to shine unobstructedly, unless you have often been abroad alone in moonlight nights. She seems to be waging continual war with the clouds in your behalf. Yet we fancy the clouds to be her foes also. She comes on magnifying her dangers by her light, revealing, displaying them in all their hugeness and blackness⁠—then suddenly casts them behind into the light concealed, and goes her way triumphant through a small space of clear sky.

In short, the moon traversing, or appearing to traverse, the small clouds which lie in her way, now obscured by them, now easily dissipating and shining through them, makes the drama of the moonlight night to all watchers and night-travellers. Sailors speak of it as the moon eating up the clouds. The traveller all alone, the moon all alone, except for his sympathy, overcoming with incessant victory whole squadrons of clouds above the forests and lakes and hills. When she is obscured, he so sympathizes with her that he could whip a dog for her relief, as Indians do. When she enters on a clear field of great extent in the heavens, and shines unobstructedly, he is glad. And when she has fought her way through all the squadron of her foes, and rides majestic in a clear sky unscathed, and there are no more any obstructions in her path, he cheerfully and confidently pursues his way, and rejoices in his heart, and the cricket also seems to express joy in its song.

How insupportable would be the days, if the night with its dews and darkness did not come to restore the drooping world! As the shades begin to gather around us, our primeval instincts are aroused, and we steal forth from our lairs, like the inhabitants of the jungle, in search of those silent and brooding thoughts which are the natural prey of the intellect.

Richter says that “the earth is every day overspread with the veil of night for the same reason as the cages of birds are darkened, namely, that we may the more readily apprehend the higher harmonies of thought in the hush and quiet of darkness. Thoughts which day turns into smoke and mist stand about us in the night as light and flames; even as the column which fluctuates above the crater of Vesuvius in the daytime appears a pillar of cloud, but by night a pillar of fire.”

There are nights in this climate of such serene and majestic beauty, so medicinal and fertilizing to the spirit, that methinks a sensitive nature would not devote them to oblivion, and perhaps there is no man but would be better and wiser for spending them out of doors, though he should sleep all the next day to pay for it; should sleep an Endymion sleep, as the ancients expressed it⁠—nights which warrant the Grecian epithet ambrosial, when, as in the land of Beulah, the atmosphere is charged with dewy fragrance, and with music, and we take our repose and have our dreams awake⁠—when the moon, not secondary to the sun,

“gives us his blaze again, Void of its flame, and sheds a softer day. Now through the passing cloud she seems to stoop, Now up the pure cerulean rides sublime.”

Diana still hunts in the New England sky.

“In Heaven queen she is among the spheres. She, mistress-like, makes all things to be pure. Eternity in her oft change she bears; She Beauty is; by her the fair endure.”

“Time wears her not; she doth his chariot guide; Mortality below her orb is placed; By her lie virtues of the stars down slide; By her is Virtue’s perfect image cast.”

The Hindus compare the moon to a saintly being who has reached the last stage of bodily existence.

Great restorer of antiquity, great enchanter! In a mild night, when the harvest or hunter’s moon shines unobstructedly, the houses in our village, whatever architect they may have had by day, acknowledge only a master. The village street is then as wild as the forest. New and old things are confounded. I know not whether I am sitting on the ruins of a wall, or on the material which is to compose a new one. Nature is an instructed and impartial teacher, spreading no crude opinions, and flattering none; she will be neither radical nor conservative. Consider the moonlight, so civil, yet so savage!

The light is more proportionate to our knowledge than that of day. It is no more dusky in ordinary nights than our mind’s habitual atmosphere, and the moonlight is as bright as our most illuminated moments are.

“In such a night let me abroad remain Till morning breaks, and all’s confused again.”

Of what significance the light of day, if it is not the reflection of an inward dawn?⁠—to what purpose is the veil of night withdrawn, if the morning reveals nothing to the soul? It is merely garish and glaring.

When Ossian, in his address to the Sun, exclaims,

“Where has darkness its dwelling? Where is the cavernous home of the stars, When thou quickly followest their steps, Pursuing them like a hunter in the sky⁠— Thou climbing the lofty hills, They descending on barren mountains?”

who does not in his thought accompany the stars to their “cavernous home,” “descending” with them “on barren mountains?”

Nevertheless, even by night the sky is blue and not black; for we see through the shadow of the earth into the distant atmosphere of day, where the sunbeams are revelling.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Thoreau opens by announcing an expedition, and the surprise is where he says it will go. He asks whether midnight is not like Central Africa to most of us, a place with its own lake and its own Nile, and he calls himself a benefactor if he can "conquer some realms from the night." In the 1850s, when this journal material was written, explorers were racing to find the source of the Nile, and the Mountains of the Moon were a famous blank on the map. Thoreau borrows that excitement and points it at his own village. The unknown is not far away. It begins at dusk, a few steps from the front door.

The first big idea is that daylight is only half the story. He says he had known night only by its complexion, and had seen the moon only through a crack in a shutter. Then comes a joke with a point. People call an idea moonshine when they do not understand it, he says, much as owls might sneer at sunshine. He is defending a kind of thought that is dim and far away and hard to look at squarely, against critics who want only ideas they can see all the way around. The moon never turns her other side to us, and he is content to be thankful for one side of a celestial idea.

Then the essay becomes a walk, and the walk is sensory rather than argumentative. In a July night the fireflies replace the butterflies, the whip-poor-will replaces the wood thrush, and the shadows of rocks and trees become more noticeable than the things themselves. Shadows reveal every small bump in the ground, so the landscape looks more varied by night than by day. Because the eyes are partly closed, other senses take over, and he describes the smell of swamp pink and tansy and the sound of tiny streams he never heard before. One of the loveliest moments is a pocket of warm air high on a hillside, left over from the day, which he says circulates about "like a dog that has lost its master."

Underneath, he is making a claim about attention. The moon does not give us more light, it gives us less, and that is the point. The dimness strips away the clutter, and the mind moves in a gentler tide. He says the light of the moon is not unlike the light of our own minds, "as bright as our most illuminated moments are." When he asks what the daylight is worth if it does not reflect "an inward dawn," he reveals what the whole essay is about. The night is a mirror for the inner life.

A first-time reader might miss that the essay is also quietly about sleep and waking. The people asleep in their beds are, in his view, missing something, and the word "moonshine" is a gentle accusation against the sleepers. Near the end he says the village street at night is "as wild as the forest," and old and new things are confounded. For a few hours human order loosens, and a person can sit not knowing whether he is on the ruins of a wall or the foundation of a new one. The closing observation, that even at night the sky is blue and not black because we see through the earth's shadow to the day beyond, is a quiet promise that the dark is not the opposite of light but a different way of looking at it.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Night and Moonlight is a mixed achievement, and the mix is part of its interest. At its best it is some of the most precise nature writing in American prose. The July night passage, with its fireflies, bullfrogs and army-like rows of corn nodding on the river terraces, is as vivid as anything Thoreau wrote. The detail that the shrub-oak leaves "are shining as if a liquid were flowing over them" is the work of a person who has actually stood in that field at midnight and looked hard. His observation about shadows, that they reveal irregularities the feet find smooth, is both true and new, and it shows how his sharp eye for the physical world feeds his larger ideas.

The weaker side is the way the essay is built. Because it was assembled from journal entries after his death, it does not travel in a straight line. It moves from the Nile to the moon to the albinos of Darien to a July night to the stars to astrology to the village street, and the joins are sometimes visible. The long middle section on the stars is a pile of quotations, from Du Bartas, Raleigh, Plotinus, Augustine and a Latin proverb, that slows the walk down. A modern reader, and especially a listener, may feel the scholarship getting in the way of the senses. Thoreau was a voracious reader, but here the reading sometimes crowds out the seeing.

Some of the wit lands and some does not. The pun on moonshine is wonderful, and the remark from a sailing companion that stars are "a kind of bread and cheese that never failed" is funny and humane. But the passage about the moon fighting the clouds, with the traveler so sympathetic that he "could whip a dog for her relief, as Indians do," is both over-extended and dated in its casual ethnography. The comparison of the thinker to an albino, drawn from an old travel book, is meant as a conceit about being pale and nocturnal in thought, yet it rests on a period description of people that we read now with discomfort.

There is also a certain self-regard in the opening. Thoreau casts himself as an explorer and a benefactor of the human race for taking walks after dark. The tone is half serious and half joking, and the joke does not always cover the pride. He warns his "hearers" not to try his thoughts by a daylight standard, which is a clever way to protect the essay from criticism, since any objection can be dismissed as coming from a person who was not there.

Still, the essay earns its place through its central move. By treating moonlight as a different mode of knowing rather than as a gloomy version of day, it gives a fresh answer to an old Romantic theme. The final paragraphs, where light is judged by whether it "reflects an inward dawn," pull the scattered observations into a single image, and the ending on the blue of the night sky is quietly perfect. The flaws are real, but they are the flaws of a patchwork, and the patches are mostly gorgeous.

III. The Impact It Had

The most direct impact of Night and Moonlight is on the afterlife of Thoreau himself. He died in May 1862, and for a year or two the essays kept appearing. His sister Sophia and his friends worked through his manuscripts, and The Atlantic Monthly published several of the late pieces, among them Walking, Autumnal Tints and Wild Apples, before this one. Night and Moonlight then appeared in the collection called Excursions in 1863, edited by Sophia, with a biographical sketch by Ralph Waldo Emerson. Together these pieces shifted Thoreau's reputation. Before his death he was known mostly as a local eccentric and the author of two books that had not sold well. Afterward he began to look like a major American writer, and the late nature essays, with their ease and their ripe, personal voice, were a large part of why.

The essay also left a long mark on how Americans talk about the night. It is a founding text for the idea that the dark has its own beauty and is not merely an absence. Naturalists, hikers and amateur astronomers have returned to it for generations, and in recent decades writers on light pollution and the loss of the dark sky have often cited Thoreau's walk as an example of what we stand to lose. When he writes that he would be a benefactor if he could show men "that there is some beauty awake while they are asleep," a modern reader hears a plea for protecting the night itself.

Among writers, the influence is gentle but wide. The tradition of the solitary walker who turns a local landscape into a philosophy runs from Thoreau through John Muir and John Burroughs to Annie Dillard, Barry Lopez and a long line of contemporary nature writers. Dillard's essays about attention, in particular, owe a debt to his habit of treating a small patch of ground as an entire world to be explored. The specific idea that a single sense or mode of seeing can be changed by changing the hour is echoed in many later night walks, from the Romantic poets back to Thoreau's own models and forward to modern writing about foraging, bird song and astronomy.

The essay has not had much of a life on the stage or screen, and it has no famous adaptations. Its life has been in the classroom and the anthology. It is often taught as an example of Transcendentalism at its most sensory, and also as a model of how to describe a place by changing your point of view. Teachers like it because it is short, because its images are concrete, and because it lets students see that a nature essay can be a work of philosophy.

Finally, it matters as evidence of how Thoreau worked. Readers of the journals know that this essay was constructed from the daily record he kept for years, and the essay shows his method plainly: observe, write it down, return to it, and slowly draw out the idea. That method, and the example of a writer who believed that one patch of ordinary ground could hold a lifetime of discovery, is probably its deepest legacy.

IV. What Readers Loved

What do readers love about this essay? First, they love the sense of permission. Most of us feel that the day is for doing and the night is for sleeping, and Thoreau gently says that we are allowed, even encouraged, to go out and look. Many people have read the essay and then, quite literally, walked outside after dark for the first time in years. It is an essay that sends you out the door.

They also love the physical detail. Thoreau does not tell us that night is mysterious and leave it there. He tells us that a distant cliff looks like "a phosphorescent space on a hillside," that the pools seen through the trees are as full of light as the sky, that the sand holds the sun's warmth all night so that if you dig a few inches you find a warm bed. These are small, checkable facts, and a reader who goes and checks them finds them true. That is a rare pleasure in prose that is also philosophical, because the philosophy never floats away from the ground.

Readers love the humor too, though it is easy to miss. The joke about moonshine, the owls scorning the sun, the sailor's remark about the stars as bread and cheese, and the idea that someone might take notes for the gazettes about what is "transpiring about us" at midnight all show a writer who does not take himself quite as solemnly as his reputation suggests. Thoreau's best readers have always noticed that he is funny, and this essay is a good place to hear it.

There is the emotional pull of the moon passage, in which a lone traveler sympathizes with the moon as she struggles through the clouds. Whatever its excesses, that image gets at something people feel on a walk at night: the odd companionship of the sky, the sense that the moon is traveling with you and that the cricket is celebrating when she breaks free. It is a small piece of pure feeling, and many readers remember it long after the Latin quotations have faded.

And finally readers love the central idea that the night is "more novel and less profane than day." The word profane is key. He means that daylight is the world of buying and selling, of work and chatter, while moonlight is a more sacred, less used time. People who feel overwhelmed by the noise of modern life tend to read this as an invitation to a kind of quiet that is still available, free, and just outside the door. It speaks to anyone who has ever felt that their best thoughts arrive after the world has gone to bed.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Compared with Walden or Civil Disobedience, Night and Moonlight drew little organized criticism in its own day, and it is worth being honest about that. It appeared posthumously, in a magazine that treated Thoreau with respect, and the discussions of it were mostly part of larger assessments of his career. There is no famous review of this essay in particular to point to. What we have instead is a set of general criticisms of Thoreau's style and outlook that apply to it, and some objections that have grown stronger over time.

One persistent complaint, voiced even by admirers in the nineteenth century, is that Thoreau can be mannered and show-offy. James Russell Lowell, in a well-known essay on Thoreau in 1865, praised his observation but complained about his love of paradox and his tendency to strain for effect. Some readers feel that touch in the opening of this essay, where the walk after dark is elevated into an exploration of the Nile, and in the way he plays with the notion of himself as a benefactor of mankind. To a skeptical reader, the grandness is out of proportion to the actual subject, which is a man walking around his village.

A related complaint is about density and learning. The essay is packed with quotations from Pliny, Sylvester's translation of Du Bartas, Raleigh, Richter, Ossian and a handful of Latin phrases. Some readers find this enriching, and others find it a barrier. They feel that Thoreau, who preached direct experience, is leaning heavily on books, and that the central stretch on the stars reads like a commonplace book rather than a walk. It is an old objection to Transcendentalist prose in general, that it can be at once anti-bookish and bookish.

Then there are the moral and cultural objections that modern readers bring. The reference to Indians whipping a dog for the moon's relief, and the extended comparison with the albinos of Darien taken from an old voyage narrative, rely on the casual and often stereotyped writing about other peoples that was common in the period. Many contemporary readers note these passages and read around them. The essay's mystical tone, with its Hindu and Greek references, can also feel to some like a loose collage in which cultures are borrowed for atmosphere rather than understood.

Finally, a more practical criticism: the essay offers little narrative, no argument in the strict sense, and little structure. Because the editors assembled it from journal entries, the order feels loose, and a reader who wants a thesis may find it elusive. Defenders reply that this is the point, that a night walk is not a march toward a conclusion. But the objection is fair, and it explains why this essay is usually ranked below his best-known work.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

To place Night and Moonlight, start with Transcendentalism, the New England movement of the 1830s and 1840s led by Emerson that argued that people could know truth directly through nature and intuition, without relying on church or tradition. Emerson's Nature, published in 1836, is the movement's founding statement, and Thoreau was his younger neighbor and student. But Thoreau pushed the idea in a more physical direction. Where Emerson often wrote about nature as a mirror for the soul, Thoreau put on boots and went to look, and this essay shows the method clearly: the mind's tide is the moon's tide, and the evidence is in his feet and nose.

The essay also descends from a long tradition of moon poetry and night writing. The Romantics, especially Wordsworth and Coleridge, wrote about walking and about moonlit scenes, and Thoreau's reference to Coleridge in the essay is no accident. Behind them lie older sources, the classical Diana and Endymion, the Hindu texts he quotes, and the English poets of the seventeenth century. Thoreau gathers all of these into a single walk, and it is one of the reasons the essay feels like a conversation across time.

Looking forward, the essay is a seed of American nature writing. John Muir's night passages in the Sierra, John Burroughs's essays on the woods near his home, and the modern lineage of Annie Dillard's Pilgrim at Tinker Creek and Barry Lopez's Arctic Dreams all grow from the notion that a close and patient look at the local world can yield large meaning. The night walk has also become its own form, running through writing on owls, stars, bats, and the loss of dark skies.

Compare it with Thoreau's own other work. Walden is the great book of daylight and seasons, while this essay is a small sibling about the other half of the day. Winter Walk and Walking, also from his late years, share the habit of treating a ramble as a philosophy, but Night and Moonlight is the most inward, because the dimness narrows the world to what you can smell, hear and feel. It also pairs naturally with Emily Dickinson's poems about dusk and Whitman's night poems, which find the same expanding feeling in the dark.

Why read it now? Partly because it is short and beautiful, and partly because its subject has become more precious. We live in a time when many people rarely see a truly dark sky, and a stretch of clear night has become something we have to travel to find. Thoreau's essay reminds us that the dark is a place with its own life, its own smells and sounds and light, and that it is available to anyone willing to put down the lamp and go for a walk. It is an old essay that has aged into a timely one.
