San Francisco's Post-1906 Auxiliary Water Supply System, 172 Brick Cisterns, and the Golden Hydrant

San Francisco — How Chief Engineer Marsden Manson built a gravity-fed high-pressure firefighting network from Twin Peaks to the bay—and why the hydrant at 20th and Church gets painted gold every April 18 (Post-Earthquake Reconstruction Era (1906–1913) to Present) • 9 min read

“Never again shall San Francisco burn for want of water when the earth shakes.” — City Engineer Marsden Manson, Report on the Auxiliary Water Supply System (1908)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Walk past the brick rings inlaid at dozens of San Francisco street intersections or stand at the southwest corner of 20th and Church streets above Mission Dolores Park beside the gleaming Golden Fire Hydrant, and you are looking at the visible surface of one of the most ingenious seismic lifelines on earth: the San Francisco Fire Department's Auxiliary Water Supply System (AWSS). After the April 18, 1906 earthquake snapped the Spring Valley Water Company's wrought-iron mains across liquefied ground and left firefighters helpless against a three-day conflagration—save for the lone working hydrant at 20th and Church that saved the Mission District—City Engineer Marsden Manson designed a $5.2 million, 135-mile independent high-pressure network fed by gravity from the 750-foot Twin Peaks Reservoir, backed by two saltwater bay pumping stations and 172 subterranean cisterns.

For years before April 1906, San Francisco Fire Chief Dennis T. Sullivan had warned the Board of Supervisors that the city was a tinderbox resting on a single point of failure: ninety percent of San Francisco's buildings were framed in resinous coast redwood, and the privately owned Spring Valley Water Company carried all its water from Peninsula reservoirs through three brittle wrought-iron conduits that crossed the San Andreas Fault and marshy bay fill. In one of the cruelest ironies in urban history, at 5:12 a.m. on April 18, 1906, the first violent jolt of the magnitude 7.9 earthquake toppled the heavy brick chimney of the California Hotel onto the adjacent firehouse at Bush and Kearny streets, crashing through the roof directly onto Chief Dennis Sullivan's bed and mortally wounding the one man who had planned the city's defense against a great conflagration.

Within those same forty-seven seconds of shaking, lateral ground spreading in the old marsh beds South of Market and at Valencia Street sheared more than three hundred breaks in the Spring Valley water mains, draining eighty million gallons of reservoir water into the dirt and leaving fire engine crews hooking their hoses up to bone-dry cast-iron hydrants as fifty-two separate fires ignited. Over the next seventy-two hours, as the firestorm consumed 4.7 square miles and 28,188 buildings across downtown, Chinatown, Nob Hill, and South of Market, a solitary wall of flame marched south along Dolores and Guerrero streets on Friday, April 20, 1906, threatening to leap 20th Street and burn the entire Mission District and Eureka Valley to the ground.

At the southwest corner of 20th and Church streets, on the high serpentine bedrock ridge overlooking Mission Dolores Park, exhausted firefighters of Engine Company 38 and hundreds of neighborhood volunteers—who had been soaking rooftops with wet burlap sacks and dipole buckets of sewer water and wine—uncapped a high-lying Spring Valley hydrant and heard a miraculous sound: a hissing gush of pressurized water. Working in six-hour relays through heat so intense it blistered the paint on their pumper engine, firefighters and residents used the water from that lone working hydrant at 20th and Church streets to hold the fire line along 20th Street and save historic Mission Dolores (1791) and the entire southern half of San Francisco.

Determined that San Francisco would never again face a firestorm with empty hoses, voters in May 1908 overwhelmingly approved a $5.2 million municipal bond issue to build City Engineer Marsden Manson's visionary Auxiliary Water Supply System (AWSS)—a firefighting water grid completely independent from the city's domestic drinking water pipes. Completed between 1909 and 1913, Manson's AWSS relied first and foremost on an indestructible physical force that required no electricity: gravity. High atop Twin Peaks at 758 feet above sea level, engineers excavated a divided 10.5-million-gallon open reservoir, connected via two mid-slope pressure-zone tanks on Ashbury Heights (490 feet) and Jones Street on Nob Hill (370 feet).

From those elevated tanks, 135 miles of extra-thick cast-iron pipes (one to two inches thick) with flexible lead-caulked bell-and-spigot joints carried fresh water to oversized, thick-walled white-topped high-pressure hydrants capable of delivering water at up to 300 pounds per square inch—so powerful that a single AWSS hydrant equals the output of three standard fire engines and requires specially braced brass manifold monitors. What if an earthquake breached a pipeline crossing one of the city's known liquefaction zones—the old creek beds and filled coves whose boundaries matched the exact 1906 ground-failure map? Manson divided the AWSS into isolated 'infirm-ground' pipe loops controlled by motorized gate valves that could instantly seal off a broken block while keeping the rest of the city pressurized.

And what if the fresh water in Twin Peaks Reservoir ran low during a multi-day conflagration? Manson built two monumental Beaux-Arts steam-powered saltwater pumping stations directly on the bay shore—Pumping Station No. 1 at Second and Townsend streets (1912) and Pumping Station No. 2 inside historic Fort Mason at Aquatic Park (1913)—each capable of pumping 10,000 gallons of inexhaustible San Francisco Bay seawater per minute into the AWSS grid. To make the system survival-proof even if buried pipes failed on a local block, Manson also revived and expanded an 1850s Gold Rush tradition: beneath 172 key street intersections across the city, crews excavated circular subterranean brick and reinforced-concrete cisterns, each holding 75,000 gallons of static water and marked on the street surface by a distinctive thirty-foot circle of red brick cobblestones.

Those 172 underground cisterns are completely detached from any pipes: even if every water main in San Francisco shattered, a fire engine can park inside the brick circle at an intersection, drop a suction hose down the center manhole, and pump 75,000 gallons of water onto surrounding buildings. Manson's post-1906 foresight was put to the ultimate test at 5:04 p.m. on October 17, 1989, when the magnitude 6.9 Loma Prieta earthquake ruptured domestic water mains in the liquefied sand fill of the Marina District and ignited a roaring three-story gas-fed fire at Beach and Divisadero streets.

While the San Francisco Fire Department's fireboat Phoenix pumped bay water from the Marina yacht harbor through five-inch portable hoses, the AWSS saltwater stations and underground cisterns held the line across the city—prompting San Francisco voters in 2010 and 2014 to invest over $400 million in modernizing and expanding the AWSS into the western neighborhoods. Every year at 5:12 a.m. on April 18, after the early-morning gathering at Lotta's Fountain on Market Street, firefighters, historians, and neighbors walk to the corner of 20th and Church streets with cans of metallic spray paint to give the Little Giant hydrant a fresh coat of shimmering gold—honoring the water that saved a city.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Three-Tier Redundancy: Gravity Reservoirs, Bay Saltwater Pumps, and 172 Cisterns

Why do civil engineers worldwide study Marsden Manson's 1908–1913 Auxiliary Water Supply System as a masterpiece of resilient systems design? Because it layers three independent failure-proof supplies: first, gravity-fed fresh water dropping 758 feet from Twin Peaks Reservoir at up to 300 psi; second, two bayfront pumping stations (plus the fireboats Phoenix, Guardian, and St. Francis) that can inject unlimited Pacific Ocean saltwater into the mains; and third, 172 pipe-independent 75,000-gallon underground cisterns marked by brick rings in the street.

2. How to Spot an AWSS High-Pressure Hydrant vs. a Domestic Hydrant

As you walk San Francisco's sidewalks, you can read the city's seismic engineering by looking at its fire hydrants. Smaller, slender white hydrants connect to the low-pressure domestic drinking water mains. The larger, heavy-shouldered cast-iron hydrants with bolted flanges and painted black, blue, or red bonnets (indicating Twin Peaks, Ashbury, or Jones Street pressure zones) are AWSS high-pressure hydrants—and the brick circles in the middle of street intersections mark the 75,000-gallon subterranean cisterns.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Keep three historical touchstones in view: first, Fire Chief Dennis T. Sullivan, who warned of the city's water vulnerability before dying in the 1906 quake; second, the Golden Fire Hydrant at 20th and Church streets, which held the southern fire line on April 20, 1906; and third, Pumping Station No. 2 at Van Ness Avenue and Aquatic Park, where historic high-pressure pumps still stand ready beside the bay.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Take the J-Church Muni line to the southwest corner of 20th and Church streets at the top of Mission Dolores Park to inspect the gleaming Golden Fire Hydrant and its bronze commemorative plaque, while enjoying one of the best skyline panoramas in the city. As you walk down 20th Street into the Mission, look at the center of the street intersections for the circular brick rings marking the 75,000-gallon underground cisterns.
