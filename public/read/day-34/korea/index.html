Hallasan's Baengnokdam Crater & 368 Parasitic Oreums: A Vertical Continent on One Island

“A mountain so high that if you stand on its summit, your hand can pull down the Milky Way (Eunhasu).” — Origin of the name Hallasan (漢拏山, 'Pulling the Milky Way')

Key Context & Why It Matters

In Jeju Island geography, villagers do not say 'Hallasan is on Jeju Island'; they say 'Hallasan IS Jeju Island.' Rising 1,947.06 meters straight out of the Korea Strait as South Korea's highest peak, Mount Hallasan is a massive Quaternary shield volcano whose gentle basaltic flanks are studded with 368 parasitic volcanic cones called oreum—the highest concentration of secondary cones on any shield volcano in the world. Crowned by the 550-meter-wide summit crater lake of Baengnokdam ('White Deer Lake') and dramatic trachyte lava domes, a single four-hour hike up Hallasan compresses a 3,000-kilometer latitudinal journey from Taiwan's subtropics to Siberia's subarctic tundra. Anchored at Mount Hallasan on Jeju Island, this dispatch traces how traditional Korean innovation and historical memory shaped the enduring identity of the peninsula.

Look at **Jeju Island** from a satellite orbiting 400 kilometers above the East China Sea, and you see immediately why Jeju elders repeat the saying: *'Jeju is Hallasan, and Hallasan is Jeju.'* The entire elliptical island—stretching seventy-three kilometers east-to-west and thirty-one kilometers north-to-south—is the exposed upper cone of a single colossal **Quaternary shield volcano**, **Mount Hallasan** (한라산, 漢拏山), rising **1,947.06 meters** (6,388 feet) above sea level as the highest mountain in South Korea. When field historians and architectural conservators arrive at Mount Hallasan Summit (Baengnokdam), Seogwipo & Jeju City, Jeju Island (33.3617° N, 126.5292° E) to study Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake), they immediately notice how the physical terrain of Mount Hallasan on Jeju Island shaped every decision made during the Cenozoic Quaternary Volcanism (1.8 Million Years Ago – 1002 CE) to Present. Even the mountain’s name is pure astronomical poetry: **Han** (한, 漢) refers to *Eunhasu* (the Milky Way galaxy), and **La** (라, 拏) means 'to grasp or pull with the hand'—signifying a peak so lofty that a climber standing on its rim can reach up and pull down the Milky Way! In the broader geopolitical and cultural timeline of the Cenozoic Quaternary Volcanism (1.8 Million Years Ago – 1002 CE) to Present, the rise of Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake) represented far more than an isolated local experiment. Across the Korean peninsula, court chroniclers and regional guilds recognized that mastering 368 Oreum (368개 오름 parasitic monogenetic volcanic cones) and Gusangnamu (구상나무 endemic Ice Age relict Korean Fir, Abies koreana) was essential to preserving cultural sovereignty and social cohesion during periods of intense ecological, political, and philosophical transformation.

Geologically, Hallasan was born through four major stages of intraplate volcanic activity spanning the last **1.8 million years** (beginning with underwater hydrovolcanic *Seogwipo Formation* eruptions on the continental shelf and culminating in the summit crater eruptions of 25,000 to 19,000 years ago, with historical eruptions recorded on the island’s flanks in **1002 CE and 1007 CE** during the Goryeo Dynasty in the *Goryeosa*!). Look closely at the summit crater of **Baengnokdam** (백록담, 白鹿潭, 'White Deer Lake'—named after the Taoist legend that star-crowned immortals descended from heaven to ride white deer and drink from its crystal waters). Measuring roughly 550 meters east-to-west, 400 meters north-to-south, and 108 meters deep with a circumference of 1.72 kilometers, Baengnokdam holds a secret visible in the color and texture of its two walls. Why is the **western wall** of Baengnokdam a sheer, towering cliff of pale gray columnar-jointed rock (including the dramatic 160-meter cliff of *Janggunbawi* and the *Yeongsil Gi-am* 'Five Hundred Generals' rock pillars), while the **eastern wall** is a gentle, dark-reddish slope of porous volcanic scoria and black basalt? Core-drilling and radiometric dating by the Jeju World Natural Heritage Center revealed that around 37,000 years ago, viscous, silica-rich **trachyte** (*jomyeonam*, 조면암) magma squeezed up like thick toothpaste to form a massive steep lava dome at the summit. Then, around **19,000 years ago** (at the peak of the Last Glacial Maximum), a hotter, fluid **basaltic** (*hyeonmuam*, 현무암) eruption blasted out just east of the trachyte dome, blowing open the Baengnokdam crater and burying only the eastern half under black basalt and red scoria—leaving the ancient pale trachyte cliff standing intact on the western half! Equally astonishing are Hallasan’s children: across the sweeping five-degree lower flanks of the shield volcano rise **368 parasitic volcanic cones** known in the native Jeju language as **Oreum** (오름, derived from *oreuda*, 'to climb'). No other shield volcano on Earth—not even Mauna Kea in Hawaii or Mount Etna in Sicily—possesses such a dense concentration of monogenetic scoria cones, tuff rings, and lava domes on a single island! Just as importantly, the secondary subsystems of Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake) were engineered to work in continuous feedback with the surrounding environment. Every *oreum*—from *Sangumburi* (a 650-meter-wide explosion pit crater that never spilled lava), *Geum-oreum*, and *Darangswi-oreum* ('Queen of the Oreums' with its perfectly symmetrical funnel crater) to *Suwolbong* and *Seongsan Ilchulbong* on the coast—erupted just once over a few weeks or months and went extinct, dotting the island like 368 green velvet hills where wild Jeju horses (*Jorangmal*) graze. Thus, a single four-hour climb up Hallasan takes you through the exact same vegetation zones as walking **3,000 kilometers from subtropical Taiwan to subarctic Siberia**! At the coastal foot of the mountain (0 to 200 meters), you walk through **subtropical broadleaved evergreens**: camellias (*dongbaek*), prickly-pear cacti growing wild on coastal basalt, and * Machilus thunbergii* (*Hubak-namu*). Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Volcanology: Dual-Magma Architecture of the Baengnokdam Summit Crater

Radiometric Ar-Ar and radiocarbon dating conducted by the Jeju World Natural Heritage Center resolved the geological puzzle of Hallasan's asymmetric summit. Around 37,000 years ago, high-viscosity, silica-rich trachyte magma extruded at the summit to form a steep-sided dome (preserving the sheer western cliff of Baengnokdam and the columnar-jointed crags of Yeongsil). Roughly 19,000 years ago, a lower-viscosity basaltic eruption pierced the eastern flank of the dome, excavating the 108-meter-deep Baengnokdam crater and mantling the eastern slope in dark scoria. Studying the structural interplay between Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake) and 368 Oreum (368개 오름 parasitic monogenetic volcanic cones) within the context of the Cenozoic Quaternary Volcanism (1.8 Million Years Ago – 1002 CE) to Present demonstrates how Korean master practitioners united empirical physics with enduring aesthetic restraint.

2. Island Biogeography: Pleistocene Glacial Relicts & 'Abies koreana'

Hallasan's upper subalpine zone (1,400–1,947 m) is a textbook laboratory of Pleistocene glacial refugia. Connected to the Asian mainland during the Last Glacial Maximum when sea levels dropped 120 meters, Jeju received boreal flora such as crowberry (Empetrum nigrum var. japonicum, 시로미) and ancestors of the endemic Korean fir (Abies koreana, 구상나무). Marooned when Holocene sea levels rose, these species survived by migrating vertically into the cold summit microclimate—where Korean scientists now operate seed banks to protect Gusangnamu from climate-driven dieback. Without these primary archival records and archaeological strata at Mount Hallasan on Jeju Island, modern historians would never have reconstructed the full lineage connecting Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake) to Gusangnamu (구상나무 endemic Ice Age relict Korean Fir, Abies koreana) across the centuries.

3. Key Korean & Jeju Concepts to Remember

Keep three Hallasan terms in mind: first, Baengnokdam (백록담, 'White Deer Lake'), the 1.72-kilometer-circumference summit crater lake at 1,947 meters; second, Oreum (오름), the native Jeju word for the 368 parasitic volcanic cones dotting the shield volcano's slopes; and third, Gusangnamu (구상나무, Abies koreana), the Korean endemic subalpine fir whose silver-backed needles and upright purple cones became the parent stock of modern European Christmas trees. Mastering these three Korean terms—Hallasan & Baengnokdam (한라산과 백록담 1,947m shield volcano and summit crater lake), 368 Oreum (368개 오름 parasitic monogenetic volcanic cones), and Gusangnamu (구상나무 endemic Ice Age relict Korean Fir, Abies koreana)—equips any traveler or reader to decode the deeper cultural grammar embedded in the landscape of Mount Hallasan on Jeju Island.
