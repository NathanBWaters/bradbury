Nightly Poem • Modernist & Imagist

Preludes

T. S. Eliot (1915) • 5 min read

From winter evenings of steak-smells and burnt-out cigarettes to muddy morning trams, four urban nocturnes uncover an infinitely gentle, infinitely suffering thing.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Preludes is one of the first great poems about the modern city, and T. S. Eliot wrote it when he was barely out of his teens. He drafted it around 1910 and 1911, while moving between Harvard in Massachusetts and Paris, and it was not published until July 1915, in Blast, a loud, brightly colored magazine edited by the painter and writer Wyndham Lewis for the avant-garde movement called Vorticism. It appeared just before Eliot's first big success, Prufrock. The title borrows from music, the short piano preludes of Chopin, but Eliot's music is made of cheap steaks, wet leaves, tired horses and rented rooms. In four brief sections it follows a city through one evening, one morning, and the people who live in it, and then closes on a strange and tender thought about suffering. It is a poem about how ordinary urban life can feel both mean and strangely sacred, glimpsed through a window.

The winter evening settles down

With smell of steaks in passageways.

Six o’clock.

The burnt-out ends of smoky days.

And now a gusty shower wraps

The grimy scraps

Of withered leaves about your feet

And newspapers from vacant lots;

The showers beat

On broken blinds and chimney-pots,

And at the corner of the street

A lonely cab-horse steams and stamps.

And then the lighting of the lamps.

The morning comes to consciousness

Of faint stale smells of beer

From the sawdust-trampled street

With all its muddy feet that press

To early coffee-stands.

With the other masquerades

That time resumes,

One thinks of all the hands

That are raising dingy shades

In a thousand furnished rooms.

You tossed a blanket from the bed,

You lay upon your back, and waited;

You dozed, and watched the night revealing

The thousand sordid images

Of which your soul was constituted;

They flickered against the ceiling.

And when all the world came back

And the light crept up between the shutters,

And you heard the sparrows in the gutters,

You had such a vision of the street

As the street hardly understands;

Sitting along the bed’s edge, where

You curled the papers from your hair,

Or clasped the yellow soles of feet

In the palms of both soiled hands.

His soul stretched tight across the skies

That fade behind a city block,

Or trampled by insistent feet

At four and five and six o’clock;

And short square fingers stuffing pipes,

And evening newspapers, and eyes

Assured of certain certainties,

The conscience of a blackened street

Impatient to assume the world.

I am moved by fancies that are curled

Around these images, and cling:

The notion of some infinitely gentle

Infinitely suffering thing.

Wipe your hand across your mouth, and laugh;

The worlds revolve like ancient women

Gathering fuel in vacant lots.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

Preludes is built like a series of short camera shots, almost a film before film poetry. It is divided into four numbered parts, and each pans across the same grimy city, from the evening, through the night, to the morning and back to the evening. There is no plot and no named character. The poem is a mood, assembled out of smells, sounds and small movements.

The first part opens with "The winter evening settles down / With smell of steaks in passageways." Right away you are in a working-class street, with cooking smells hanging in the stairwells. Then comes the marvellous bleak phrase "The burnt-out ends of smoky days." A day is imagined as something like a cigarette, smoked down to the butt. Then rain whips "grimy scraps / Of withered leaves" and newspapers around your feet, and the section closes with a lonely cab-horse steaming and stamping at the corner, and "the lighting of the lamps." That last line is softly ironic. Lamplight might sound romantic, but here it is just the next dreary stage of the evening.

The second part takes us to morning, which "comes to consciousness / Of faint stale smells of beer." The city is waking like a hungover person. Feet press toward the coffee-stands, and the poem imagines hands raising "dingy shades / In a thousand furnished rooms." The phrase "other masquerades" suggests that daily life is just another costume party, a performance repeated by thousands of strangers.

The third part switches to "you," and enters a single room. A person tosses a blanket from the bed, lies awake and watches "the thousand sordid images / Of which your soul was constituted" flicker on the ceiling. It is an astonishing way to describe the mind at dawn, when your own mental refuse comes crowding up. When light arrives, you have "such a vision of the street / As the street hardly understands," a moment of clarity that the busy world cannot share.

The fourth part widens again. A "soul" is "stretched tight across the skies / That fade behind a city block," or "trampled by insistent feet." The crowd, with "short square fingers stuffing pipes," and "eyes / Assured of certain certainties," is the "conscience of a blackened street / Impatient to assume the world." The speaker steps forward in the first person, "I am moved by fancies that are curled / Around these images," and names a sudden tenderness for "some infinitely gentle / Infinitely suffering thing." Then he undercuts it. "Wipe your hand across your mouth, and laugh; / The worlds revolve like ancient women / Gathering fuel in vacant lots." The poem ends by shrugging off pity, as if embarrassed by its own feeling, and leaves us with an image of cosmic poverty.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

Preludes works through the sharpness of its seeing and the tact of its pacing. Eliot has a rare eye for the telling detail, the stale beer, the withered leaves, the cab-horse, the sparrows in the gutters, and he arranges these details with a musician's care. Each section has its own tempo. The first is a slow settling, the second a stirring, the third intimate and slightly feverish, and the fourth reflective and more abstract. The variety keeps a poem with no plot from going slack.

The language is exact and often surprising. The phrase "The conscience of a blackened street / Impatient to assume the world" delivers a whole social portrait in a few words. It evokes the self-righteous, determined crowd that thinks it owns the future, while the soot of the street suggests how little of that confidence is deserved. Similarly, the line about the soul being "trampled by insistent feet / At four and five and six o'clock" captures the way a mass of commuters can flatten individual spirit. These are real discoveries, not just decorations.

The poem also handles feeling with unusual restraint. Eliot lets the grimness do its own work and only gives way to compassion in a single quick turn near the end. The line "The notion of some infinitely gentle / Infinitely suffering thing" is moving partly because it arrives so unexpectedly and is so unadorned. The repetition of "infinitely" is simple, almost childlike, and effective.

The weaknesses are mostly those of a very young poet. The poem does not quite resolve its own attitude. The closing lines turn away from the vision of suffering with a sort of cynical shrug, and some readers feel that Eliot is a little too eager to disown his own sympathy. The final image of the ancient women gathering fuel is memorable, but its meaning is elusive, and it can feel more like a flourish than a conclusion.

There is also a distance in the poem's treatment of people. The city dwellers are seen from outside, as feet, hands, fingers and eyes, rather than as individuals. That is deliberate, and part of the poem's point about how modern life reduces people to parts, but it also gives the poem a slightly patrician air. Eliot observes the poor with an educated detachment, and one occasionally senses a touch of disdain behind the pity.

Yet these complaints are small against the poem's accomplishment. At barely fifty lines, it creates an entire atmosphere, and it does so with an economy that most poets of the time could not match. As an early work it is astonishingly assured, and it holds its place beside the more famous Prufrock.

III. The Impact It Had

Preludes mattered because it helped establish the modern urban lyric in English. Before Eliot, the city appeared in English poetry mostly as a backdrop or a moral warning. The French poet Charles Baudelaire had made the modern city a subject of serious art a half century earlier, and Jules Laforgue had given it a tone of weary irony, but English poetry had been slow to follow. Eliot, who read both closely, carried their methods across the Channel and the Atlantic, and Preludes was one of the first results.

Its publication in Blast in 1915 is significant. Blast was the manifesto of Vorticism, a short-lived British avant-garde movement that sought a harder, more energetic art for the machine age. Appearing there put Eliot's poem in company with experimental painting and typography, and signaled that this was a radical new kind of verse. It also reflects the energetic support of Ezra Pound, who was then championing new writing and connecting Eliot with Lewis. The poem's brief, visual, image-driven manner has obvious kinship with Imagism, the movement Pound had helped to launch.

Within Eliot's career, Preludes belongs to the early group of poems collected in Prufrock and Other Observations in 1917, and it helped build his reputation as the poet of the modern city. Its images of rooms, streets and dawn return in The Waste Land, with its crowds flowing over London Bridge, and in the later city scenes of Four Quartets. The phrase about the "thousand sordid images / Of which your soul was constituted" anticipates the fragmentary inner world of the longer poems.

Beyond Eliot, the poem influenced a generation of poets who turned to the city for subject matter. Writers of the 1930s, such as W. H. Auden and Louis MacNeice, wrote urban poetry that owes something to this tradition, as did American poets like Hart Crane and William Carlos Williams, who took the city in different, more celebratory directions. The idea of a poem as a sequence of brief glimpses, edited together like film shots, became a standard technique.

In schools, Preludes is taught as a miniature primer in modernist method, showing how a poem can build meaning from images rather than argument. It is also valued as an example of how Eliot used musical form, with its title and its movement through contrasting sections, to structure a poem. Though less famous than Prufrock or The Waste Land, it has quietly earned a secure and respected place in the canon of twentieth-century poetry.

IV. What Readers Loved

Readers have loved Preludes first for its immediacy. You do not need to know anything about Eliot, modernism or music to feel it. The smell of steaks in a passageway, the damp leaves, the horse steaming at the corner, are things we can almost taste and touch. The poem draws you into a place within a few lines, and that sensory directness is rare in poetry that is also intellectually ambitious.

They have loved its mood of urban loneliness, which feels astonishingly current. Anyone who has lived in a big city, who has looked at the lit windows of strangers, ridden the early train or woken at dawn in a rented room, will recognize the feelings the poem captures. The idea that thousands of people are doing the same small routines, each in a private room, behind a shade, and each unseen by the others, is a staple of urban experience, and Eliot said it with great economy.

Many readers are particularly moved by the third section, the poem's most intimate moment. The picture of someone lying awake at night, watching the images of their own life flicker on the ceiling, is one of the most truthful descriptions of insomnia and self-reckoning in the language. The detail of the person curling "the papers from your hair" in the morning is homely and touching, and gives the poem a human warmth that its colder passages might otherwise lack.

Fans of Eliot also value the poem as a glimpse of the poet before fame. It shows him already in command of his main methods, the fragment, the sudden turn, the mixture of disgust and compassion, yet with a freshness that sometimes gets lost in his later, more heavily annotated work. Readers who find The Waste Land forbidding often find Preludes a more approachable way into his world.

And finally there is the moment of tenderness near the end. The notion of an "infinitely gentle / Infinitely suffering thing" suggests something like a compassion at the heart of the city's misery, a hint of the sacred in the ordinary. Whether one reads it as religious or simply human, readers have found it a quietly powerful moment, all the more affecting because the poem so quickly turns away from it. It lets us feel that even in a poem so determined to be hard, something soft has slipped through.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

It should be said honestly that Preludes has not generated a great storm of critical debate. It is a modest, short poem by a very famous author, and much of the commentary is appreciative or simply explanatory. Nevertheless, there are some real lines of criticism, and they are worth noting.

The most frequent is the charge of condescension. Critics have asked whether the poem looks down on the people it describes. The crowd is reduced to feet, hands and fingers, and described as having eyes "assured of certain certainties," a phrase that carries an unmistakable note of superiority. Some readers see here the attitude of an educated, comfortable young man observing the lives of the poor from a safe distance, with more fastidiousness than fellow feeling. Later critics concerned with class have found this a recurring limitation in early Eliot, and Preludes offers a clear example.

A second objection concerns the retreat at the end. After the brief moment of sympathy, the speaker instructs "you" to wipe a hand across the mouth and laugh. Some critics find this a failure of nerve, a way of dodging the emotional consequences of what the poem has just seen. Others read it more sympathetically, as a bitter and realistic acknowledgement of how people actually cope with pity. But there is no consensus, and the ending remains a point of argument.

A third question is whether the poem is more of a technical exercise than a fully achieved work. Because it is an early piece, apparently written under the influence of Laforgue and Baudelaire, some readers see it as derivative, an impressive imitation rather than a wholly original voice. The title's reference to music and to Wordsworth's The Prelude is also sometimes seen as more clever than productive, a pun that does not deepen the poem greatly.

There was also the question, at the time, of how readers would receive such unpoetic material. In the 1910s, many readers were not accustomed to poems that dwelt on stale beer and dirty feet, and some found the subject matter ugly or unworthy. That resistance has largely faded, but it was real. Taken together, the objections are modest. They suggest the poem is a minor but polished work, with limits of sympathy and ambition, rather than a flawed one. Most critics agree that these limits are part of its character as a youthful, sharp-eyed sketch.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Preludes sits in the early phase of high modernism, in the same cluster of poems as Prufrock and Rhapsody on a Windy Night, and it shares their sense of the modern city as both a physical place and a state of mind. Modernism, broadly, was the movement in early twentieth-century art that tried to break from the smooth conventions of the nineteenth century and capture modern experience in new, often fragmented forms. Preludes is a small but characteristic example.

Its main ancestry runs through France. Charles Baudelaire, in Les Fleurs du mal and the Parisian Scenes that Eliot admired, first treated the ugliness and beauty of the modern metropolis as material for serious art. Jules Laforgue added a tone of ironic weariness and a flexible, conversational line. Eliot absorbed both, and also drew on the English tradition of Wordsworth, whose long autobiographical poem The Prelude gave Eliot's title an ironic echo. Where Wordsworth finds growth of the mind in nature, Eliot finds the mind mulling over streets and rented rooms.

There is also a link to music and to the visual arts. Chopin's piano preludes, short, atmospheric pieces, lend the poem its form, and the picture-like quality of the poem's sections connects it to the Imagist demand for direct treatment of the thing and to the Vorticist taste for hard-edged modern scenes. It is a poem that seems to anticipate cinema, with its cutting between scenes and its focus on telling details.

What descends from it is a large body of urban poetry. The tradition runs from the Auden generation to the city sequences of later poets like Frank O'Hara in New York, though O'Hara's mood is warmer, and the broader idea that a poem can be built from a walk through a city. Eliot's own later London poetry, including the great crowd scenes of The Waste Land, grows from here.

In comparison with Eliot's other early poems, Preludes is more compressed and less ironic than Prufrock, less dreamlike than Rhapsody on a Windy Night, and more openly tender than either. It reads like a rehearsal for the larger structures to come, and the sudden flash of compassion at the end anticipates the religious concern of his later career.

Why read it now? Because it is brief, vivid and true to an experience still shared by millions. It will take you five minutes. It shows what a young poet, with a good ear and a sharp eye, can do with a winter evening. And it leaves behind a haunting question, about what the "infinitely gentle, infinitely suffering thing" might be, that lasts well beyond the final line.
