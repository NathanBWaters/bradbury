Tongyeong Najeonchilgi: Cutting Starlight from Abalone Shells into Poisonous Lacquer

Korea — Where Admiral Yi Sun-sin's twelve naval workshops birthed master artisans who saw 0.1-millimeter mother-of-pearl ribbons into self-polymerizing urushiol sap (Goryeo & Joseon Naval Headquarters (1604) to Present) • 9 min read

“From the toxic sap of the lacquer tree comes eternal darkness; from the shell at the bottom of the cold sea comes eternal light.” — Master Song Bang-ung, Living National Treasure of Najeonjang

Key Context & Why It Matters

Overlooking the emerald islands of the Hallyeosudo Marine National Park on Korea's southern coast, the port city of Tongyeong takes its very name from the Samdo Sugun Tongjeyeong—the Supreme Naval Headquarters of the Three Provinces established by Admiral Yi Sun-sin and permanently sited here in 1604 around the grand wooden hall of Sebyeonggwan (National Treasure No. 305). To manufacture weapons and royal tribute goods to fund the fleet, the naval command established the Twelve Workshops (Sibigongbang), bringing together the finest master craftsmen in the kingdom. Foremost among them were the masters of Najeonchilgi (mother-of-pearl lacquerware), who inlaid paper-thin iridescent abalone shell from Tongyeong's cold currents into jet-black natural ottchil lacquer.

Step inside a quiet, north-lit workshop in Tongyeong on the southern coast of South Gyeongsang Province, overlooking the island-studded waters of the Hallyeosudo Strait. At a low wooden workbench (sangja), a master artisan of Najeonjang (National Intangible Cultural Heritage No. 10) sits hunched over a wooden V-notched sawing board. Using a custom fret saw whose blade is ground from a watch spring thinner than a sewing needle, the master cuts a sheet of iridescent New Zealand or Tongyeong abalone shell (jeonbok-pae) only 0.1 millimeters thick into microscopic plum blossoms, cranes, and geometric lattices that shimmer in electric emerald, violet, and magenta.

This thousand-year-old Korean art is Najeonchilgi : Na (spiral conch/shell), Jeon (inlay/adornment), Chil (natural ottchil tree lacquer), and Gi (vessel). Already in the twelfth century during the Goryeo Dynasty, Chinese envoy Xu Jing marveled in his 1123 Gaoli Tujing that Korean mother-of-pearl inlay was 'so minute and exquisite that it must be reckoned the finest in the world.' Yet how did the southern fishing port of Tongyeong become the undisputed capital of Korean Najeonchilgi and traditional craft for the last four hundred years? The answer lies directly in Admiral Yi Sun-sin and the aftermath of the 1592–1598 Imjin War!

Even the city’s name, Tong-yeong, is an abbreviation of Samdo Sugun Tongjeyeong (—'Supreme Naval Command Headquarters of the Three Provinces' of Jeolla, Gyeongsang, and Chungcheong), a post created for Admiral Yi Sun-sin in 1593. After the war ended, in 1604, the sixth Supreme Naval Commander Yi Gyeong-jun established the permanent naval capital at the sheltered harbor of Dugyupo (modern Tongyeong), centering it around the monumental single-story wooden hall of Sebyeonggwan (National Treasure No. 305—meaning 'Hall of Washing Weapons in the Milky Way,' one of the largest traditional wooden halls in Korea alongside Gyeongbokgung’s Gyeonghoeru!). To make the naval headquarters financially self-sustaining without draining the war-torn royal treasury in Seoul, the Tongjeyeong commanders established an extraordinary industrial-military complex right inside the fortress compound called the Sibigongbang (—The Twelve Naval Artisan Workshops).

Under military discipline, the Sibigongbang conscripted and salaried the most gifted master artisans across the southern provinces to manufacture both naval weapons (cannons, matchlocks, iron armor, war drums, signal flags) and high-value luxury tribute goods (jinsangpum—sent to the Royal Court in Seoul and sold to merchants to pay for warships and sailors’ rations!): brassware, horsehair hats (gat), bamboo blinds, saddles, fans, cabinets (soban), and above all Najeonchilgi (Mother-of-Pearl & Lacquer Workshops)! Why did Tongyeong produce the most luminous mother-of-pearl in East Asia? Because the cold, nutrient-rich tidal currents sweeping between Tongyeong’s 570 islands nourish wild abalone (Haliotis discus) and turban shells (and). To protect their soft bodies against strong rocky currents and seasonal temperature swings, Tongyeong abalones secrete a uniquely dense crystalline matrix of aragonite calcium carbonate platelets (CaCO3, each platelet just 0.5 micrometers thick—the exact wavelength of visible light!) interleaved with elastic conchiolin biopolymer proteins.

When white light hits those 0.5-micrometer aragonite crystal steps, thin-film constructive interference splits the light into blazing structural colors—emerald green, sapphire blue, and rose pink—that never fade for a thousand years because they contain zero chemical dye! Equally miraculous is the black adhesive and armor that holds those shell fragments: Ottchil (natural lacquer harvested by slashing the bark of the Korean lacquer tree, Toxicodendron vernicifluum). Raw ottchil sap contains urushiol—the exact same violent allergen found in poison ivy! Only after years of exposure do Chiljang (lacquer masters) build immunity. From a polymer-chemistry perspective, ottchil is a natural miracle that synthetic plastics still struggle to match. Unlike paint or varnish—which dries by evaporating a solvent—raw ottchil cures inside a warm, humid wooden cabinet (chiljang, kept at 20°C–25°C and 75%–80% relative humidity!) because the endogenous copper-containing enzyme laccase absorbs moisture and oxygen from the air to cross-link the urushiol molecules into a three-dimensional thermoset polymer network that is completely impervious to boiling water, acids, alkalis, termites, and rot for millennia!

Preparing the wooden core (baekgol, made from seasoned red pine or paulownia) and applying the mother-of-pearl requires more than forty-five painstaking stages over six to twelve months. First, the wood joints are reinforced with linen or hemp cloth (bebareugi), coated in mixtures of raw lacquer and fired clay powder, and wet-sanded flat with charcoal blocks. meanwhile, the Najeon master grinds curved abalone shells flat on whetstones, boils them, and splits them down to 0.1-millimeter-thin ribbons (sangsa), using two signature Korean techniques that distinguish Korean Najeonchilgi from all others: That Tongyeong Najeonchilgi (mother-of-pearl inlaid lacquerware) survived those dark decades is a testament to quiet human courage and custodial devotion.

1. Jureumjil (Fret-Sawing): Stacking six to eight paper-thin sheets of abalone shell glued together and sawing out intricate curvilinear motifs—chrysanthemums, phoenixes, deer, pine needles, and clouds—with a hair-thin steel fret saw. 2. Kkakgijil / Kkeungijil (Snapping Mosaic): Taking a long, 0.5-millimeter-wide strip of abalone shell (cut) and using the tip of a small iron knife and the thumb to snap and score tiny sub-millimeter rectangular tiles at lightning speed directly onto the glue-coated wooden surface—Up to 50,000 individual shell snaps to cover a single jewelry box with a shimmering geometric tortoise-shell or lattice mosaic that curves smoothly around three-dimensional corners!

In the twentieth century, Tongyeong gave birth to the towering modern masters of Korean lacquer art: Kim Bong-ryong (1902–1994, Living National Treasure who won international medals in Paris and trained a generation in Tongyeong and Wonju) and his fellow Tongyeong master Song Ju-an (1901–1981), followed by Song’s son Song Bang-ung (1940–2020)—who spent decades restoring Goryeo Buddhist sutra boxes and creating contemporary masterpieces now held in the British Museum, the Victoria and Albert Museum, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Stand inside the restored Sibigongbang (Twelve Workshops) courtyard beside the towering columns of Sebyeonggwan in Tongyeong today as an artisan’s knife clicks rhythmically—ttak, ttak, ttak—snapping abalone starlight onto a black lacquer tray. Four hundred years after Admiral Yi’s sailors guarded these straits, Tongyeong still turns the poison of the mountain tree and the shell of the deep sea into inextinguishable light.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Optical Physics & Polymer Chemistry: Aragonite Interference and Laccase Curing

Materials scientists analyzing Goryeo and Tongyeong Najeonchilgi point to the marriage of two natural nanomaterials. First, the iridescent abalone nacre consists of hexagonal aragonite (CaCO3) tablets (~500 nm thick, matching the wavelength of visible light) stacked like microscopic bricks; constructive optical interference reflects pure structural wavelengths without organic pigments that photodegrade. Second, Toxicodendron vernicifluum sap (ottchil) polymerizes enzymatically via laccase in 80% humidity, forming a cross-linked catechol film resistant to aqua regia and microbial decay.

2. Military-Artisanal History: The 1604 'Sibigongbang' (Twelve Workshops) of Tongjeyeong

Why did a naval base become Korea's premier fine-craft capital? When the Samdo Sugun Tongjeyeong (Supreme Naval Headquarters of the Three Provinces) was permanently established at Tongyeong in 1604 around Sebyeonggwan Hall (National Treasure No. 305), the naval command organized the Sibigongbang (12)—twelve specialized manufacturing guilds inside the fortress walls. By pooling the south coast's top shell-cutters, lacquerers, brass-smiths, and cabinetmakers to produce royal tribute and commercial goods, the navy funded its warships while elevating Tongyeong craft to national perfection.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Keep three craft terms in mind: first, Najeonchilgi, traditional mother-of-pearl inlaid lacquerware; second, Jureumjil and Kkeungijil, the twin techniques of fret-sawing curvilinear motifs and snapping 0.5-millimeter shell strips into geometric mosaics; and third, Ottchil, natural lacquer sap cured in humid wooden chambers.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Travel to the restored Samdo Sugun Tongjeyeong complex in Culture-dong, Tongyeong, where you can stand under the massive timber ceiling of the 1605 Sebyeonggwan Hall and walk directly through the reconstructed Twelve Workshops (Sibigongbang)—watching artisans saw abalone shells and brush ottchil lacquer. Afterward, visit the Tongyeong City Museum and the Tongyeong Ottchil Art Museum overlooking Hallyeosudo Bay.
