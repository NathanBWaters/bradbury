Alcatraz Island: From 1859 Civil War Brick Citadel to the 1969–1971 Indians of All Tribes Occupation

San Francisco — How a barren twenty-two-acre sandstone rock in the Golden Gate tidal stream served as coastal fortress, military stockade, federal penitentiary, and birthplace of modern Red Power (Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971)) • 9 min read

“We, the native Americans, re-claim the land known as Alcatraz Island in the name of all American Indians by right of discovery.” — Indians of All Tribes, The Alcatraz Proclamation (November 20, 1969)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rising 135 feet out of freezing 53°F tidal currents 1.25 miles north of San Francisco's Fisherman's Wharf, the twenty-two-acre sandstone rock of Alcatraz ('Isla de los Alcatraces,' named by Spanish lieutenant Juan Manuel de Ayala in August 1775) has condensed four distinct eras of American history onto a single windswept outcrop. Fortified in the 1850s with the West Coast's first lighthouse and 111 massive Rodman smoothbore cannon to hold San Francisco for the Union during the Civil War, it became a U.S. Army prison holding Hopi resisters and World War I conscientious objectors, then the maximum-security Federal Penitentiary (1934–1963), and finally—for nineteen transformative months beginning November 20, 1969—the liberated heart of the Indians of All Tribes occupation led by Richard Oakes and LaNada War Jack.

Before any brick wall or steel cell bar touched its ridge, the barren twenty-two-acre block of fractured Franciscan greywacke sandstone was known to Ohlone peoples as a seasonal gathering site for seabird eggs and as a place of isolation, and on August 5, 1775, Spanish naval officer Juan Manuel de Ayala charted it inside San Francisco Bay as 'La Isla de los Alcatraces' after the thousands of brown pelicans (Pelecanus occidentalis) roosting on its guano-whitened cliffs. Following the American seizure of California and the 1850 presidential order by Millard Fillmore reserving the rock for military defense, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers officers led by Lieutenant Zealous B. Tower blasted sheer vertical scarps out of the island's perimeter slopes, activated the West Coast's first operational lighthouse on June 1, 1854, and erected a three-story brick Citadel atop the crest.

When the American Civil War broke out in April 1861 and Southern sympathizers in California plotted to outfit Confederate privateers to seize Sierra gold shipments, Fort Alcatraz stood as the Gibraltar of the Pacific: armed with 111 coastal artillery pieces—including fifteen-inch Rodman smoothbore columbiads capable of hurling 450-pound iron spheres across the shipping channel—without ever firing a shot in anger. Because the icy, five-knot ebb tides rushing out the Golden Gate made swimming to the mainland nearly suicidal, the Army began locking military prisoners inside the brick basement casemates in 1859, expanding the facility during the late nineteenth century to hold Confederate sympathizers, Modoc War captives, and nineteen Hopi men from Oraibi, Arizona, imprisoned in 1895 strictly for refusing to surrender their children to forced government boarding schools.

Between 1909 and 1912, military convicts tore down the upper stories of the 1859 brick Citadel (leaving its subterranean moat and basement foundations intact beneath the floor) to erect the world's largest reinforced-concrete building of its day: the 600-cell Pacific Branch, U.S. Disciplinary Barracks, where World War I conscientious objectors—including Hutterites Jacob and Michael Hofer—suffered solitary confinement in the dark lower dungeons. In October 1933, amidst a national wave of Depression-era bank robberies and kidnappings, the U.S. Department of Justice acquired the island from the War Department, hired Prison Warden James A. Johnston, retrofitted 'B' and 'C' cellblocks with tool-proof chrome-nickel steel bars and electromagnetic gun detectors, and opened the United States Penitentiary, Alcatraz Island, on August 11, 1934.

Unlike ordinary prisons aimed at rehabilitation, federal Alcatraz held an average of only 260 inmates—never more than one percent of the federal prison population—drawn from convicts deemed incorrigible elsewhere, including Al Capone (AZ-85), George 'Machine Gun' Kelly (AZ-117), Robert Stroud ('The Birdman of Alcatraz,' AZ-594), and Harlem poet and number-runner Ellsworth 'Bumpy' Johnson (AZ-1117). The true psychological torment of 'Broadway'—the central corridor between B and C blocks—was not physical brutality but proximity to freedom: on New Year's Eve, when the wind blew from the south across the water from the Saint Francis Yacht Club and Marina Boulevard, prisoners lying in five-by-nine-foot cells could clearly hear women laughing, clinking champagne glasses, and big-band music drifting across the bay.

Over twenty-nine years of federal operation, thirty-six inmates staged fourteen separate escape attempts, culminating in the violent May 2–4, 1946 'Battle of Alcatraz' (when Bernard Coy scaled the West Gun Gallery bars) and the famous night of June 11, 1962, when Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin chipped through salt-damaged utility vent concrete with sharpened spoons, left plaster dummy heads with real barbershop hair in their beds, and vanished into the bay on a raincoat-fabric raft. Just nine months after the Morris-Anglin escape, Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy ordered the penitentiary closed on March 21, 1963: salt spray had severely corroded the structural rebar of the cellhouse, every drop of fresh water had to be barged to the island's 250,000-gallon steel water tower at nearly a million gallons a week, and per-inmate operating costs were triple those of any mainland federal prison.

For six years the abandoned island sat vacant under a skeleton caretaker crew until March 9, 1964, when five Lakota Sioux men led by Richard McKenzie and Belva Cottier landed briefly to claim the surplus federal land under the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie—planting the legal and symbolic seed for what exploded five years later after the San Francisco American Indian Center at 16th and Valencia streets burned down in October 1969. In the pre-dawn darkness of November 20, 1969, eighty-nine Native American students and urban families—calling themselves Indians of All Tribes and led by twenty-seven-year-old Mohawk San Francisco State student Richard Oakes and Shoshone-Bannock Berkeley student LaNada Means (War Jack)—evaded a Coast Guard blockade aboard the charter boat Monte Cristo in Sausalito and leaped onto the Alcatraz dock.

Writing with red paint across the concrete dockside buildings and the high steel water tower—'PEACE AND FREEDOM. WELCOME. HOME OF THE FREE INDIAN LAND' and 'YOU ARE NOW ON INDIAN LAND'—Oakes delivered the satirical yet deadly serious Alcatraz Proclamation, offering to buy the rock from the United States for $24 in glass beads and red cloth, noting that the island lacked running water, fertile soil, or mineral wealth and therefore 'already resembled most Indian reservations.' Over nineteen months (November 20, 1969 to June 11, 1971), an elected Indigenous council operated the Big Rock School in the former officers' quarters, ran a health clinic and communal kitchen, and broadcast scholar-activist John Trudell's weekly program Radio Free Alcatraz from the cellhouse over KPFA Berkeley to hundreds of thousands of listeners nationwide.

Even after tragedy struck on January 3, 1970—when Oakes's twelve-year-old stepdaughter Yvonne Oakes fell to her death down a three-story concrete stairwell in the darkened quarters—and after the federal government cut off electric power and water barges before marshals removed the final fifteen occupants on June 11, 1971, the occupation fundamentally changed U.S. federal policy. Directly pressured by the global spotlight on Alcatraz, President Richard Nixon formally repudiated the federal policy of tribal 'Termination' in July 1970, returned sacred Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo, and inaugurated the era of Indigenous Self-Determination—a legacy honored every Thanksgiving morning and Indigenous Peoples' Day when thousands gather on Alcatraz for the Sunrise Ceremony before passing into the Golden Gate National Recreation Area.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Four Eras on Twenty-Two Acres: Why Geography Shaped Alcatraz

Alcatraz's entire history stems from its geological isolation right inside the throat of the Golden Gate. Because every ship entering San Francisco Bay had to pass within range of its cliffs, the U.S. Army fortified it in the 1850s as the anchor of a 'Triangle of Fire' alongside Fort Point and Fort Mason. That identical ring of frigid, four-to-six-knot tidal currents later turned the rock into a natural moat for military and federal prisoners—and in 1969 gave Indigenous activists an unmistakable stage visible from every hillside in the Bay Area.

2. Concrete Corrosion, Salt Air, and the 1962 Escape Mechanics

Why could Frank Morris and the Anglin brothers chip through the back walls of their B-Block cells using silver-brazed dime-and-spoon blades in 1962? When the Army built the concrete cellhouse in 1909–1912 without a waterproof vapor barrier and the Bureau of Prisons later piped uninsulated saltwater flushing lines through the utility corridor, constant dampness and salt crystallization leached calcium hydroxide from the concrete around the six-by-nine-inch ventilation grilles, turning the concrete crumbly enough to excavate behind painted cardboard.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three landmarks on the island: first, the 1859 Brick Citadel basement beneath the Main Cellhouse, where Hopi fathers from Oraibi were held in 1895 for resisting forced assimilation; second, the 1940 Water Tower, still bearing the red-painted 1969 inscription 'Peace and Freedom. Welcome. Home of the Free Indian Land' carefully conserved by the National Park Service; and third, Agave Trail and the historic terraced gardens planted by military families and inmate gardeners like Elliott Michener that survived forty years without human watering.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Board the ferry at Alcatraz Landing, Pier 33, along the Embarcadero to cross the 1.25-mile channel to the Alcatraz Dock. Pause immediately above the landing dock to read the 1969 'Indians Welcome' red lettering on the Quartermaster warehouse and the Water Tower, climb the switchback road through the 1850s brick Sally Port into the Main Cellhouse to hear the legendary audio tour voiced by former correctional officers and inmates, and step onto the West Road to view the Golden Gate Bridge framed by wild nasturtiums and nesting western gulls.
