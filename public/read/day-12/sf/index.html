Alcatraz Island: From 1859 Civil War Brick Citadel to the 1969–1971 Indians of All Tribes Occupation

San Francisco — How a barren twenty-two-acre sandstone rock in the Golden Gate tidal stream served as coastal fortress, military stockade, federal penitentiary, and birthplace of modern Red Power (Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971)) • 20 min read

“We, the native Americans, re-claim the land known as Alcatraz Island in the name of all American Indians by right of discovery.” — Indians of All Tribes, The Alcatraz Proclamation (November 20, 1969)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Rising 135 feet out of freezing 53°F tidal currents 1.25 miles north of San Francisco's Fisherman's Wharf, the twenty-two-acre sandstone rock of Alcatraz ('Isla de los Alcatraces,' named by Spanish lieutenant Juan Manuel de Ayala in August 1775) has condensed four distinct eras of American history onto a single windswept outcrop. Fortified in the 1850s with the West Coast's first lighthouse and 111 massive Rodman smoothbore cannon to hold San Francisco for the Union during the Civil War, it became a U.S. Army prison holding Hopi resisters and World War I conscientious objectors, then the maximum-security Federal Penitentiary (1934–1963), and finally—for nineteen transformative months beginning November 20, 1969—the liberated heart of the Indians of All Tribes occupation led by Richard Oakes and LaNada War Jack.

Standing today at the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island in the heart of Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33) (37.8270° N, 122.4230° W), a visitor who listens closely beneath the hum of Muni buses and the salt-laden Pacific breeze can still trace the physical contours of Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971). Before any brick wall or steel cell bar touched its ridge, the barren twenty-two-acre block of fractured Franciscan greywacke sandstone was known to Ohlone peoples as a seasonal gathering site for seabird eggs and as a place of isolation, and on August 5, 1775, Spanish naval officer Juan Manuel de Ayala charted it inside San Francisco Bay as 'La Isla de los Alcatraces' after the thousands of brown pelicans (*Pelecanus occidentalis*) roosting on its guano-whitened cliffs. Every curbstone, cast-iron utility cover, and weathered timber along this corridor preserves the lived memory of how Mohawk student leader Richard Oakes, Shoshone-Bannock organizer LaNada War Jack, and military engineer Lieutenant William H. C. Whiting and a generation of San Franciscans confronted transforming an isolated sandstone fortress and carceral rock into a beacon of Indigenous self-determination, transforming an improbably steep and seismically restless peninsula into an enduring laboratory of civic invention.

To understand why Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') emerged precisely where it did, one must first examine the underlying topography and nineteenth-century historical forces that shaped Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33). Following the American seizure of California and the 1850 presidential order by Millard Fillmore reserving the rock for military defense, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers officers led by Lieutenant Zealous B. Tower blasted sheer vertical scarps out of the island's perimeter slopes, activated the West Coast's first operational lighthouse on June 1, 1854, and erected a three-story brick Citadel atop the crest. In a port city bounded on three sides by deep tidal waters and dissected by steep Franciscan bedrock ridges, physical geography was never a passive backdrop; it dictated property lines, wind velocity, water pressure, and the daily survival strategies of everyone who worked along the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island.

The documentary record preserved in the San Francisco History Center and contemporary municipal archives reveals how rapidly the stakes escalated during August 1775, 1859–1963, and the 19-month occupation of November 20, 1969–June 11, 1971. When the American Civil War broke out in April 1861 and Southern sympathizers in California plotted to outfit Confederate privateers to seize Sierra gold shipments, Fort Alcatraz stood as the Gibraltar of the Pacific: armed with 111 coastal artillery pieces—including fifteen-inch Rodman smoothbore columbiads capable of hurling 450-pound iron spheres across the shipping channel—without ever firing a shot in anger. Contemporary observers who walked past the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island noted that what appeared at first glance to be a localized technical or civic experiment was in fact a decisive test of whether San Francisco could reconcile frontier improvisation with permanent urban infrastructure.

At the center of this unfolding drama stood Mohawk student leader Richard Oakes, Shoshone-Bannock organizer LaNada War Jack, and military engineer Lieutenant William H. C. Whiting, whose background, temperament, and practical mastery of masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics proved indispensable when conventional wisdom faltered. Because the icy, five-knot ebb tides rushing out the Golden Gate made swimming to the mainland nearly suicidal, the Army began locking military prisoners inside the brick basement casemates in 1859, expanding the facility during the late nineteenth century to hold Confederate sympathizers, Modoc War captives, and nineteen Hopi men from Oraibi, Arizona, imprisoned in 1895 strictly for refusing to surrender their children to forced government boarding schools. Colleagues and skeptics alike remarked on the relentless attention to physical detail—measuring tolerances, inspecting raw materials on the wind-scoured docks, and arguing late into the night over blueprints and ledger books while foghorns groaned across the Golden Gate.

The material and engineering realities of masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics imposed unforgiving constraints on every phase of the work at Alcatraz Island ('The Rock'). Between 1909 and 1912, military convicts tore down the upper stories of the 1859 brick Citadel (leaving its subterranean moat and basement foundations intact beneath the floor) to erect the world's largest reinforced-concrete building of its day: the 600-cell Pacific Branch, U.S. Disciplinary Barracks, where World War I conscientious objectors—including Hutterites Jacob and Michael Hofer—suffered solitary confinement in the dark lower dungeons. Whether dealing with the corrosive salt mist rolling off the Pacific, the shifting equilibrium of bay mud and Franciscan chert, or the logistical bottlenecks of hauling heavy equipment across the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island, each obstacle demanded a bespoke solution forged on site rather than copied from an Eastern textbook.

As the work progressed through August 1775, 1859–1963, and the 19-month occupation of November 20, 1969–June 11, 1971, the surrounding streets of Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33) became an open-air theater where working-class laborers, immigrant craftsmen, journalists, and curious citizens gathered daily to witness the transformation. In October 1933, amidst a national wave of Depression-era bank robberies and kidnappings, the U.S. Department of Justice acquired the island from the War Department, hired Prison Warden James A. Johnston, retrofitted 'B' and 'C' cellblocks with tool-proof chrome-nickel steel bars and electromagnetic gun detectors, and opened the United States Penitentiary, Alcatraz Island, on August 11, 1934. Newspaper dispatches of the era captured the sensory intensity of the scene: the sharp tang of creosote and wet mortar, the rhythmic clatter of tools echoing off neighboring facades, and the unmistakable pride of a community watching a new civic monument take tangible form.

Yet no major transformation in San Francisco history ever unfolded without fierce political, financial, or ideological friction. Unlike ordinary prisons aimed at rehabilitation, federal Alcatraz held an average of only 260 inmates—never more than one percent of the federal prison population—drawn from convicts deemed incorrigible elsewhere, including Al Capone (AZ-85), George 'Machine Gun' Kelly (AZ-117), Robert Stroud ('The Birdman of Alcatraz,' AZ-594), and Harlem poet and number-runner Ellsworth 'Bumpy' Johnson (AZ-1117). Behind closed doors in City Hall and inside the merchant exchanges downtown, entrenched interests questioned the cost, challenged the legal jurisdiction, or attempted to divert the momentum of Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') toward private speculation—forcing Mohawk student leader Richard Oakes, Shoshone-Bannock organizer LaNada War Jack, and military engineer Lieutenant William H. C. Whiting and allies to defend their vision in the court of public opinion.

A pivotal turning point arrived when theoretical plans collided with a moment of acute crisis along the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island. The true psychological torment of 'Broadway'—the central corridor between B and C blocks—was not physical brutality but proximity to freedom: on New Year's Eve, when the wind blew from the south across the water from the Saint Francis Yacht Club and Marina Boulevard, prisoners lying in five-by-nine-foot cells could clearly hear women laughing, clinking champagne glasses, and big-band music drifting across the bay. In that crucible, the margin between catastrophic failure and lasting triumph measured only inches or hours, testing the structural integrity of masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics and the collective resolve of every worker who refused to abandon the post.

Inside the workshops, drafting rooms, and field stations at Alcatraz Island ('The Rock'), the day-to-day craft of masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics produced innovations that soon radiated far beyond Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33). Over twenty-nine years of federal operation, thirty-six inmates staged fourteen separate escape attempts, culminating in the violent May 2–4, 1946 'Battle of Alcatraz' (when Bernard Coy scaled the West Gun Gallery bars) and the famous night of June 11, 1962, when Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin chipped through salt-damaged utility vent concrete with sharpened spoons, left plaster dummy heads with real barbershop hair in their beds, and vanished into the bay on a raincoat-fabric raft. Precise empirical measurements, hand-built instruments, and hard-won field protocols turned what had begun as a local San Francisco problem into a benchmark studied by engineers, naturalists, and urban reformers around the globe.

The human texture of Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') was equally defined by the ordinary men and women whose names rarely appeared on bronze dedication plaques but whose labor animated every square foot of the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island. Just nine months after the Morris-Anglin escape, Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy ordered the penitentiary closed on March 21, 1963: salt spray had severely corroded the structural rebar of the cellhouse, every drop of fresh water had to be barged to the island's 250,000-gallon steel water tower at nearly a million gallons a week, and per-inmate operating costs were triple those of any mainland federal prison. Their oral histories, family photographs, union cards, and handwritten letters remind us that San Francisco's grandeur was built step by step by hands calloused by wind, cold water, and tireless craftsmanship.

When the defining moment of completion or confrontation arrived in August 1775, 1859–1963, and the 19-month occupation of November 20, 1969–June 11, 1971, the entire city turned its eyes toward Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') in Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33). For six years the abandoned island sat vacant under a skeleton caretaker crew until March 9, 1964, when five Lakota Sioux men led by Richard McKenzie and Belva Cottier landed briefly to claim the surplus federal land under the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie—planting the legal and symbolic seed for what exploded five years later after the San Francisco American Indian Center at 16th and Valencia streets burned down in October 1969. What had once been dismissed by doubters as an impractical dream now stood before the public as an undeniable physical reality, altering how residents moved through the city, how they understood their natural environment, and how they imagined their shared civic future.

In the decades that followed August 1775, 1859–1963, and the 19-month occupation of November 20, 1969–June 11, 1971, shifting economic tides, generational turnover, and new technological regimes repeatedly tested the survival of Alcatraz Island ('The Rock'). In the pre-dawn darkness of November 20, 1969, eighty-nine Native American students and urban families—calling themselves Indians of All Tribes and led by twenty-seven-year-old Mohawk San Francisco State student Richard Oakes and Shoshone-Bannock Berkeley student LaNada Means (War Jack)—evaded a Coast Guard blockade aboard the charter boat *Monte Cristo* in Sausalito and leaped onto the Alcatraz dock. While other nineteenth- and twentieth-century structures across the peninsula fell to the wrecking ball or vanished beneath modern redevelopment, the enduring presence at the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island adapted to new uses without surrendering its essential historical character.

Seismic shocks, coastal storms, and rapid urban densification provided further proof of the resilience embedded in masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics. Writing with red paint across the concrete dockside buildings and the high steel water tower—'PEACE AND FREEDOM. WELCOME. HOME OF THE FREE INDIAN LAND' and 'YOU ARE NOW ON INDIAN LAND'—Oakes delivered the satirical yet deadly serious Alcatraz Proclamation, offering to buy the rock from the United States for $24 in glass beads and red cloth, noting that the island lacked running water, fertile soil, or mineral wealth and therefore 'already resembled most Indian reservations.' Each time the ground trembled or political priorities shifted, inspectors, historians, and neighborhood advocates returned to the original work of Mohawk student leader Richard Oakes, Shoshone-Bannock organizer LaNada War Jack, and military engineer Lieutenant William H. C. Whiting, discovering anew how deeply its design was tuned to the unique rhythms of the San Francisco peninsula.

By the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, a new generation of preservationists, scientists, and community organizers recognized that Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') was more than a relic of Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971); it was a living ecological and cultural keystone. Over nineteen months (November 20, 1969 to June 11, 1971), an elected Indigenous council operated the Big Rock School in the former officers' quarters, ran a health clinic and communal kitchen, and broadcast scholar-activist John Trudell's weekly program *Radio Free Alcatraz* from the cellhouse over KPFA Berkeley to hundreds of thousands of listeners nationwide. Through painstaking archival recovery, structural stabilization, and public stewardship, the site at the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island was safeguarded so that future generations could experience its lessons firsthand.

Walking the perimeter of Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') today at 37.8270° N, 122.4230° W, one can observe the exact interplay of architecture, geology, and memory that makes Central San Francisco Bay (1.25 miles north of Pier 33) inexhaustible to the attentive observer. Even after tragedy struck on January 3, 1970—when Oakes's twelve-year-old stepdaughter Yvonne Oakes fell to her death down a three-story concrete stairwell in the darkened quarters—and after the federal government cut off electric power and water barges before marshals removed the final fifteen occupants on June 11, 1971, the occupation fundamentally changed U.S. federal policy. From the weathered textures of masonry coastal fortification, maximum-security cellhouse acoustics, and maritime blockade logistics to the sightlines stretching toward the bay or the surrounding hills, the physical landscape reads like an open manuscript in which every chapter of Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971) remains legible.

Ultimately, the story of Alcatraz Island: From 1859 Civil War Brick Citadel to the 1969–1971 Indians of All Tribes Occupation endures because it captures the quintessential San Francisco paradox: a city perpetually perched on the edge of natural and social upheaval that responds by building works of extraordinary beauty, audacity, and solidarity. Directly pressured by the global spotlight on Alcatraz, President Richard Nixon formally repudiated the federal policy of tribal 'Termination' in July 1970, returned sacred Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo, and inaugurated the era of Indigenous Self-Determination—a legacy honored every Thanksgiving morning and Indigenous Peoples' Day when thousands gather on Alcatraz for the Sunrise Ceremony before passing into the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. Long after the last footsteps fade along the Sally Port, Main Cellhouse ('Broadway'), and Water Tower of Alcatraz Island at dusk, the legacy of Mohawk student leader Richard Oakes, Shoshone-Bannock organizer LaNada War Jack, and military engineer Lieutenant William H. C. Whiting and Alcatraz Island ('The Rock') continues to shape the moral and material bedrock of the city by the Golden Gate.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Four Eras on Twenty-Two Acres: Why Geography Shaped Alcatraz

Alcatraz's entire history stems from its geological isolation right inside the throat of the Golden Gate. Because every ship entering San Francisco Bay had to pass within range of its cliffs, the U.S. Army fortified it in the 1850s as the anchor of a 'Triangle of Fire' alongside Fort Point and Fort Mason. That identical ring of frigid, four-to-six-knot tidal currents later turned the rock into a natural moat for military and federal prisoners—and in 1969 gave Indigenous activists an unmistakable stage visible from every hillside in the Bay Area. Close examination of Alcatraz Island in Alcatraz Island, 1.25 Miles Offshore in San Francisco Bay (37.8270° N, 122.4230° W) highlights how primary historical records, physical craftsmanship, and enduring local geography reinforce every detail of this Spanish Survey (1775) to Indigenous Occupation (1969–1971) account.

2. Concrete Corrosion, Salt Air, and the 1962 Escape Mechanics

Why could Frank Morris and the Anglin brothers chip through the back walls of their B-Block cells using silver-brazed dime-and-spoon blades in 1962? When the Army built the concrete cellhouse in 1909–1912 without a waterproof vapor barrier and the Bureau of Prisons later piped uninsulated saltwater flushing lines through the utility corridor, constant dampness and salt crystallization leached calcium hydroxide from the concrete around the six-by-nine-inch ventilation grilles, turning the concrete crumbly enough to excavate behind painted cardboard.

3. Key San Francisco Concepts & Landmarks to Remember

Hold onto three landmarks on the island: first, the 1859 Brick Citadel basement beneath the Main Cellhouse, where Hopi fathers from Oraibi were held in 1895 for resisting forced assimilation; second, the 1940 Water Tower, still bearing the red-painted 1969 inscription 'Peace and Freedom. Welcome. Home of the Free Indian Land' carefully conserved by the National Park Service; and third, Agave Trail and the historic terraced gardens planted by military families and inmate gardeners like Elliott Michener that survived forty years without human watering.

4. Where to Walk This Story in San Francisco Today

Board the ferry at Alcatraz Landing, Pier 33, along the Embarcadero to cross the 1.25-mile channel to the Alcatraz Dock (37.8270° N, 122.4230° W). Pause immediately above the landing dock to read the 1969 'Indians Welcome' red lettering on the Quartermaster warehouse and the Water Tower, climb the switchback road through the 1850s brick Sally Port into the Main Cellhouse to hear the legendary audio tour voiced by former correctional officers and inmates, and step onto the West Road to view the Golden Gate Bridge framed by wild nasturtiums and nesting western gulls.
