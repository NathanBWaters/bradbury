The Kingfisher Jade of Gangjin: Goryeo Celadon Kilns and the Invention of Sanggam Inlay

Korea — How twelfth-century potters along the southwestern coast reduced iron oxide in pine-fueled climbing kilns to trap the color of autumn sky after rain (Goryeo Dynasty (10th–13th Century)) • 11 min read

“The earthenwares of Goryeo they call bisaek (kingfisher color); in recent years their craftsmanship has grown so exquisite and their luster so pure that they surpass all other kilns under heaven.” — Xu Jing, Illustrated Account of the Embassy to Goryeo (Gaoli Tujing, 1123)

Key Context & Why It Matters

Along the sheltered tidal coves of Gangjin Bay at the southwestern tip of the Korean peninsula, more than 188 climbing dragon kilns (deunggama) smoked continuously from the tenth through the thirteenth centuries, producing the legendary Goryeo Cheongja (celadon) for the royal court in Gaeseong. Even Chinese Song Dynasty connoisseurs—who possessed the famed Ru and Yue imperial kilns—declared Goryeo's translucent jade-green glaze (bisaek, 'kingfisher color') one of the 'Ten Firsts Under Heaven.' Achieving that luminous blue-green hue without green pigment required starving the kiln of oxygen at 1,250°C so ferric oxide reduced to ferrous oxide, followed in the mid-twelfth century by a purely Korean breakthrough: sanggam (inlaid celadon), where potters etched cranes and clouds into leather-hard clay and filled them with white and black slip.

Walk across the terraced hillsides of Sadang-ri and Yongun-ri in Daegu-myeon, Gangjin County, where green mountain ridges tumble down to the calm waters of Gangjin Bay, and the soil beneath your boots crunches with a pale, glassy music. Dig your fingers two inches into the grass beside the stream, and you pull up shards of twelfth-century stoneware coated in a glaze the color of a kingfisher’s wing or the Korean sky just after an autumn shower. Over 188 ancient kiln mounds (yoji) dot this single coastal valley—making Gangjin the silicon valley of medieval East Asian ceramics, producing more than half of all national-treasure Goryeo Cheongja surviving today.

Why did the royal court of the Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392), seated four hundred kilometers north in the capital of Gaegyeong (modern Kaesong), concentrate its premier state ceramic workshops (so) in this remote southwestern corner of Jeolla Province? Gangjin possessed a rare convergence of four geological and maritime factors: deposits of fine sedimental clay enriched with the exact 1.5-to-3-percent iron and titanium ratios needed for celadon; hillside forests of dense Korean red pine (sonamu) that burned with a long, resinous, ash-free flame; abundant quartz sand and wood ash for fluxing glazes; and a sheltered deep-water bay from which tribute boats (joseon) could sail up the western coast directly to the capital.

By the early twelfth century, during the reign of King Yejong and King Injong, Gangjin potters perfected a glaze hue they called bisaek —literally 'kingfisher-feather color.' When the Song Dynasty scholar-envoy Xu Jing visited Goryeo in 1123 and published his Xuanhe Fengshi Gaoli Tujing ('Illustrated Account of the Chinese Embassy to Goryeo'), he marveled at celadon wine ewers shaped like bamboo shoots, lotus-pedestal incense burners crowned with ducks or lions, and bowls so luminous they resembled carved nephrite jade. The magic of bisaek lies in high-temperature reduction chemistry rather than added dye. No green or blue pigment ever touched a Goryeo celadon pot. Instead, the raw clay body and the thin liquid glaze—compounded from feldspathic rock powder mixed with leached oak, pine, and camellia wood ash—contained trace amounts of iron oxide (1 to 3 percent) and titanium dioxide. If fired in an oxygen-rich atmosphere (oxidation firing), iron turns ferric (Fe2O3), producing a dull yellowish-brown or brick-red glaze.

To transform that rust-colored iron into translucent kingfisher jade, Gangjin master kiln-stokers (daejang) constructed long, sloped tunnel kilns called deunggama (climbing kilns) dug at a fifteen-to-twenty-degree angle up the hillside and built from refractory clay arches. After biscuit-firing the fragile vessels at 750°C, dipping them in ash glaze, and placing them inside protective clay saggers (gapbal) atop tiny white quartzite spurs so the melting glaze would not fuse to the kiln floor, the stokers bricked up the entrance and lit the lower firebox. When the internal temperature of the climbing kiln reached 1,150°C to 1,250°C—judged entirely by the incandescent white-orange color of the flame viewed through spyholes—the kiln master ordered the side air vents sealed with wet mud while tossing bundles of green pine boughs into the chamber. Starved of outside oxygen, the carbon monoxide in the thick smoke aggressively stripped oxygen atoms directly out of the molten iron oxide in the glaze, reducing ferric iron (ferric ions) to ferrous iron (ferrous ions) and creating microscopic wollastonite crystals and tiny trapped gas bubbles that scattered light like mountain mist.

Even slight errors in timing or air leakage ruined an entire firing of five hundred vessels. Whenever Gangjin potters opened a cooled kiln and found a warping flaw, a smoke stain, or a glaze shade that fell short of royal standards, they smashed the defective bowls on the spot with iron hammers to protect the reputation of the kiln—leaving behind six-meter-deep mounds of broken bisaek sherds that archaeologists excavate today. Around the middle of the twelfth century, Korean potters took a bold creative leap away from Chinese ceramic models. Whereas Song Dynasty potters favored monochrome glazes or carved relief, Goryeo artisans invented sanggam cheongja —inlaid celadon. Borrowed from the metalwork technique of hammering silver wire into bronze (ipsa) and mother-of-pearl into lacquer (najeon), sanggam turned the clay surface into a graphic canvas.

Working while the wheel-thrown vessel was still leather-hard (ban-geonjo), the potter used a sharp bamboo or iron stylus to incise delicate recessed lines—cranes soaring upward with outstretched necks, weeping willow branches, chrysanthemum medallions, or boys playing among lotus reeds. Next, the artisan brushed thick liquid white kaolin clay (baekto) or iron-rich black clay (heukto) into the carved grooves, scraped the surface flush with a curved blade so only the inlaid lines remained, biscuit-fired the vessel, and coated it in translucent bisaek glaze. During the final 1,250°C reduction firing, the iron-rich black clay inlay vitrified into jet black, the pure kaolin inlay fired snow-white, and the overlying celadon glaze melted into a clear jade window roughly 0.3 millimeters thick. Because the white and black clays had slightly different thermal expansion coefficients than the gray stoneware body, only supreme mastery of clay levigation (subi, —washing and settling clay in tiered water vats for months) prevented the inlays from popping out in the fire.

Look at South Korea’s National Treasure No. 68, the Celadon Prunus Vase with Inlaid Cloud and Crane Design (Cheongja Sanggam Unhakmun Maebyeong), housed at the Kansong Art Museum. Rising forty-two centimeters with swelling shoulders that taper to a slender ankle and flare again at the foot, its surface is covered with sixty-nine circular roundels. Inside each circle, white cranes with black beaks and legs fly upward toward heaven while cranes outside the circles glide downward among stylized white clouds—a visual poem of Seon Buddhist liberation and longevity. Just as exquisite was the acoustic and mechanical engineering of Goryeo open-work (tugak) and ewer design. In the Celadon Ewer in the Shape of a Dragon-Headed Tortoise or the Water Dropper in the Shape of a Duck (National Treasure No. 74), potters concealed internal siphon tubes so water could be poured into the vessel without a visible lid hole and dispensed drop by drop onto an inkstone simply by covering a tiny airhole with a fingertip.

Tragedy overtook the Gangjin kilns in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Beginning in 1231, successive Mongol invasions forced the Goryeo royal court to flee to Ganghwa Island, disrupting tribute shipping routes, while fourteenth-century raids by Wako (Japanese pirates) devastated the coastal villages of Gangjin Bay. Forced to evacuate inland, the Gangjin potters scattered across the peninsula, and their aristocratic sanggam celadon gradually evolved into the freer, earthy, brush-slipped Buncheong ware of the early Joseon Dynasty. For nearly five hundred years, the exact chemical recipe and firing curve of bisaek celadon and sanggam inlay were lost—to the point where late-Joseon scholars wrote lamented poems wondering how their ancestors had captured liquid jade in clay. Then, in 1914, surveys in Daegu-myeon rediscovered the vast Sadang-ri kiln fields, and after Liberation a generation of pioneering Korean ceramicists—led by Yu Geun-hyeong (Living National Treasure No. 13), Hwang Jong-gu, and Gangjin master Cho Ki-jung—spent decades testing hundreds of clay mixtures and wood-ash glazes.

By analyzing excavated twelfth-century kiln saggers and glaze droplets from Sadang-ri No. 23 and No. 41 under electron microscopes, Korean ceramic scientists decoded the exact silica-alumina-calcium ratios and 1,250°C pine-reduction profile that gave Goryeo celadon its microscopic bubble veil. In Gangjin today, the Goryeo Celadon Museum operates authentic wood-fired climbing kilns alongside modern research studios. Stand beside a firing deunggama in Sadang-ri on an autumn night when pine flames roar through the stepped chambers and sparks drift toward the stars over Gangjin Bay. Three days later, when the bricked doorway is broken open and a warm maebyeong vase is lifted into the sunlight, the soft bingyeol (ice-crackle) network pings audibly in the cool air—singing the same crystalline note that twelfth-century potters heard eight hundred years ago.

Literary & Historical Analysis

1. Materials Science: Why Reduction Firing & Micro-Bubbles Create 'Bisaek' Jade

Electron microscopy and Mössbauer spectroscopy conducted by the National Museum of Korea and KAIST revealed why Goryeo celadon looks like translucent nephrite jade rather than flat green glass. First, firing in a carbon-monoxide reduction atmosphere converts yellow ferric oxide (Fe2O3) into blue-green ferrous oxide (FeO) at roughly a 7:3 ratio. Second, the wood-ash glaze has a relatively low maturation viscosity around 1,230°C, trapping millions of microscopic gas bubbles and unreacted quartz grains that scatter incident light via the Tyndall effect, producing a soft, misty depth.

2. The 12th-Century Taean & Jindo Shipwrecks: Underwater Time Capsules

Since 2007, marine archaeologists from the National Research Institute of Maritime Cultural Heritage have excavated several twelfth- and thirteenth-century Goryeo wooden cargo ships (such as the Taean Mado shipwrecks) that sank in the West Sea while carrying celadon from Gangjin and Buan to Gaegyeong. Preserved in anaerobic tidal mud for 800 years alongside wooden cargo tags (mokgan) recording the exact shipping date, sender, and recipient in the capital, more than 30,000 pristine celadon bowls, ewers, and honey jars have been recovered without a scratch.

3. Key Korean Concepts & Vocabulary to Remember

Anchor this dispatch with three ceramic terms: first, Bisaek (kingfisher color), the luminous blue-green glaze hue achieved through pine-fueled reduction firing; second, Sanggam, the twelfth-century Korean invention of incising patterns into leather-hard clay and inlaying contrasting white (baekto) and black (heukto) slip; and third, Maebyeong (plum vase), the S-curved vessel form whose broad shoulders and narrow waist became the iconic silhouette of Goryeo aesthetics.

4. Where to Walk This Story in Korea Today

Visit the Gangjin Goryeo Celadon Museum and the Sadang-ri Kiln Site in Gangjin County, South Jeolla Province, where you can peer inside excavated twelfth-century climbing kilns and watch Living National Treasure potters incise sanggam cranes by hand. In Seoul, visit the 3rd-floor Celadon Gallery of the National Museum of Korea in Yongsan or the Kansong Art Museum to see the original Inlaid Cloud and Crane Maebyeong.
