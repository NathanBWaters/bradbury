Dream-Children: A Reverie

Charles Lamb (1822) • 8 min read

By a quiet fireside, Elia tells his little son and daughter stories of their great-grandmother and his deceased brother—until the children slowly fade into the shores of Lethe.

Key Context & Why It Matters

Dream-Children: A Reverie by Charles Lamb appeared in 1822 in the London Magazine, and a year later in his collection Essays of Elia. Lamb was a clerk at the East India Company who wrote in his spare hours under the playful pen name Elia. His life had been marked by tragedy. In 1796 his sister Mary, in a fit of madness, killed their mother, and Charles took on the lifelong care of Mary rather than marry. The essay begins as a cozy family scene, a father telling his two small children about their great-grandmother and the old country house where she lived. It is a famous example of the personal essay, the form in which a writer speaks in an intimate, shifting, confiding voice. Underneath its gentleness is a quiet portrait of loss, regret and the lives we never got to have. It is short, so listen for what the storyteller says, and for what he avoids saying.

Children love to listen to stories about their elders, when they were children; to stretch their imagination to the conception of a traditionary great-uncle, or grandame, whom they never saw. It was in this spirit that my little ones crept about me the other evening to hear about their great-grandmother Field, who lived in a great house in Norfolk (a hundred times bigger than that in which they and papa lived) which had been the scene—so at least it was generally believed in that part of the country—of the tragic incidents which they had lately become familiar with from the ballad of the Children in the Wood. Certain it is that the whole story of the children and their cruel uncle was to be seen fairly carved out in wood upon the chimney-piece of the great hall, the whole story down to the Robin Redbreasts, till a foolish rich person pulled it down to set up a marble one of modern invention in its stead, with no story upon it. Here Alice put out one of her dear mother's looks, too tender to be called upbraiding.

Then I went on to say, how religious and how good their great-grandmother Field was, how beloved and respected by every body, though she was not indeed the mistress of this great house, but had only the charge of it (and yet in some respects she might be said to be the mistress of it too) committed to her by the owner, who preferred living in a newer and more fashionable mansion which he had purchased somewhere in the adjoining county; but still she lived in it in a manner as if it had been her own, and kept up the dignity of the great house in a sort while she lived, which afterwards came to decay, and was nearly pulled down, and all its old ornaments stripped and carried away to the owner's other house, where they were set up, and looked as awkward as if some one were to carry away the old tombs they had seen lately at the Abbey, and stick them up in Lady C.'s tawdry gilt drawing-room. Here John smiled, as much as to say, 'that would be foolish indeed.'

And then I told how, when she came to die, her funeral was attended by a concourse of all the poor, and some of the gentry too, of the neighbourhood for many miles round, to show their respect for her memory, because she had been such a good and religious woman; so good indeed that she knew all the Psaltery by heart, ay, and a great part of the Testament besides. Here little Alice spread her hands. Then I told what a tall, upright, graceful person their great-grandmother Field once was; and how in her youth she was esteemed the best dancer—here Alice's little right foot played an involuntary movement, till, upon my looking grave, it desisted—the best dancer, I was saying, in the county, till a cruel disease, called a cancer, came, and bowed her down with pain; but it could never bend her good spirits, or make them stoop, but they were still upright, because she was so good and religious.

Then I told how she was used to sleep by herself in a lone chamber of the great lone house; and how she believed that an apparition of two infants was to be seen at midnight gliding up and down the great staircase near where she slept, but she said 'those innocents would do her no harm'; and how frightened I used to be, though in those days I had my maid to sleep with me, because I was never half so good or religious as she—and yet I never saw the infants. Here John expanded all his eye-brows and tried to look courageous.

Then I told how good she was to all her grandchildren, having us to the great house in the holidays, where I in particular used to spend many hours by myself, in gazing upon the old busts of the Twelve Caesars, that had been Emperors of Rome, till the old marble heads would seem to live again, or I to be turned into marble with them; how I never could be tired with roaming about that huge mansion, with its vast empty rooms, with their worn-out hangings, fluttering tapestry, and carved oaken panels, with the gilding almost rubbed out—sometimes in the spacious old-fashioned gardens, which I had almost to myself, unless when now and then a solitary gardening man would cross me—and how the nectarines and peaches hung upon the walls, without my ever offering to pluck them, because they were forbidden fruit, unless now and then,—and because I had more pleasure in strolling about among the old melancholy-looking yew trees, or the firs, and picking up the red berries, and the fir apples, which were good for nothing but to look at—or in lying about upon the fresh grass, with all the fine garden smells around me—or basking in the orangery, till I could almost fancy myself ripening too along with the oranges and the limes in that grateful warmth—or in watching the dace that darted to and fro in the fish-pond, at the bottom of the garden, with here and there a great sulky pike hanging midway down the water in silent state, as if it mocked at their impertinent friskings,—I had more pleasure in these busy-idle diversions than in all the sweet flavours of peaches, nectarines, oranges, and such like common baits of children. Here John slyly deposited back upon the plate a bunch of grapes, which, not unobserved by Alice, he had meditated dividing with her, and both seemed willing to relinquish them for the present as irrelevant.

Then in somewhat a more heightened tone, I told how, though their great-grandmother Field loved all her grandchildren, yet in an especial manner she might be said to love their uncle, John L—, because he was so handsome and spirited a youth, and a king to the rest of us; and, instead of moping about in solitary corners, like some of us, he would mount the most mettlesome horse he could get, when but an imp no bigger than themselves, and make it carry him half over the county in a morning, and join the huntsmen and ride to the hounds; and how gratifying it was to my weakness when I was lame-footed to have him carry me on his back for many a mile when I could not walk for pain.

And then I told how in after life he became lame-footed too, and I did not always—I fear—make allowances enough for him when he was impatient, and in pain, nor remember sufficiently how considerate he had been to me when I was lame-footed; and how when he died, though he had not been dead an hour, it seemed as if he had died a great while ago, such a distance there is betwixt life and death; and how I bore his death as I thought pretty well at first, but afterwards it haunted and haunted me; and though I did not cry or take it to heart as some do, and as I think he would have done if I had died, yet I missed him all day long, and knew not till then how much I had loved him. I missed his kindness, and I missed his crossness, and wished him to be alive again, to be quarrelling with him (for we quarrelled sometimes), rather than not have him again, and was as uneasy without him, as he their poor uncle must have been when the doctor took off his limb. Here the children fell a-crying, and asked if their little mourning which they had on was not for uncle John, and they looked up, and prayed me not to go on about their uncle, but to tell them some stories about their pretty dead mother.

Then I told how for seven long years, in hope sometimes, sometimes in despair, yet persisting ever, I courted the fair Alice W—n; and, as much as children could understand, I explained to them what coyness, and difficulty, and denial meant in maidens—when suddenly, turning to Alice, the soul of the first Alice looked out at her eyes with such a reality of re-presentment, that I became in doubt which of them stood there before me, or whose that bright hair was; and while I stood gazing, both the children gradually grew fainter to my view, receding, and still receding till nothing at last but two mournful features were seen in the uttermost distance, which, without speech, strangely impressed upon me the effects of speech: 'We are not of Alice, nor of thee, nor are we children at all. The children of Alice called Bartrum father. We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams. We are only what might have been, and must wait upon the tedious shores of Lethe millions of ages before we have existence, and a name'—and immediately awaking, I found myself quietly seated in my bachelor arm-chair, where I had fallen asleep, with the faithful Bridget unchanged by my side—but John L. (or James Elia) was gone for ever.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On the surface, nothing happens. A father sits with his two children, Alice and John, one evening, and tells them about their great-grandmother Field, who looked after a big old country house, kept the family dignity alive, knew the Psalms by heart, had once been the best dancer in the county and was bowed by cancer but never in spirit. The children react as children do. Alice's foot twitches at the mention of dancing, John widens his eyebrows when ghosts are mentioned, and both seem willing to move on whenever the story grows heavy.

But the stories gradually shift. The first memories are of a house and an old woman. Then comes the uncle, John L., a handsome, spirited boy who rode the wildest horses and carried his lame little brother on his back for miles. Later this same uncle grew lame himself and died, and the father confesses he was not always patient with him. The tone changes. The father admits he missed his brother's kindness and even his crossness, and wanted him back just to quarrel with him again. The children cry. They ask whether their little mourning clothes are for uncle John, and they beg to hear about their pretty dead mother instead.

That is the first jolt. These children are in mourning for someone, and their mother is dead. The father then speaks of the long courtship, seven years of hope and despair, of the fair Alice W—n. As he talks, the daughter's face seems to turn into the face of the girl he once courted, and the children begin to fade, receding into the distance. They speak: "We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams. We are only what might have been." The father wakes up in his bachelor armchair, with his sister Bridget beside him. John L. is gone for ever.

So the whole scene was a daydream. There are no children. The woman he courted did not marry him. The essay takes the reader through a warm illusion and ends with a bare room. The pen name Elia and the invented names hide real people: John was Lamb's actual brother, who died in 1821, and Bridget is his sister Mary, with whom he lived all his adult life. The woman he courted was based on Ann Simmons, who married someone else.

Readers sometimes miss the ghost story hidden in the middle. The great-grandmother is not frightened of the apparition of two infants gliding on the staircase, the ghosts of the Children in the Wood from the old ballad, because she says those innocents would do her no harm. The children in the essay, in a sense, are the same kind of spirit, harmless, sweet, and only half real. The essay is about wanting something so much that you can hold it for an evening, then let it go.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The central structural idea is excellent: let the reader fall for the fiction, and only at the very end pull it away. Because the early paragraphs are so affectionate and detailed, the closing revelation is not just a twist but an emotional blow. Lamb also prepares it cleverly. The children interrupt, the father glances at their faces, Alice gives her mother's looks, John smiles when a joke lands. These small touches make the children vivid. When they dissolve, you feel as though you have lost real people, which is exactly the point.

The prose is a pleasure to read. Lamb writes in long sentences that wander and double back, with an old-fashioned flavor, and he rhymes humor with sorrow in a way that feels natural rather than staged. The aside about the foolish rich person who pulled down the carved wooden chimney-piece to put up a marble one with no story on it is both funny and revealing. The marble has no story, and Lamb is a man who lives for stories. The line about a great house's ornaments looking awkward when carried off, like tombs set up in a tawdry drawing-room, shows the same eye for how things lose meaning when they are moved from their place.

The deepest strength is emotional restraint. The central grief, that his brother died and that he is alone, is never stated at the outset. It comes out sideways, in the memory of the lame boy carried on a horseman's back and the admission that he did not make allowances for his brother's pain. That confession is the most moving passage because it does not ask for sympathy.

There are weaknesses, though, and a modern reader may feel them. The essay's prettiness can tip into sentiment. The children are almost too sweet, and the sorrow is occasionally cushioned by the very whimsy that makes it bearable. The line about "the tedious shores of Lethe", Lethe being the river of forgetfulness in Greek myth, is a touch ornate, and the final phrase, "only what might have been," is lovely but familiar. The devices of dreaming and waking were already well-worn, and some may feel Lamb leans on a literary convention rather than inventing one.

There is also an element of coyness. Lamb gives the real people initials and nicknames, hides the cause of Mary's illness entirely, and leaves most of the facts outside the frame. That reticence is part of the essay's dignity, but it can leave the sorrow feeling a little under-explained. If you did not know about Lamb's life, you might read the ending as mild wistfulness rather than as the cry of someone who gave up marriage to care for a sister.

Still, those faults are small against the effect. The essay is short, controlled and exact about the way memory edits what it keeps. It is more skillful than it first looks.

III. The Impact It Had

Dream-Children is one of the pieces on which Lamb's reputation as the great English personal essayist was built. The Essays of Elia, published in 1823 with a second series in 1833, made him famous as a writer who could turn the small details of a clerk's life, a roast pig, old china, a visit to a theater, a memory of his schooldays, into literature. This essay was among the most anthologized of them, and for much of the nineteenth century it stood as a model of what the personal essay could do.

The impact on the form was lasting. Before Lamb, essays were often lessons or arguments. Lamb showed that an essay could be a performance of a particular sensibility, a character with moods, jokes, weak spots and sorrows. His invention of Elia, a half-disguised version of himself, allowed him to be intimate without being naked. Later essayists took up that lesson, writers such as Robert Louis Stevenson, Max Beerbohm, and in the twentieth century the many writers of the personal essay who treat memory and family with humor. Even people who have never read him write in a tradition he helped shape.

The essay also helped fix a certain image of Lamb himself. Victorian readers made a cult of Charles and Mary Lamb, the devoted brother and sister, the gentle bachelor and his troubled sister who had been through terrible events. Many readers came to the essays through the biography. The story of Lamb sacrificing marriage and family to care for Mary made the sorrow in Dream-Children feel real and noble. It is one of those cases where the life enriches the page, and the page in turn fills out the life.

In education, the essay has long been a staple of anthologies of English prose and courses on Romantic-era writing. It turns up in discussions of dreams and memory in literature, as an example of the "reverie" tradition, and in studies of the way essays present the self. Teachers use it to show how a piece can be both charming and devastating, and to show how an ending can recast everything before it.

It has less obvious screen or stage legacy than novels, but its central image, the unborn children who say they are only what might have been, has been echoed again and again in writing about regret, from poems about lost possibilities to stories where a dreamer meets the family he never had. Few readers forget the sight of those children shrinking into the distance.

IV. What Readers Loved

The first thing admirers praised in Lamb was his charm, a quality that is hard to define but easy to feel. His friends, a circle that included the poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge and the essayist William Hazlitt, treasured him as witty, kind and a little odd. Hazlitt, who was not an easy man to please, included Lamb among the figures he wrote about with affection. That personal love carried over to the essays. Victorian readers in particular adored what they called gentle Elia, and the phrase stuck for generations.

What they loved in Dream-Children specifically was its tenderness. The picture of a bachelor imagining the family he might have had touched readers because it is an experience many people know in some form: the sense of a life you did not lead sitting beside the one you did. People who had lost siblings, or who had given up a love for duty or circumstance, found their own feelings voiced with unusual delicacy. The essay never wallows. It treats the pain with a light hand, and that lightness is part of why it is believable.

Readers also loved the humor tucked inside the sadness. There is a funny, domestic quality to the scene: the children's reactions, the father catching Alice's foot dancing and looking grave until it stops, the gentle swipe at the rich person with the marble chimney-piece. The jokes make the ending hit harder, because they establish a world you do not want to leave.

Writers have admired the craft. The slow shift from anecdote to confession, and from confession to vision, is something later essayists have studied. The phrase "less than nothing, and dreams" has been quoted by readers who want to describe the pain of unrealized lives, and the closing line, with its quiet reference to John L. being gone for ever, is often cited as an example of how to end an emotional piece without overstatement.

There is also a more general affection for the idea of the essay as companionship. Lamb makes the reader feel like a guest at the fireside, trusted with a family story and some private feelings. Many readers say they first fell in love with essays through Lamb, and Dream-Children is a likely reason. It teaches that an essay can be a way of keeping company with someone who has been dead nearly two centuries, which is in its own way a kind of the dreaming the essay describes.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Lamb has never lacked admirers, but he has also had critics, and their objections tend to circle around sentiment and style. The most persistent charge is that his writing can be too sweet and too self-consciously quaint. Readers influenced by modernist taste in the twentieth century, with its preference for plain, hard-edged prose, often found Lamb's antique diction, his fondness for old authors and his playful archaisms a bit precious. The fear is that the charm becomes a mannerism, a kind of performance of lovable eccentricity.

Dream-Children has drawn this complaint in particular, because its subject invites sentiment. Dead mothers, loving children and a lonely bachelor by the fire could easily be a recipe for sugar. Some critics have said that the essay relies on the reader's readiness to weep, and that the figures of the children are more emblems than people. Others point out that the ending depends on a familiar device, the dream that dissolves, and that it tidies up a very messy grief into a neat literary shape.

A second line of criticism concerns what the essay leaves out. Because Lamb protects his private world with initials and invented names, the essay can seem evasive. He mentions that his brother's death haunted him but says nothing of the other great catastrophe of his life, the death of his mother at his sister's hand. Readers wanting an unguarded confession may be frustrated. Defenders respond that the evasion is the whole point: Lamb was a man who survived by keeping his sorrows behind a smile, and the essay's restraint is a form of honesty about that.

There is also a historical point. Some contemporaries of Lamb disliked the Elia persona on political and moral grounds. Lamb was friendly with radicals and with religious doubters, and his easy, whimsical tone could be read as frivolous. Critics who preferred more public, argumentative writing felt that the personal essay was a minor form, a kind of literary chat. That prejudice has faded, but it shaped the early reception.

More recently, scholars have noted a certain social narrowness. The essay is about a bourgeois household and an old country house with servants and a housekeeper, and it takes for granted a world in which such memories and such leisure exist. Readers interested in broader questions of class or gender sometimes wonder about the women in the essay, who are mostly idealized figures: the pious grandmother, the pretty dead mother, the courted Alice. They are loved but seldom heard.

In fairness, most critics who object to the style still admit the force of the ending, which is hard to wave away.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

Dream-Children sits at the meeting point of two traditions. One is the English essay of the eighteenth century, the urbane, observant periodical writing of Joseph Addison and Richard Steele in The Spectator, which taught readers to enjoy character sketches and moral reflection in a conversational tone. The other is the Romantic emphasis on memory, childhood and the inner life, associated with Wordsworth, Coleridge and later writers. Lamb combined the two: the manners of the Spectator with the feeling of the Romantic poets.

Within the essay tradition, the dream vision or reverie has its own lineage. Addison's famous Vision of Mirza, in which the writer is shown human life as a bridge over a sea of mist, belongs here, as does the seventeenth-century prose of Sir Thomas Browne, whom Lamb adored. Dream-Children takes that old device of the dream and gives it a very private, domestic subject. Instead of the fate of mankind, it asks about one man's missing family.

What descends from it is the modern personal essay, the form that treats a writer's own life as legitimate material. Hazlitt's own essays, Virginia Woolf's essays on memory, E. B. White's reflections on family and time, and many later memoirists are in some sense cousins of Elia. The idea that a short piece can start in comedy and end in grief owes a good deal to Lamb. In fiction, the theme of the unlived life, the child who never was, the road not taken, appears in many forms, from ghost stories to the novels of regret that fill the library shelves.

Comparisons are illuminating. Wordsworth's Immortality Ode treats childhood as a lost radiance, but in poetry and with grand philosophy. Lamb does something humbler and perhaps more heartbreaking, giving the loss a table, a chair, a pair of children and a fire. Compare also Hazlitt, who in the same period wrote about his own feelings with fierce directness, where Lamb prefers disguise. Or compare Thomas Hardy, whose later poems about the missed chances of love cover similar ground with a bleaker tone. Lamb is softer and more consoling, but no less aware of time's cruelty.

Why read it now? Because its subject has not aged. People still imagine the families they might have had, the paths they did not take, and the people who are gone. The essay offers neither a cure nor a lecture, only a way of seeing the feeling clearly and then letting it go. And it is brief enough to read in a few minutes, and strange enough that, once you reach the ending, you will want to go back and read it again.
