The Selfish Giant

Oscar Wilde (1888) • 8 min read

“My own garden is my own garden; any one can understand that, and I will allow nobody to play in it but myself.”

Key Context & Why It Matters

Oscar Wilde is remembered for dazzling stage comedies and razor-sharp one-liners, which makes "The Selfish Giant" a surprise. It is a tender, simple fairy tale, and it appeared in 1888 in a little book called The Happy Prince and Other Tales. Wilde wrote these stories partly with his two young sons, Cyril and Vyvyan, in mind, and he was a loving father who enjoyed telling them stories. The tale draws on the fairy tales of Hans Christian Andersen, on Christian imagery, and on a typical Victorian concern with how the rich treat the poor and the young. It is short enough to read in five minutes, and it works on children and adults at once. Its subject is what happens to a person, and to a place, when everything is fenced off and kept for oneself. Listen for the seasons, because the weather in this story is a kind of moral barometer, and for the quiet surprise near the very end.

Every afternoon, as they were coming from school, the children used to go and play in the Giant’s garden. It was a large lovely garden, with soft green grass. Here and there over the grass stood beautiful flowers like stars, and there were twelve peach-trees that in the spring-time broke out into delicate blossoms of pink and pearl, and in the autumn bore rich fruit. The birds sat on the trees and sang so sweetly that the children used to stop their games in order to listen to them. “How happy we are here!” they cried to each other.

One day the Giant came back. He had been to visit his friend the Cornish ogre, and had stayed with him for seven years. After the seven years were over he had said all that he had to say, for his conversation was limited, and he determined to return to his own castle. When he arrived he saw the children playing in the garden. “What are you doing here?” he cried in a very gruff voice, and the children ran away. “My own garden is my own garden,” said the Giant; “any one can understand that, and I will allow nobody to play in it but myself.” So he built a high wall all round it, and put up a notice-board: TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED. He was a very selfish Giant.

The poor children had now nowhere to play. They tried to play on the road, but the road was very dusty and full of hard stones, and they did not like it. They used to wander round the high wall when their lessons were over, and talk about the beautiful garden inside. “How happy we were there,” they said to each other.

Then the Spring came, and all over the country there were little blossoms and little birds. Only in the garden of the Selfish Giant it was still winter. The birds did not care to sing in it as there were no children, and the trees forgot to blossom. Once a beautiful flower put its head out from the grass, but when it saw the notice-board it was so sorry for the children that it slipped back into the ground again, and went off to sleep. The only people who were pleased were the Snow and the Frost. “Spring has forgotten this garden,” they cried, “so we will live here all the year round.” The Snow covered up the grass with her great white cloak, and the Frost painted all the trees silver. Then they invited the North Wind to stay with them, and he came. He was wrapped in furs, and he roared all day about the garden, and blew the chimney-pots down. “This is a delightful spot,” he said, “we must ask the Hail on a visit.” So the Hail came. Every day for three hours he rattled on the roof of the castle till he broke most of the slates, and then he ran round and round the garden as fast as he could go. He was dressed in grey, and his breath was like ice.

“I cannot understand why the Spring is so late in coming,” said the Selfish Giant, as he sat at the window and looked out at his cold white garden; “I hope there will be a change in the weather.” But the Spring never came, nor the Summer. The Autumn gave golden fruit to every garden, but to the Giant’s garden she gave none. “He is too selfish,” she said. So it was always Winter there, and the North Wind, and the Hail, and the Frost, and the Snow danced about through the trees.

One morning the Giant was lying awake in bed when he heard some lovely music. It sounded so sweet to his ears that he thought it must be the King’s musicians passing by. It was really only a little linnet singing outside his window, but it was so long since he had heard a bird sing in his garden that it seemed to him to be the most beautiful music in the world. Then the Hail stopped dancing over his head, and the North Wind ceased roaring, and a delicious perfume came to him through the open casement. “I believe the Spring has come at last,” said the Giant; and he jumped out of bed and looked out.

What did he see? He saw a most wonderful sight. Through a little hole in the wall the children had crept in, and they were sitting in the branches of the trees. In every tree that he could see there was a little child. And the trees were so glad to have the children back again that they had covered themselves with blossoms, and were waving their arms gently above the children’s heads. The birds were flying about and twittering with delight, and the flowers were looking up through the green grass and laughing. It was a lovely scene, only in one corner it was still winter. It was the farthest corner of the garden, and in it was standing a little boy. He was so small that he could not reach up to the branches of the tree, and he was wandering all round it, crying bitterly. The poor tree was still quite covered with frost and snow, and the North Wind was blowing and roaring above it. “Climb up! little boy,” said the Tree, and it bent its branches down as low as it could; but the boy was too tiny.

And the Giant’s heart melted as he looked out. “How selfish I have been!” he said; “now I know why the Spring would not come here. I will put that poor little boy on the top of the tree, and then I will knock down the wall, and my garden shall be the children’s playground for ever and ever.” He was really very sorry for what he had done. So he crept downstairs and opened the front door quite softly, and went out into the garden. But when the children saw him they were so frightened that they all ran away, and the garden became winter again. Only the little boy did not run, for his eyes were so full of tears that he did not see the Giant coming. And the Giant stole up behind him and took him gently in his hand, and put him up into the tree. And the tree broke at once into blossom, and the birds came and sang on it, and the little boy stretched out his two arms and flung them round the Giant’s neck, and kissed him. And the other children, when they saw that the Giant was not wicked any longer, came running back, and with them came the Spring.

“It is your garden now, little children,” said the Giant, and he took a great axe and knocked down the wall. And when the people were going to market at twelve o’clock they found the Giant playing with the children in the most beautiful garden they had ever seen. All day long they played, and in the evening they came to the Giant to bid him good-bye.

“But where is your little companion?” he said: “the boy I put into the tree.” The Giant loved him the best because he had kissed him. “We don’t know,” answered the children; “he has gone away.” “You must tell him to be sure and come here to-morrow,” said the Giant. But the children said that they did not know where he lived, and had never seen him before; and the Giant felt very sad.

Every afternoon, when school was over, the children came and played with the Giant. But the little boy whom the Giant loved was never seen again. The Giant was very kind to all the children, yet he longed for his first little friend, and often spoke of him. “How I would like to see him!” he used to say. Years went over, and the Giant grew very old and feeble. He could not play about any more, so he sat in a huge armchair, and watched the children at their games, and admired his garden. “I have many beautiful flowers,” he said; “but the children are the most beautiful flowers of all.”

One winter morning he looked out of his window as he was dressing. He did not hate the Winter now, for he knew that it was merely the Spring asleep, and that the flowers were resting. Suddenly he rubbed his eyes in wonder, and looked and looked. It certainly was a marvellous sight. In the farthest corner of the garden was a tree quite covered with lovely white blossoms. Its branches were all golden, and silver fruit hung down from them, and underneath it stood the little boy he had loved. Downstairs ran the Giant in great joy, and out into the garden. He hastened across the grass, and came near to the child. And when he came quite close his face grew red with anger, and he said, “Who hath dared to wound thee?” For on the palms of the child’s hands were the prints of two nails, and the prints of two nails were on the little feet. “Who hath dared to wound thee?” cried the Giant; “tell me, that I may take my big sword and slay him.” “Nay!” answered the child; “but these are the wounds of Love.” “Who art thou?” said the Giant, and a strange awe fell on him, and he knelt before the little child. And the child smiled on the Giant, and said to him, “You let me play once in your garden, to-day you shall come with me to my garden, which is Paradise.” And when the children ran in that afternoon, they found the Giant lying dead under the tree, all covered with white blossoms.

Literary & Historical Analysis

I. What It Really Means

On its face, "The Selfish Giant" is a small fable about a garden. Children are in the habit of playing every afternoon in the Giant's lovely garden, with its soft grass, its flowers like stars and its twelve peach trees. The Giant has been away for seven years visiting a friend, the Cornish ogre, and when he returns he finds the children there, drives them out and builds a high wall. He puts up a notice board, and its message is blunt: "TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED." His justification is a tidy bit of circular logic: "My own garden is my own garden." It sounds like common sense, and that is exactly why it is so chilling. The Giant thinks of ownership as a closed door, and the story answers that a closed door is also a closed life.

What follows is the story's central image. When spring comes everywhere else, it refuses to come to the Giant's garden. Snow and Frost move in, joined by the North Wind and the Hail, and the place stays in winter all year. The Giant, puzzled, sits at his window and says he hopes the weather will change. This is the moral cleverly turned into weather: because he has shut out the children, he has shut out the whole cycle of growth and joy. The garden's coldness is simply the outward form of the Giant's own cold heart. Notice that the Giant does not know why he is suffering. Selfishness, the story suggests, is often a blindness before it is a vice.

The thaw begins when a linnet sings at his window, and he sees the children have crept back through a hole in the wall. In every tree sits a child, and the trees are blooming. Only one corner is still in winter, where a small boy is too little to climb a tree. The Giant's heart melts, and he lifts the boy into the branches, knocks down the wall with an axe and tells the children the garden is theirs. The act of giving the garden away is what finally makes it his, and makes it alive.

The last section turns the fable into something else. Years later the Giant is old and feeble, and the little boy returns. The Giant sees marks of two nails on the boy's palms and feet and asks who has dared to wound him. The child answers, "these are the wounds of Love," and tells him he will come to the child's own garden, "which is Paradise." The child is Christ, and the story quietly becomes a parable about grace: the Giant's kindness to a child has been kindness to God. When the children find the Giant dead beneath the tree, covered in white blossoms, the story closes on a note of peace, not sorrow. The winter inside him has finally ended.

II. How Well It Works: A Critique

The story's greatest strength is its economy and its sound. Wilde writes in short, clear sentences with the rhythm of something told aloud, and the story reads beautifully when spoken. The opening paragraph paints the garden in a few bright strokes, and the repetition of phrases such as "How happy we were there" gives the tale the quality of a song with a refrain. The personification of the seasons is delightful. Snow covers the grass "with her great white cloak," Frost paints the trees silver, and the Hail is "dressed in grey" with breath "like ice." Wilde gives the cruel weather the manners of unwelcome house guests who decide to stay on and break the roof slates, and that touch of humor keeps the story from heaviness.

The structure is also excellent. It moves in a clean cycle from happiness to loss to winter to renewal, and then onto the larger cycle of a life from youth through old age to death. The little boy who cannot climb the tree is a perfect emotional hinge, because he is both the smallest figure in the story and the one who turns it. The image of winter at the end, which the old Giant no longer hates because "it was merely the Spring asleep," is one of the most graceful lines in the tale, and it shows that the Giant has learned a patient kind of wisdom.

But an honest critic has to admit that the story is also sentimental, and that its sentiment can be sticky. The children are mostly an undifferentiated mass of innocence, and the Giant's conversion is quick and total. A reader may feel that the moral machinery is a little too visible, and that the Giant is more of a symbol than a person. The Christian ending, in particular, is the most debated part. For some readers it is a luminous and moving surprise. For others it is a sudden shift from storybook to sermon, in which the child's grand revelation arrives too neatly and too sweetly. Even readers who admire the ending sometimes feel that Wilde reaches for religious feeling more readily here than elsewhere in his work.

There is another quiet tension. Wilde was a skeptical, witty man who delighted in paradox, and this story is utterly earnest. That earnestness can feel at odds with what we know of his temperament, though it may be that he was simply good at changing voices. Taken as a whole, the story works because it is short, sincere and shapely. Its weaknesses are the weaknesses of its genre, in which simple good and simple evil are the rule, and it handles them with more grace than most.

III. The Impact It Had

"The Selfish Giant" became one of the most beloved children's stories in English, and it has stayed in print and in school curricula for well over a century. Together with "The Happy Prince" and the other tales in the same book, it established Wilde as a writer who could do more than epigrams. It showed a gentle and humane side of him that surprised readers who knew him only as the witty aesthete. When his name later became associated with scandal after his trial and imprisonment in the 1890s, these tales were among the works that kept a warmer picture of him alive.

The story has been retold in picture books, read on radio, staged as plays and school pageants, and adapted into musical and operatic form and into animation. It remains a favorite of primary school teachers because its message of sharing is easy to grasp, and its language is a model of clear, musical English. It is also frequently read at funerals and in church services, because its last scene offers comfort about death.

The tale's reach also extends into surprising places. The idea of a hard-hearted owner whose selfishness blights his own land echoes in later writing about property and community. Wilde's essay "The Soul of Man Under Socialism," published a few years later in 1891, argues in much more abstract language that private property can warp the people who hold it as well as the people shut out by it. Many readers see the Giant's wall as a first, childlike version of that idea. In recent decades the title was borrowed for a British film, directed by Clio Barnard and released in 2013, which is a gritty drama about two boys in a northern English town. It is not an adaptation of the fairy tale in any strict way, but it borrows the title and the theme of selfishness and kindness set against social hardship.

For literary history, the tale helped confirm a late Victorian shift in children's literature. Where earlier moral tales were often heavy and bossy, writers such as Wilde and, before him, Hans Christian Andersen showed that a fairy tale could be poetic, sad and deeply felt without lecturing. Later writers of literary fairy tales owe something to that example, and the story remains a touchstone for anyone who wants to write a modern fable.

A last point is worth noting. Wilde's story is quietly modern in refusing to punish the Giant forever. Mercy is allowed to win, and the winter is understood as a pause rather than a sentence, which has made it a comfort to generations.

IV. What Readers Loved

The most obvious thing readers love is the garden itself. Wilde makes it so vivid, with its pink and pearl blossoms and its singing birds, that its loss feels like a real bereavement. Children respond to the simple justice of the plot, in which a bully is shut out of happiness by his own behavior. Adults respond to something deeper, a feeling that they know this Giant, and that they have sometimes been him in small ways. Readers often say that the story made them think about their own walls, whether of pride, of property or of fear.

They also love its tenderness. The Giant is not a monster. He is gruff, self-absorbed and lonely, with a limited conversation, as Wilde says drily, but he is capable of changing. His transformation, when he sees the weeping boy and says "How selfish I have been!", is one of the most satisfying turns in children's literature, because it is so unforced. The detail that the other children run away when they see him, frightened, and that only the boy with tear-filled eyes does not notice him, is the kind of true psychological touch that makes a fable feel alive.

Many readers cherish the last scene most of all. The reappearance of the little boy after years, with the marks of the nails, produces a shiver that even non-religious readers feel. The sentence "You let me play once in your garden, to-day you shall come with me to my garden, which is Paradise" has been quoted at funerals and in sermons precisely because it holds out a promise of reward for simple kindness. The Giant dies in peace under a tree covered in blossoms, and the story says that death can be gentle.

Finally, readers admire the music of the prose. People who read the story aloud to children often remark on how well the sentences carry. It is a story designed for the ear, with repetitions and refrains, and with a flow that makes the whole thing feel like a song. That is also why it works so well as audio. It asks very little of a reader and gives a great deal back, and for many people it is the first literary work that ever made them cry.

Many readers also mention how much the story matters to them as parents or teachers, and how often they have found themselves reaching for it when a child asks why sharing matters.

V. What Critics Pushed Back On

Little in the way of hostile criticism greeted "The Happy Prince and Other Tales" when it appeared, and in general the book was received warmly, though specific reviews of this one story are not especially well documented. Most of the pushback has been indirect and has come from later readers weighing the story against Wilde's reputation and its own sweetness.

The most frequent objection is sentimentality. Critics who prize toughness and irony have found the story too sugary, with its angelic children, its quick conversion and its heavenly ending. For these readers the story is lovely but easy, and it solves the problem of selfishness by having a magical child appear and everything turn out well. In real life, walls do not come down because a child cries in a tree.

A second objection concerns the Christian ending. Some readers feel that the revelation that the boy is Christ turns a humane and universal fable into a narrowly religious one, and that it was an odd move for a writer who was so often at odds with conventional religion. Others note that Wilde had a lifelong fascination with Christ as a figure of suffering and individual feeling, and that his interest was aesthetic and emotional more than doctrinal. This has led to debate about whether the ending is sincere devotion, a poet's fascination with a symbol, or a bit of both. A few readers have also found the idea that the Giant is rewarded with heaven for a late-life change of heart a little too easy, since the children he shut out spent years without a place to play.

A third line of criticism is political, and it points in an unexpected direction. Some readers see in the story an endorsement of generosity as a private virtue and wonder whether it lets structural questions off the hook. The Giant's conversion is personal, not systemic. He is moved to open the garden by feeling, not by law or justice, and the story offers no sense of what should happen if the owner stays hard-hearted. Others say that this misreads a fairy tale, which is not a political pamphlet.

Finally, a few have asked whether the story is quite as suitable for children as it appears, since it ends with a death and a vision of wounds. Most readers feel that Wilde handles this delicately, but the point has been raised.

The tale is also admired for how little it explains. It trusts the child listener to feel the sadness of the missing boy, and trusts the adult listener to understand who he is.

VI. Its Place in the Bigger Picture

"The Selfish Giant" belongs to the tradition of the literary fairy tale, a form in which a known author, rather than anonymous folk tradition, makes up a story with the feel of an old one. Its closest ancestor is Hans Christian Andersen, whose tales mix charm with sorrow and whose stories often end in death and a hint of heaven. Wilde's own "The Happy Prince" and "The Nightingale and the Rose" belong to the same family, with their gentle melancholy and their emphasis on sacrifice. Behind Andersen lies the older tradition of the moral parable and the Christian allegory, in which a simple story carries a spiritual truth.

The tale also belongs to a Victorian conversation about childhood. In the nineteenth century, writers were increasingly interested in the idea of children as innocents who could redeem the adult world, an idea you can find in Dickens, in the poetry of Wordsworth before him, and in the later boom in children's literature around the time of Lewis Carroll. The Giant's garden, a paradise from which children are shut out and to which they return, fits that strain of thought, and the story's emotional logic is that adults are saved by the children they would shut out.

Among later works, it can be set beside other stories about walls and gardens. The secret garden as a place of healing is a major theme in children's books, as is the idea of a hoarder or miser who is melted by kindness, a theme familiar from Dickens's A Christmas Carol. In the Giant we can see a cousin of Scrooge, with the difference that Wilde's tale is gentler and shorter, and ends in a far more mystical register. Comparisons might also be drawn with stories of gift and sacrifice such as Shel Silverstein's The Giving Tree, though that is a very different book with a different attitude toward giving.

It still matters because its central question has not gone away. Who gets to use the green spaces of the world, and what do we lose when we wall them off? It is also an unusually gentle gateway to Wilde. A reader who knows him only from The Importance of Being Earnest may be surprised to find that the same hand wrote this, and that it is full of warmth and conviction. It takes five minutes to read, and it leaves something behind.
